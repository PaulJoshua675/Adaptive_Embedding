The confidence probe misses capability gaps and PITs more often than near misses at both scales (Figure 3A; Table 11); a trace is missed when its predicted correctness at the final step is at least the cutoff of 0.5. Overall calibration is compatible with this pattern by failure type, because incorrect traces are the minority of the calibration population. Controlled comparison. We test the hypothesis that the best reachable value at step 0, V ∗ 0 , trace length, answer position and problem difficulty account for the higher miss rate of PITs than of near misses. We first compare the two failure types within bins of V ∗ 0 (Figure 3B), and then fit a logistic model of trace misses on the failure type and the four variables. The difference between PITs and near misses after controls is the average marginal effect of changing a trace’s failure type from near miss to PIT in this model (Appendix B). It exceeds the minimum effect at 8B, at +0.125 [+0.104, +0.143]. At 4B the difference is nonzero and below the minimum effect, at +0.037 [+0.012, +0.064] (Table 11). Capability gaps have V ∗ 0 ≤θ by definition and PITs have V ∗ 0 > θ, so the two failure types occupy disjoint ranges of V ∗ 0 , and their miss rates are compared without controls. Combined probe. We call the probe trained on rollout rates of incorrect traces the combined probe. It reads the confidence probe’s input, the hidden states at the read site, and keeps the final label as the target on correct traces. At the final step, the confidence probe gives PITs higher scores than near misses at both scales, and the combined probe gives PITs lower scores than near misses (Figure 3C); each probe is scored on its own held-out traces. The two probes differ in training labels, attention mask, step weighting and training schedule (Table 3; Appendix B), so this reversal cannot be attributed to the labels alone. Failure-type classifier. The confidence probe’s miss rates for capability gaps and PITs differ by less than two percentage points at both scales (Table 11), so we test the separation of the two failure types from a completed incorrect trace. A transcoder is a fixed sparse approximation of an MLP sublayer [Dunefsky et al., 2024, Paulo et al., 2025]. The failure-type classifier differs from the confidence probe in input and in scoring problems instead of traces: it reads the transcoder activations at layers 19 to 24 of one completed incorrect trace per problem and separates problems labelled capability gap from problems labelled PIT (Appendix B.1). It exceeds the strongest tested baseline that uses no hidden states by 0.055 AUROC at 8B and 0.049 at 4B. The margin is applied to the mean over six repetitions of five-fold cross-validation on the same problems, so the 8B result meets the margin and the 4B result does not. At both scales the interval of the difference excludes zero and contains the margin in each of the six repetitions. The same classifier on the hidden states at these layers scores lower at both scales (Appendix B.1). Our intuition is that the width of the transcoder input, 40 to 64 times that of the hidden states, accounts for part of this gap; a random encoding of equal width was not tested. The failure-type classifier reads a completed trace and predicts a label of its problem, so it applies only after a trace has ended incorrectly. Appendix B.1 reports the baselines, the six repetitions, and the differences after removing problems whose labels may be defective. Predicted correctness at the PIT step. At both scales, the confidence probe’s predicted correctness is lower at the PIT step than at the other steps of the trace. The position-matched steps of a trace are its other steps whose scaled position t/(L −1), for step t of a trace with L steps, lies within 0.15 of the PIT step’s. Against these steps the decrease remains at 8B.
