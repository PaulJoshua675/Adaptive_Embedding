Likewise, we observe that while aggregating independent network decisions yields a modest collective benefit, this benefit increases in both magnitude and scope when pooled- feedback is introduced. There is also evidence that variability among individual judgments is important for crowd wisdom (Barrera-Lemarchand et al., 2024). Likewise, it was important that we randomized and trained our ESNs individually so their dynamics would be distinct during testing, allowing them to make distinct contributions to interactions and their collective effects via pooled-feedback. Finally, network structure and response weighting can amplify or suppress crowd wisdom (Baten & Mahjabin, 2025; Becker et al., 2017). Such effects could be simulated and tested with our model, and simulations of larger social networks under various coupling rules may help us understand the functional connections between individual and social dynamics. The generality of our framework aside, we focused on a classic nine-choice speaker classifi- cation benchmark so we could investigate and analyze the model in depth. Bahrami et al. (2010) used a two-alternative visual discrimination task, so it would be useful to test if simulations more specific to their task that estimate visual discriminability from psychometric curves might provide additional insights into their results. It would also be useful to simulate other tasks and results that both do and do not confer a benefit to collective interaction. 5.2 Collective Stabilization of Positive Feedback We found that the benefit of interaction over statistical facilitation only held for relatively unstable reservoirs that needed pooling to guide their runaway dynamics. Runaway dynamics came from positive feedback between each reservoir and its read-out units, and such dangers of positive feedback are common and well-known in neural and behavioral dynamics. In the case of decision-making, positive feedback can manifest as "overthinking," such as when a student revises a correct multiple-choice answer because deliberation continues after sufficient evidence has been reached. One can imagine that interaction with a peer might help the student avoid overthinking and stick with their original answer. Positive feedback can also enable exploration and adaptation but unchecked it runs the risk of causing instability (Brandman & Meyer, 2008; May, 1972). For example, attempts to maintain a constant vocal volume often lead to unintentional amplification (Zollinger & Brumm, 2011), while excessive positive feedback in brain circuits can produce seizures (Huguenard & McCormick, 2007; Jirsa et al., 2014). Negative feedback stabilizes neural dynamics, supporting homeostasis, and disruptions in the balance of positive and negative feedback contribute to psychiatric disorders like schizophrenia (Molina et al., 2020). Neural computation is thought to emerge near this balance at the "edge of chaos" (Hengen & Shew, 2025; Kello, 2013), a regime also exploited by reservoir computing to maximize both stability and flexibility (Bertschinger & Natschläger, 2004). Our work demonstrates that balancing positive and negative feedback can emerge from the interplay of individual and collective dynamics. Agents like reservoir networks that are individually susceptible to positive feedback can achieve stable performance by leveraging negative feedback loops introduced through dyadic and group interaction. This finding extends the idea that nonlinear interactions across scales can transform the dynamical properties of individual components and overall behavior to collective systems (Kelty-Stephen et al., 2023). In particular, collective near-critical dynamics can emerge from interactions among agents whose individual dynamics are not themselves optimally tuned (Bessone & Plantec, 2026).
