For each annotated event, the question generator takes the video together with the event description and generates candidate questions about either the target event itself or its surrounding context, an expected answer, along with the question timestamps t. We consider diverse question formats, including open-ended, multiple-choice, and yes/no questions. To prevent data leakage, we filter out the samples used in OVO-Bench (Niu et al., 2025). Given a generated question, the answer generator then processes the video up to timestamp t and produces a model answer. Finally, the verifier takes the generated question, expected answer, model answer, and the video context surrounding the target event to assess both the validity of the question and the correctness of the answer. An unreasonable question or an inconsistent answer typically results in disagreement between the expected answer, generated answer, and supporting video context. We therefore retain only QA pairs that pass the verification stage to form the final training dataset. We use Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (Qwen Team, 2026) as both the question and answer generator, and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B as the verifier. Please see more details in the Appendix B.1 and B.2. Dataset Statistics. MaLoW-Instruct contains 381K instruction samples on 56K videos. We summarize the distributions and statistics of the videos and questions in Fig. 3-2. Approximately 36% of the QA pairs have query timestamps that occur after the corresponding event has ended, providing explicit supervision for learning the MaLoW module to retain and retrieve historical information. Moreover, for many real-time questions, part of the relevant event falls outside the current observation window, further encouraging the model to leverage historical context through MaLoW. The question types are approximately evenly distributed among multiple-choice, open-ended, and yes/no questions. The videos are collected from 14 datasets, among which CosMo-Cap (Wang et al., 2024a), YouCook2 (Zhou et al., 2018), Charades-STA (Gao et al., 2017), and ActivityNet-Captions (Krishna et al., 2017) together account for 62.5% of all videos. More dataset statistics are presented in the Appendix B.3. 4 Experiments and Results 4.1 Streaming Video Understanding Benchmarks. Following the prior works (Shen et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a), we evaluate MaLoW on the Real-Time split of StreamingBench (Lin et al., 2026), as well as the Real-Time and Backward splits of OVO-Bench (Niu et al., 2025). The Backward splits of OVO-Bench mostly focus on retrospect to the past and comprise three tasks: Episodic Memory (EPM), Action Sequence Identification (ASI), and Hallucination Detection (HLD). Among them, EPM is particularly well suited for assessing the effectiveness of the memory mechanism introduced by MaLoW. Experiment Protocol. We build MaLoW on top of a diverse set of VLMs, including models pre-trained for offline video understanding (Qwen3-VL-4B (Bai et al., 2025) and Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026)) and models designed for streaming video (VideoChat3-4B (Li et al., 2026) and MOSS-VL-Realtime-11B (Wang et al., 2026)). Across all base VLMs, MaLoW operates in a streaming setting with a local context window: at each time step t, only the most recent video clip Ct is provided as the current observation, while all preceding clips C0:t−1 are consolidated into the evolving model weights. 6
