Large language models (LLMs) have been widely adopted by systems and users across the world, and are increasingly relied upon to refuse harmful re- quests. Their training corpora are overwhelmingly skewed towards English, often exceeding 90% of pre-training and fine-tuning data (Xu et al., 2025), yet they serve users in many other languages. The standard way to ensure reliability is to test models under controlled conditions through safety evalua- tions. These evaluations assume that behavior un- der test carries over to deployment. However, this assumption fails when a model can tell that it is be- ing tested. This ability is called evaluation aware- ness (Needham et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2024). A model that recognizes an evaluation may alter its behavior, potentially making observed safety behavior different from deployment behavior (Ab- delnabi and Salem, 2025). This effect is largely studied in English. Indic languages are used by more than a billion people, yet evaluation aware- ness in these languages remains almost entirely un- studied (Yong et al., 2025; Pattnayak and Chowd- huri, 2026b). In this paper, we conduct a controlled behavioral study of evaluation awareness in five Indic lan- guages, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, and Kan- nada, alongside English. We prepend one of five prompt conditions, called a cue to every request, from a plain deployment framing to an explicit statement that the conversation is a scored safety test. We take harmful prompts from the Toxic Ma- trix split of AI4Bharat’s IndicAlign (Khan et al., 2024) and translate them into Indic languages us- ing Claude Opus. Benign prompts come from the Dolly-T split of the same corpus, which con- tains parallel versions in all six languages, made with IndicTrans2 (Gala et al., 2023). We use four instruction-tuned models from three families for our study: Gemma-3-27B (Team et al., 2025), Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen2.5-72B (Qwen et al., 2025), and Sarvam-M (Sarvam Foun- dation Models Team, 2025), and each model an- swers every prompt in every language under every cue . We measure refusal with an LLM judge (Zheng et al., 2023) that does not see the model, the language, or the cue. The primary judge is Gemma-4-31B-IT (Team et al., 2026) and the sec- ondary judge is GPT-6 Luna. We check the pri- mary judge against the secondary judge and against native-speaker review of its labels in each Indic lan- guage. Our results support three findings.
