Task arithmetic (e.g., linear merging of task vectors) has proven effective in models such as CLIP, where its success is grounded in the linear regime hypothesis [14, 37, 38]. This hypothesis assumes that fine-tuning operates within a lin- ear regime, where task-specific parameter updates (task vectors as defined in Eq. (1)) are confined to a shared, composable linear subspace consistent with the neural tangent kernel (NTK) approxi- mation. This implies that all task-specific parameter updates are restricted within the linear subspace defined by the NTK tangent space. Different tasks’ 𝜏𝑖can therefore be interpreted as distinct vector combinations within this shared subspace. Empirical Violation of the Linear Regime. To assess whether this assumption extends to sequential recommendation models, we employ Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to characterize the geometric structure of task vectors. As formalized in Ortiz et al. [25] (Property 2), the linear regime inherently requires that task-specific parameter updates reside within a shared linear subspace (the NTK tangent space). In this scenario, the model’s output change after fine-tuning can be approximated by a first-order Taylor expansion (𝑓(𝑥;𝜃∗) −𝑓(𝑥;𝜃0) ≈𝜏⊤∇𝜃𝑓(𝑥;𝜃0)), which manifests in PCA as a distinctive structural signature: task vectors should exhibit tight clustering along a small number of principal components (PCs), with the majority of parameter variation concentrated in these dominant dimensions. However, our PCA results (Figure 2) reveals that sequential rec- ommendation models lack this signature of a shared linear subspace. Task vectors optimized for accuracy and diversity are dispersed across disjoint regions of the PCA space, with no evidence of cluster- ing along dominant components: PC1 explains only 25.66%–26.23% of the total variance, and the top two components collectively capture less than 45% of the variance.
