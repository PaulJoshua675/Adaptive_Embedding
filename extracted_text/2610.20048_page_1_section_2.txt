Subject indexing is the task of enriching a metadata record for a textual resource (a document representing an article or a book, for example) with multiple subjects that cover the content of the resource. This is typically done by professional subject indexers who are also domain experts, i.e., librarians working at the library that has the resource in its collection. In order to benefit the downstream use cases of library subject indexing, descriptors are taken from a pre-defined, controlled vocabulary, such as a thesaurus. However, with the growing number of library catalogs, it is challenging to keep pace purely with manual workflows. Using computational methods, typically machine learning methods, to automatically identify appropriate subject terms from a controlled vocabulary is called automated subject indexing. It allows libraries to keep pace with the growing volume of digital materials and ensures that their collections remain organized and easily navigable in the digital age. The size of the thesaurus used for indexing purposes can vary significantly across different institutions. However, many of the thesaurus maintained by the libraries have several thousand de- scriptors ranging from 1,000-20,000+ subjects. A large label set combined with limited training data per label makes the task an extreme multi-label classification (XMLC) problem.. XMLC tasks are also usually characterized by highly skewed label distribution with long tail labels. This phenomenon is also observed in auto- mated subject indexing. Depending on the amount of training data available, it can also be considered a low-resource setting. Adapting a strategy to suit this need involves identifying where traditional approaches fail. Flat XMLC classifier may address the typical XMLC problem of handling a large label set, but it treats each subject independently, and the label quality is bound to the strength of the training signal. Thus, the performance of such models is competitive for head labels but suffers for the long tail which only appears a few times in the training data. They also tend to fail on unseen labels because the model cannot predict a label that it has never seen before. Retrieval-based rerankers such as cross-encoders are not bound by the label frequency constraint since they predict a subject by evaluating each document- subject pair independently. The subjects are assigned without any knowledge of where they are positioned in the thesaurus. However, subject indexers are advised to be as specific as possible (i.e., pick subjects that are closer to the leaves of the descriptor hierarchy), but if a document treats very broad topics, then the content can only be described by more generic descriptors. Choosing an adequate number of descriptors and the correct level of granularity and specificity lies within the responsibility and discretion of the subject indexing expert. Thus, the reranker cannot recognize that a more specific descriptor in the same branch of the hierarchy fits the document better. For example, a document about “Automation, Innovation and Economic Crisis: Surviving the Fourth Industrial Revolution“ has a gold subject: Technological change.
