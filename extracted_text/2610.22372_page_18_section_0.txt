A Additional Information This appendix includes additional experimental details and results that support the main findings in Section 4.2. A.1 Ethics Statement LIBERO-PeRM is built from procedurally generated preference-conditioned tasks, layouts, trajec- tories, and evaluation signals in simulation. Human feedback is used to review generated data and validate preference plausibility, but no personal data or real-world user logs are released or used as policy-training data. Its intended use is research on personalized robotic manipulation, including preference learning, generalization, composition, and conflict handling. Our dataset construction process is designed to reduce ethical and legal risk. We build on LIBERO [23] scenes and simula- tor assets, then generate preference-conditioned variants through minimal layout edits, procedural trajectory generation, and automatic preference metrics. Preference descriptions, satisfaction rules, and benchmark annotations are created for research use rather than copied from external products or commercial systems. Any public release should preserve attribution to upstream assets and follow the licenses and terms of use of LIBERO and other dependencies. The benchmark also has important limitations. Our preference metrics only capture observable execution outcomes in simulation and cannot represent open-ended, private, or highly subjective human preferences. As a result, LIBERO- PeRM should not be treated as a substitute for human evaluation in safety-critical or real-world personalized deployment. We intend this benchmark to support controlled scientific evaluation, not direct real-world decision making. We take responsibility for the released benchmark artifacts. The LIBERO-PeRM dataset and open- source code are released under the MIT license. A.2 Training Details For non-zero-shot experiments, we further tune π0.5 with LoRA [75]. We scale the training budget with the number of rollouts: for every 4,000 rollout episodes, we use roughly 45,000 optimization steps on eight A6000 Ada GPUs with a learning rate of 5×10−5. For the main fine-tuning comparison, All-Pref denotes one checkpoint jointly trained on Motion, Skill, and User-Profile preference tasks; it is not an ensemble or an average of category-specific checkpoints. We evaluate each fixed checkpoint in 10 independent runs and report mean ± standard deviation in Table 3. The variation therefore reflects randomized evaluation rollouts, not training seeds. B Preference Selection Criteria We select a preference only if it satisfies five criteria: 1) expressible in natural language; 2) can be grounded in existing LIBERO [23] scenes; 3) satisfiable without changing the task goal; 4) measurable by an automatic preference metric; and 5) reusable across multiple tasks or layouts. Examples include the Motion Preference preference “speed up”, measured by execution steps; the Skill Preference preference “pick up an object by its handle”, measured by waypoints and contact points; and the User Profile preference “prefer dairy product”, measured by the executed subgoal. We exclude preferences that require scene-external information, unsafe or task-infeasible behavior, or subjective judgments that cannot be mapped to observable execution outcomes.
