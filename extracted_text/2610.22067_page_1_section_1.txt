Long-context language models support ever longer context windows, and evalu- ations typically treat context length as the main indicator of difficulty. However, context length alone cannot explain why models fail on long-context tasks that re- quire reasoning over complex information. Contexts of equal length can pose very different challenges: a task may be solvable from a single local passage, or it may require integrating evidence distributed across the full context. We quantitatively characterise this difficulty through three properties: evidence scope, how much necessary information must be found; evidence dispersion, how widely that infor- mation is scattered within the context; and reasoning depth, how many dependent steps are needed to derive an answer from it. We construct LURCH, a multi-domain question-answering benchmark of 6,550 instances with supporting evidence and reasoning graphs, enabling all three properties to be quantified. Evaluations of proprietary and open-weight models show that: (1) at fixed context length, all our three properties are strongly negatively correlated with model performance; (2) as context length increases, performance degrades sharply on samples with dispersed evidence, whereas it remains relatively stable on samples with localised evidence; and (3) difficulty compounds across properties, as questions with moderately high values on all three are harder than those with an extreme value on just one.
