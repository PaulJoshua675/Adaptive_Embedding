Each session is split independently into chunks of about ten turns, so chunks never cross session boundaries. The chronological chunk stream is then grouped into RL windows of at most five chunks; a window may span sessions. The per-chunk probe pass writes the PRM items for chunk t. It reads the full text of chunk t plus the extracted facts of chunks t−1 and t−2, used only to resolve references and decide what is new. The cross-chunk outcome pass writes ORM items from the facts of the whole window and earlier history. Structural filters and a synthetic-answer discriminability test produce the final artifact. The bottom row lists the rules that keep answer information out of the reward path (Section 5.6). Why not the full history. Dependencies that span more than a few chunks are covered by the cross- chunk outcome pass, so the PRM pass does not need the whole sequence. Full history also lowers annotation quality. In a controlled comparison on identical chunks, full-history annotation used 33 times more input tokens, while the two-chunk context produced 25% more proper nouns and 59% more numbers per extracted fact. With the full sequence in view, the annotator’s attention spreads away from the current chunk and its facts become more generic, which is the over-generalization failure (Section 5.4) that probes are meant to detect. 5.4 Failure-surface taxonomy Table 4 lists the local failure modes. Cross-chunk questions add transition, coexistence, temporal, aggregation, multi-hop, and negative recall.
