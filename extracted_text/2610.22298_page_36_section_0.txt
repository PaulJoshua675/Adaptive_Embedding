C.4.2 Pediatric Endocrinology Consolidated Clinical Second Opinion — Pediatric Endocrinology Clinical Summary An 8-year-old boy presents with an 18-month history of a right retroareolar breast mass that has enlarged over the past 6 months. He has recently developed fine, straight, lightly pigmented pubic hair and scant axillary hair. His growth parameters (height 68th percentile, weight 72nd percentile) and bone age (8 years) are appropriate for his chronological age. Examination reveals a 2 × 2 cm firm, mobile, non-adherent right subareolar mass, a small left breast bud, and prepubertal testes (3 mL) and phallus. Breast ultrasound demonstrates a well-circumscribed, oval, hypoechoic, hypovascular mass with through-transmission and no malignant features. Assessment and Differential Diagnosis The clinical picture is most consistent with asymmetric benign gynecomastia occurring in the setting of isolated premature adrenarche. • The character of the pubic and axillary hair (fine, straight, lightly pigmented), combined with prepubertal testes and a bone age equal to chronological age, strongly supports physiologic adrenarche rather than true gonadarche or an androgen-secreting lesion. • The presence of a contralateral breast bud indicates a bilateral process with asymmetric prominence, which is classic for gynecomastia. While the ultrasound's through-transmission might raise the question of a benign cystic or ductal lesion, this acoustic feature is also frequently seen with discoid glandular proliferation. Pathologic entities that must be excluded include: • Estrogen- or hCG-secreting tumors (e.g., testicular Leydig/Sertoli cell tumor, mediastinal germinoma, adrenal neoplasm). These can present with gynecomastia and may initially have subtle systemic signs, though the normal bone age and growth velocity are reassuring against a long-standing, high-grade hormonal excess. • Primary breast neoplasms (e.g., juvenile fibroadenoma, secretory carcinoma). These are exceedingly rare, and the current ultrasound features are reassuring, but they remain in the differential if the mass exhibits atypical growth or if endocrine evaluation is entirely unrevealing. To finalize this opinion, the following are essential: • Laboratory panel: LH, FSH, estradiol (ultrasensitive), total/free testosterone, DHEAS, androstenedione, 17- hydroxyprogesterone, prolactin, β-hCG, AFP, TSH, and free T4. • Chest imaging results: To exclude a mediastinal mass (e.g., germinoma). • Testicular ultrasound: To rule out an occult testicular tumor, which is mandatory if estradiol or β-hCG is elevated, and prudent regardless given the asymmetric presentation. Recommendations 1. Laboratory Interpretation Framework: • If gonadotropins (LH, FSH) and testosterone are prepubertal, estradiol is normal/low, and tumor markers (β-hCG, AFP) are negative, the diagnosis of benign prepubertal gynecomastia with adrenarche is supported. • If estradiol is elevated with suppressed gonadotropins, urgent localization via testicular ultrasound and adrenal/chest imaging is required. • If DHEAS/androstenedione are elevated with prepubertal testosterone, this confirms adrenarche and argues against a gonadal or hCG-driven process. 2. Imaging: Review the pending chest imaging. Obtain a high-resolution scrotal/testicular ultrasound to exclude occult testicular pathology. 3. Management: If the laboratory and imaging evaluations are reassuring, expectant management with reassurance is appropriate. The family should be counseled that prepubertal/early pubertal gynecomastia is common and often self-limited. The family history of late-onset breast cancer in the paternal grandmother does not alter management or increase the risk of pediatric breast pathology. 4. Follow-up: Re-evaluate every 3 to 6 months, assessing auxology, Tanner staging, testicular volumes, and mass size. 5. Surgical Referral: Routine fine-needle aspiration or immediate biopsy is not indicated given the classic presentation and reassuring ultrasound. However, referral to pediatric surgery for tissue diagnosis (core-needle or excisional biopsy) should be pursued if the mass continues to enlarge significantly, becomes fixed or painful, develops suspicious sonographic features, or persists without regression beyond 24 months despite a benign endocrine workup. 36
