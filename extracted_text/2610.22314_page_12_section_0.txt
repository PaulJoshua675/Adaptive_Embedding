Beyond guidance, the Qwen3.5 and Gemma 4 models lose most to formulaic responding, flagged in 34–57% of Qwen3.5 replies and 18–35% of Gemma 4 replies, against at most 9% for any other counsellor. Their replies repeat a validation followed by a question about feelings whatever the patient asked, including after repeated requests for practical direction. The smallest model also loses track of the conversation: Qwen3.5 4B misses shared context in 39% of its replies. Among replies observed at each turn position, formulaic responding is flagged in 2% of replies in the first two turns and 26% in turns 19–20 (Figure 9). Counsellors keep their boundaries. Boundary issues (anthropomorphizing and out-of-scope re- sponding) do not contribute significantly to MHQR (these are flagged in at most 5.1% of any counsellor’s replies). Counsellors rarely present themselves as human, or step outside a support role with these pa- tients. However, we note that our patient pool does not model risk explicitly: related work has shown that LLMs do cross these boundaries when specifically probed (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Fidelity flags are more frequent in later sessions. Changes in MHQR between first and later sessions vary across counsellors (Figure 5). For instance, models such as GLM-5.3 and Sonnet 5 score better in later sessions, whereas Muse Glimmer and Gemma 4 perform worse. For every counsellor, later sessions add weight from replies flagged for both Factual & Contextual Fidelity and Guidance Calibration, with the share of replies with a fidelity issue more than doubling (7.6% to 17.2%). In these cases it is often the case the counsellor asks again about something the patient has already told it. Counsellors that improve offset this by exploring less overall, as expected when moving from an intake-like first session to follow-up sessions, whereas those that get worse keep exploring about what is already in their memory.
