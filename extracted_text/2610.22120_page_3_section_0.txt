Scene Reconstruction Using Ubiquitous Wireless Signals 2 PRELIMINARIES AND PROBLEM FORMULATION 2.1 WIRELESS PROPAGATION A signal transmitted from a base station reaches a receiver along a set of propagation paths de- termined by the surrounding environment. Each path is a sequence of interactions with the physical structures of the scene: it may travel directly, reflect specularly off a building, diffract at an edge, or be blocked. This composition defines a forward operator H = F(s, p) mapping scene and link geometry to the observed channel: the scene is written into the channel through the path parameters, in a form the receiver observes only in superposition. Importantly, not all parts of the environment participate in the measurement. Only structures a path interacts with, allowing free propagation, blocking the direct ray, or serving as reflection or diffraction points, enter the path set and thus the channel. We call this region the propagation- relevant set of an observation; structures outside it leave the observation exactly unchanged. 2.2 THE INVERSE PROBLEM Objective. We observe a scene through the wireless links a deployed network already maintains. A base station serves K users, where user k at position pk contributes a channel measurement Hk, giving the observation set O =  (Hk, pk) K k=1. Denoting ak for the propagation-relevant set of observation k, the observable set A = S k ak is the region of the scene influencing at least one measurement. We represent the environment as a two-dimensional occupancy grid over a square region with spatial resolution ∆R, s ∈{0, 1}N×N, where si = 1 indicates that cell i contains a structure, and seek to recover s from O, inverting F, without parametric assumptions on the number, shape, convexity, or location of the structures. Why the problem is ill-posed. Under the standard Hadamard framework (Bakushinsky & Gon- charsky, 2012), the problem is ill-posed because the observations do not uniquely determine the scene, despite a feasible solution existing. Non-uniqueness arises from three sources (Appendix B). First, structures outside A leave every measurement unchanged, so the likelihood is exactly invariant to them: flipping any combination of those cells yields a scene explaining the observations equally well, and the number of such scenes is exponential in |Ac|. Second, even inside A, the forward map is many-to-one, since finite bandwidth and finite aperture resolve delays and angles only to a limit, so scatterers displaced within those limits yield indistinguishable measurements. Third, a structure enters the channel only through the role it plays for a given link — reflecting, blocking, or leaving the signal untouched — and that role varies with where it sits relative to transmitter and receiver: the same building reflects for one user, blocks another, and is invisible to a third. Scenes whose structures play the same roles for every link are indistinguishable at any resolution, so unlike the second source, this degeneracy is not removed by better hardware. Multiple observations narrow the ambiguity by intersecting the families of scenes each admits, and additional users and reflected paths enlarge A, but the intersection does not collapse to a single scene: the invariance outside A persists, and the resolution and role ambiguities remain within it. 2.3 A PROBABILISTIC FORMULATION Because the data do not determine a unique scene, we do not seek one. We take the solution object to be the posterior over scenes p(s|O) ∝p(O|s)p(s), whose structure is dictated by the physics of wave propagation.
