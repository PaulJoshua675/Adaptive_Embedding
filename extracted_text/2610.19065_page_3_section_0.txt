We first show that GR leads on BV while DR leads on vNDCG. We then measure how popularity, rating, and brand contribute to GR’s value advantage. Finally, we rerank DR with the same static item value and test whether the gap reverses. 5.1 The Metric-Dependent GR–DR Gap We begin with the empirical disagreement. GR leads on BV, while raw DR leads on vNDCG@50, Recall@50, and target NDCG@50 on both datasets as shown in Table 1. GR wins the paired BV com- parison more than twice as often as raw DR (Figure 2(a)), and its lead remains when users without a relevant retrieved item receive zero. DR’s vNDCG@50 lead is 2.9% in 2014 and 5.1% in 2023, with confidence intervals above zero (Figure 2(b)). Value weighting com- presses DR’s relevance advantage by an order of magnitude. We conduct a full sweep on value weights to demonstrate robustness. Across the 231-point weight simplex shown in Fig. 3, the sign of the vNDCG gap holds on 223 points on 2014, failing only in popularity- dominated edge cases, and on all 231 points on 2023. 5.2 Diagnosing GR’s Value Advantage Among users where GR wins BV, we use Equation 1 to break its value lead over DR into popularity, rating, and brand. Popularity contributes for 49.8% of the lead on 2014 and 65.4% on 2023. It is the largest component on both datasets, and its share is higher on the larger 2023 catalog. Rating and brand contribute the remainder, but neither provides the same cross-dataset dominance. This pattern narrows the interpretation of GR’s value edge. Each value component is computed once per item and does not depend on the current history. The measured lift is therefore largely captured by static item-level properties, especially popularity. Repeated ex- posure in training offers a possible explanation for GR’s value lead. The released 2014 popularity score correlates with how often held out targets appear in training histories (Spearman 𝜌= 0.582).
