E MORE CASE STUDIES Full AVC-Reward input–output. Fig. 8 shows a complete input–output example of AVC- Reward. The clip contains a coherent kitchen scene with several events, including chef speech, chopping, and an off-screen phone ring. The phone ring can plausibly originate outside the frame, but the visible chef shows no reaction or other visual evidence supporting its interaction with the scene. AVC-Reward first analyzes the audio and video independently to extract their scenes and major events, and then performs audio-to-video and video-to-audio verification for the extracted events. The final stage combines the scene-level information and cross-modal evidence to judge the three consistency dimensions. The complete reasoning process is contained in the <think> block, followed by the three scores and a diagnostic conclusion. Additional evaluation cases. Fig. 9, together with the evaluation cases in Fig. 5, further shows how AVC-Reward handles different forms of complexity in generated audio-video content. The two-person conversation case in Fig. 5 tests multi-event disentanglement: two people are visible, but the audio provides clear speech evidence for only one of them. The puppet case tests multi-layer audio, where character speech, ambient factory sounds, and background music play different roles in the scene. The additional cases extend this evaluation to several other situations. The band case contains mul- tiple instruments and vocals at the same time, testing concurrent event verification. The choir case changes from a conductor to the singers and requires the evaluator to verify different valid audio- video relations across the clip. The hammer case tests precise event-level temporal verification by introducing a clear delay between the visible impact and its sound. The concert-ending case tests fine-grained visual evidence for sound production: the performance has ended and the orchestra begins packing up and bowing, although the performance audio continues. Across these cases, AVC-Reward remains close to human judgments and provides conclusions that identify the relevant event or relation. The positive choir and band examples further show that complex but valid relations can still receive high consistency judgments. Additional generation cases. Fig. 10, together with the generation cases in Fig. 5, shows how the same event-level evaluation capabilities are reflected in generation after using AVC-Reward for optimization. In the dog–cat case in Fig. 5, the optimized model better preserves sequential multi- event composition, with the dog and cat opening their mouths for their respective sounds. In the cooking case, it better handles multi-layer audio by retaining narration while also generating visible chopping with synchronized chopping sounds. The additional cases cover further generation complexities. In the waterfall case, the optimized model preserves concurrent waterfall sound and human speech. In the election-ad case, visual cuts, sound effects, footsteps, and narration form different event-dependent relations that are more consis- tently generated. The water-pouring case shows finer audio attributes, where the water-flow sound changes with the evolving visual event. The piano case shows finer visual evidence for sound pro- duction, with finger presses, key motion, and pitch changes forming a more coherent relation. To- gether, these examples qualitatively mirror the capabilities examined by AVC-Reward: finer checks of individual events, their cross-modal relations, audio attributes, and visible sound-producing evi- dence lead to more complete event generation and more consistent audio-video relations. Together, these cases cover the main capabilities examined on both the evaluation and generation sides, including multi-event composition, concurrent events, complex cross-modal relations, multi- layer audio, precise temporal verification, and fine-grained audio or visual evidence. 28
