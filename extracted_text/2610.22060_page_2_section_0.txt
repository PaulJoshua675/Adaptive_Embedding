They also sample an image with an order of magnitude less compute than continuous diffusion models, as shown in Fig. 1. Image generation quality depends on discrete tokens that are both expressive and easy for a generator to model. Previous discrete tokenizers focus on reconstruction performance, which targets the first property, expressivity. However, generation also depends on the second property, which we call generative learnability: how easily a generator fits the token distribution at a given budget. These two properties do not always align, as observed for both continuous and discrete image tokenizers and generators (Gu et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025; Ramanujan et al., 2025). Generative learnability is related to the structure of a representation (Skorokhodov et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2026), which is in turn induced by the training target. Pixel reconstruction prioritizes high-variance image details over features useful for perception (Balestriero and Lecun, 2024), and using self-supervised learning (SSL) objectives to predict features instead of pixels yields more semantically meaningful representations (Assran et al., 2023). We refer to the representations of encoders trained with SSL objectives in feature space as pretrained visual features (see Def. 1). Moreover, RAE (Zheng et al., 2026b) and RAEv2 (Singh et al., 2026) show that the benefits of pretrained visual features go beyond representation learning and successfully transfer to image generation. Wang et al. (2024) trained discrete tokens by reconstructing the features of image-understanding encoders instead of pixels, and showed that these tokens can outperform pixel-trained VQ tokens for image generation. Yet the discrete tokenizers that followed still use pixel reconstruction as the central loss, and add pretrained visual features with additional losses (Xiong et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026a; Chen et al., 2026b). This raises the question of whether pixel reconstruction loss is essential for modern discrete tokenizer training used for downstream image generation. We argue that it is not. Feature objectives should shape both the encoder, pretrained in feature space, and the discrete tokens. We introduce feature-loss tokenization (FeaTok), which separates discrete representation learning from image reconstruction. Instead of a pixel decoder and a pixel reconstruction loss, a feature decoder that reconstructs pretrained visual features shapes the discrete tokens. A separate image decoder is trained with pixel losses on detached tokens and only renders generated token sequences, so pixel losses never shape the discrete representation. We additionally discretize the encoder’s global representation (e.g., CLS token), and generate it before the patch tokens, giving them image-level context. We call this global-augmented generative modelling. Ablations in Sec. 2.2 show that FeaTok has better spatial structure and generates better than tokenizers trained with pixel or combined losses for all four encoders we study, and that global-augmented modelling contributes to this improvement. On class-conditional ImageNet-1k (Russakovsky et al., 2015) at 256×256, FeaTok advances the quality–compute Pareto front for image generation (Fig. 1). We measure quality by gFDr6, the mean Fréchet-distance ratio across six feature spaces rather than the Inception space alone (Yang et al., 2026). FeaTok improves on the strongest discrete baselines by 29% without guidance and by 25% with it, and outperforms recent continuous generators at a matched sampling cost in both settings. The gains grow at 512×512: relative to the previously best discrete model (BAR (Yu et al., 2026)), FeaTok reduces gFDr6 by 43% without guidance and 39% with guidance, up from 32% and 25% at 256×256, and it also outperforms RAE. FeaTok further achieves higher rewards than discrete and continuous baselines on three human-preference models (Kirstain et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2025) without reward-based training. Finally, reusing the ImageNet-trained tokenizer, FeaTok surpasses the baselines on three text-to-image benchmarks (Ghosh et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024a; Hu et al., 2024) after 75K pretraining updates and only 500 fine-tuning updates.
