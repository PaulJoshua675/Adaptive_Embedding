Existing proxies characterize architectures from different physical perspectives. Gradient and pruning-based methods, such as SynFlow [27], measure parameter sensitivity or information flow, while activation-based methods such as NASWOT [21] capture expressivity through feature-map statistics or activation patterns. Although these proxies provide useful training-free priors, each individual proxy usually reflects only one local property of an architecture, making its ranking reliability highly dependent on the dataset and search space. 2.3 Few-Shot Prediction and Proxy Fusion Few-shot NAS predictors aim to reduce the number of labeled architectures re- quired for accurate performance estimation. Recent methods usually rely on graph topology encoding, self-supervised learning, or supernet-based mecha- nisms [33,29,17,16]. While topology-aware predictors can improve prediction quality, they introduce additional overhead from graph construction, message passing, and structured inference. Under extremely limited labels, learning high- dimensional architecture embeddings from scratch may also suffer from cold-start difficulty and overfitting. Another related direction studies the fusion of multiple ZCPs, for example through adaptive weighting [14]. However, purely zero-cost fusion often relies on manually designed aggregation rules or search heuristics, and thus cannot di- rectly learn task-dependent relationships between proxy signals and final accu- racy. FENAS bridges few-shot prediction and ZCP fusion by representing each architecture with complementary topology-agnostic physical priors and using minimal ground-truth supervision for data-driven integration. With CAST, het- erogeneous proxy scores are aligned and transformed into a compact feature space, allowing lightweight meta-learners to adaptively combine expressivity, trainability, connectivity, and capacity cues for robust architecture ranking. 3
