Explanation significantly reduced perceived workload (F(1, 11) = 16.37, p = 0.002, η2 p = 0.598), while prior had no effect on workload (F(1, 11) < 0.01, p = 0.985, η2 p < 0.001). A Friedman test on the rankings was not significant (χ2(3) = 4.30, p = 0.231, N = 12), giving no support for H6 (mean ranks, where 1 is most preferred: Informed + Explanation = 2.08, Uninformed + Explanation = 2.17, Uninformed = 2.75, Informed = 3.00). While some participants valued directed attention (P4 noted that “getting a cue about which feature to attend to is helpful,” and P6 found it “definitely easier to teach the robot when [they] knew what it was unclear about”), others felt con- strained by focusing on a single dimension (P11 wasn’t “sure how much to prioritize [the feature] over the other factors,” and P8 instead “liked balancing the [features]” themselves). These responses suggest a trade-off: explanations help users focus their attention, but can conflict with a desire to balance multiple objectives, motivating attention schemes that broaden as features are resolved. Demonstration-based Feedback Our approach assumes that preference queries resolve under- specification with less effort than additional demonstrations. To test this, we compared NASA-TLX responses from our study’s explanation conditions against existing data from Merker et al. [3], where a separate cohort of participants gave corrective kinesthetic demonstrations in response to natural- language explanations (N = 12 each, between subjects). Physical demand was lower for prefer- ence queries (M = 3.8, SE = 1.9) than for kinesthetic demonstrations (M = 31.7, SE = 6.9; t(12.6) = 3.89, p = 0.002). Overall workload was also lower (M = 17.4 vs. M = 24.2), though not significantly (t(22.0) = 1.31, p = 0.205), and the gap shrinks once physical demand is excluded (M = 20.1 vs. M = 22.7; t(21.3) = 0.45, p = 0.658).
