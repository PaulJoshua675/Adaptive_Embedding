2.1 Rating Scale Design in Measurement Survey and psychometric research demonstrates that a rating scale design affects how humans rate the same construct. We review this evidence for the three design elements we manipulate, namely format, sign, and range. Format. Social scientists measure scalar con- structs with two broad families of rating scales. For one, attitudes, emotions, and perceptions have traditionally been measured with ordinal Likert- type scales (e.g., 1–5 or 1–7) (Likert, 2017; Anas- tasiadou, 2011),1. For another, computational tools increasingly assign continuous scores, in- cluding VADER sentiment scores ranging from −1 to 1 (Hutto and Gilbert, 2014) and Perspec- tive API toxicity scores ranging from 0 to 1 (Jig- saw, n.d.). Compared with ordinal scales, con- tinuous scales preserve more information and al- low finer-grained judgments (Treiblmaier and Filz- moser, 2011). However, studies comparing the two formats have not found that either one consistently yields higher reliability or validity (Treiblmaier and Filzmoser, 2011; Svensson, 2000). Regardless of which format is preferable, the two formats pro- duce different ratings of the same stimuli (Lawless and Malone, 1986). Range. Rating scales differ in range, that is, the number of points they offer (e.g., 3, 5, 11, or 21 points). Changing the number of points changes the measurement properties of a scale, but findings are inconclusive on whether more points are better. Some studies suggest that adding points can im- prove distributional properties and internal consis- tency (Leung, 2011; Xu and Leung, 2018; Chang, 1994), which may in turn improve validity and reli- ability (Kusmaryono et al., 2022). Others find that overly fine-grained scales may reduce consistency and reliability in certain contexts (Cai et al., 2016; Chang, 1994). Sign. A rating scale with the same number of points can be signed or unsigned. A signed scale includes negative values and is centered on 0 (e.g., −1 to 1), whereas an unsigned scale uses only non- negative values (e.g., 1 to 3). The sign of the scale changes how respondents interpret its endpoints. For example in an empirical study, when the same verbal endpoints were labeled −5 to +5 instead of 0 to 10, human respondents selected the lower half of the scale less often.
