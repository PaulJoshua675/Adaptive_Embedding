LLM-based multi-agent systems. LLM-based multi-agent systems combine role specialization, communication, and memory to support collective behavior. Generative Agents studies emergent social interactions among agents with memory and reflection (Park et al., 2023), while CAMEL ex- plores cooperation through role-playing (Li et al., 2023). MetaGPT and ChatDev organize software development through structured workflows and specialized roles (Hong et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024); AutoGen and AgentVerse provide frameworks for programmable conversations and collab- orative agent teams (Wu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). Multiagent debate improves reasoning through iterative exchange and revision of proposed answers (Du et al., 2024). Beyond manu- ally specified workflows, GPTSwarm optimizes graph connectivity (Zhuge et al., 2024), AgentNet adapts decentralized routing through experience and evolving connections (Yang et al., 2025), and Graph-of-Agents selects agents and refines responses through graph-based collaboration (Yun et al., 2026). However, these systems generally adapt interactions at inference time. MPT learns policies through weight updates, allowing agent capabilities and self-orchestration to improve together. Multi-agent reinforcement learning. Learning decentralized policies from collective feedback is a central problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning. MADDPG and MAPPO use centralized training with decentralized execution (Lowe et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2022), while VDN and QMIX factorize team value functions into agent-level utilities (Sunehag et al., 2018; Rashid et al., 2020). COMA addresses credit assignment through a counterfactual baseline that marginalizes one agent’s action while holding the others fixed (Foerster et al., 2018). Coordination is also learned explicitly: RIAL and DIAL learn communication protocols (Foerster et al., 2016), CommNet jointly learns con- tinuous communication and control (Sukhbaatar et al., 2016), and IC3Net learns when agents should communicate (Singh et al., 2019). Recent work extends multi-agent training to LLMs: Multiagent Finetuning updates specialized models using their own interaction data (Subramaniam et al., 2025); Dr. MAS stabilizes reinforcement learning through agent-wise advantage normalization (Feng et al., 2026); and CoLLM studies centralized and decentralized critics for training decentralized LLM col- laborators (Liu et al., 2026). However, these methods do not connect the choice of the next agent with how downstream reward is distributed across agents. MPT couples turn allocation and credit assignment through learned bids, using task feedback to jointly improve participation and policies. Economics in agents. Economic coordination offers a longstanding perspective on distributed intelligence. Hayek emphasizes how prices communicate dispersed knowledge (Hayek, 1945), while Minsky describes intelligence as arising from interactions among individually limited com- ponents (Minsky, 1986). The Contract Net Protocol allocates tasks through negotiation (Smith, 1988), and market-oriented programming uses markets for decentralized resource allocation (Well- man, 1993). Economic mechanisms also connect participation to learning: Holland’s bucket brigade transfers credit along chains of active rules (Holland, 1995), and Baum’s Hayek Machine develops economic credit assignment (Baum, 1996; 1999). More recently, Economy of Minds uses auctions to coordinate LLM agents and guide the evolution of prompts and population with frozen model weights (Qi et al., 2026). SALE selects among agents’ proposed strategies using cost–value scoring and shared auction memory (Alazraki et al., 2026). However, recent LLM-based economic systems primarily use feedback to evolve prompts while keeping weights frozen. MPT uses feedback to train action and bidding policies, enabling agents to learn how to act and when to participate. 5
