The candidates follow a fixed order: top-center, top-left, top-right, center, middle-left, middle-right, bottom- center, bottom-left, bottom-right. After each of the first two decisions, the selected rectangle is cropped from the original pixels, encoded by the same frozen visual pipeline, and appended with its decision history. Cropping from the original rather than from the previous crop avoids compounding resampling. The original view stays in context for global cues, while the crop spends visual tokens on the selected region. Candidate text describes the current region, but coordinates always refer to the original image. After three decisions, the output is the center of B3, which gives an effective 27 × 27 grid with a correct-cell center error of at most 1/54 of the image width or height per axis. An error at an earlier decision can exclude the correct region, so this geometric bound does not guarantee prediction accuracy. Crop refinement costs two additional image encodings per point, and its benefit is tested against matched alternatives in Section 4.3. Extending to multiple points prediction. A single greedy path returns one location. To recover several locations with the same trained model, we expand multiple paths and assign each final cell the product of its conditional probabilities along the path. Together, these probabilities form a spatial map in which separated high-probability regions can yield multiple predictions. We retain cells above a fixed threshold relative to the map’s peak, group connected cells, and return the center of the highest-probability cell in each group. The number of output points is determined by the resulting groups. This requires no additional training or learned stop decision. Full expansion visits 91 decision nodes and yields 729 cells; the evaluation decoder prunes low-probability root branches to reduce this cost. Traversal and component extraction use model predictions only, with no target masks or true point counts. Section A.4 gives the fixed decoding rules, and Section F.2 evaluates their quality and computational tradeoffs. 3.2 Model Architecture The framework in Section 3.1 defines the candidate classes and refinement rules; a model must still deter- mine which class is supported by the visual observation and question. Following Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026), we adapt a pretrained VLM to make these decisions. Its contextual representations draw on visual and linguistic knowledge acquired during large-scale pretraining, providing a basis for interpreting the scene and evaluating possible answers.
