Preprint. Under Review. Prompt 3: Target Paper Summarization Prompt System: You are an expert research scientist. You are given (a) a numbered set of summarized inspiration documents and (b) the full text of a target paper that was written building on those inspirations. Your job is to recover the single core research idea of the target paper and express it in a fixed SI schema (problem gap / core claim / method / inspiration), grounding it in the numbered inspiration documents you were given -- NOT in the target paper’s own bibliography. Be faithful to the target paper: do not invent contributions it does not make. Return only a single JSON object. User: You are given a set of inspiration documents (each an already-summarized paper) and the full text of a TARGET PAPER that builds on them. Recover the target paper’s SINGLE core research idea and express it as one idea object in the SI schema below. The fields play DIFFERENT roles; a card whose fields collapse into restatements of each other is not useful. • problem gap: the gap the target paper addresses, drawn from across the inspiration documents -- the open question, tension, or unresolved difficulty that connects them and that none resolves on its own. State it as one clear gap, NOT as the target paper’s specific solution to it. BEGIN this field with the single best-fitting OPPORTUNITY PATTERN label in square brackets, chosen from the taxonomy below, then the prose gap (1--2 sentences). Pick the ONE dominant pattern even if several apply. If NONE genuinely fits, use ‘‘[Other]’’ rather than forcing a poor match -- a wrong label is worse than ‘‘[Other]’’. Example: ‘‘[Bridge Opportunity] Work on X and on Y each ... but neither connects ...’’. • core claim: the couple of sentences the whole idea lives or dies on -- ‘‘we claim that X.’’ It must be something that could turn out to be wrong: a definite, testable assertion/hypothesis/insight, not a topic or a direction. Do not just restate the gap, and do not describe what you would do about it. From these sentences alone a reader should know exactly what the target paper is betting on (1--2 sentences). • method: at a high level, HOW the claim WOULD BE turned into research -- the kind of move (combining ideas, extending to a new setting, making a fragile result robust, deriving formally, mapping a space empirically, building an artifact) and how that move addresses the gap. Write it as a PROPOSED approach, forward-looking, as if the idea has not yet been carried out: use present/infinitive framing (‘‘to test this, X’’; ‘‘the approach is to X’’), NOT past tense and NOT ‘‘the authors/the paper do X’’. Do not narrate what was done or built. Concrete specifics (a setting, a target system, a dataset) may be named, but state them as part of the plan, never as accomplished facts. Keep it at the level of an approach, NOT a step-by-step protocol. BEGIN this field with the single best-fitting METHOD PARADIGM label in square brackets, chosen from the taxonomy below, then the prose approach (2--3 sentences). Pick the ONE dominant paradigm even if several apply. If NONE genuinely fits, use ‘‘[Other]’’. Example: ‘‘[Synthesis / Unification] To test this, the approach is to ...’’. • inspiration: prose (three to five sentences) that ARGUES how the idea arises FROM the inspiration documents -- the relationship BETWEEN them, not a per-document restatement. This field must be CRITICAL, not a credit ledger. It must (1) name each cited source’s limitation -- for every Document leaned on, say what it got wrong, missed, stopped short of, or left unresolved, so the idea reads as a response to a concrete shortcoming, not a recombination of things that already worked (‘‘Document 3 measured X but only under assumption A’’); and (2) justify the non-obvious leap -- argue why connecting these documents is non-trivial, so the idea does not read as the obvious next step anyone would take (if the connection IS routine, say that plainly rather than inflating it). The idea may combine sources (naming which supplied the gap vs. the method vs. the analogy), resolve a tension between them, reframe or invert what a source assumed, or rest mainly on an idea imported from outside the provided documents, named explicitly (it will carry no Document ⟨N⟩). Refer to the numbered documents by their exact ‘‘Document ⟨N⟩’’ labels wherever the idea genuinely builds on them; do not invent a document link. Write problem gap, core claim, and method so they read the same whether or not the idea has been tested: state the gap, the claim/bet, and the approach, NOT the outcome. Do NOT report results, metrics, or use evaluation verbs (‘‘show’’, ‘‘achieve’’, ‘‘outperform’’). Do NOT narrate the work as already completed: avoid past tense and completed-action verbs (‘‘designed’’, ‘‘built’’, ‘‘ran’’, ‘‘evaluated’’, ‘‘conducted’’) and do not attribute actions to ‘‘the authors’’ or ‘‘the paper’’ -- the card describes a forward-looking idea, not a recounting of what a paper did. Keep problem gap broad enough that core claim is doing real narrowing work.
