We include two controls: random, which re- moves the same number of directions as subspace removal but does so at random, and permuted, which subtracts the mean of a mismatched lan- guage. All directions are estimated over five in- dependent estimation corpora samples, and we re- port the mean and standard deviation across these samples, which measure how much each correction depends on the particular estimation sample rather than on language identity. Every correction recovers recall, but not in the same way. Mean removal, subspace removal, and steering raise cross-lingual recall over the un- touched baseline, and differ in what else moves. Mean removal and steering recover recall while cut- ting the own-language share that drives the crowd- ing: on mE5-large/Belebele steering lowers it from 69% to 23% and mean removal to 44%. Where rotation does help, it does so by pulling rankings to- ward English, so its English share climbs, most vis- ibly on mE5-large/Multi-EuP, where it rises from 35% to 70%, reproducing the bias the correction is meant to remove. Subspace removal is the mildest, moving every quantity only slightly. We report paired significance tests in Appendix G. We note that our design choices are not load-bearing: the rank-fusion constant and the weight wℓ(x) do not affect the results (Appendix F). Mean removal and steering trade off. Steering gives the higher recall and the lower own-language share, de-crowding more aggressively: on the same cell, steering drives the own-language share to 8% against 18% for mean removal, at recall 98.8. Steer- ing also holds up on Multi-EuP, the non-parallel collection, improving recall on the mE5 models, whereas mean removal degrades mE5-base there, where its per-language means are least reliable. Be- tween the two effective corrections, the choice is a trade-off rather than a ranking. On the parallel collections, steering gives the higher nDCG: on Qwen3-8B/Belebele it reaches 83.9 against 82.9 for mean removal. Mean removal leads instead to the non-parallel Multi-EuP and in cross-lingual rank agreement, so its rankings are better ordered where steering’s gains are smallest. Appendix H breaks these effects down by language, showing that both corrections help the lowest-scoring lan- guages most. Steering’s direction is related to but not reducible to the language-mean offset; we ana- lyze this in Appendix I. Controls establish specificity. Two controls con- firm that the corrections act on language identity rather than on the geometry of the space in general. Removing an equal number of random directions, in place of the language subspace, leaves retrieval within noise of the baseline, so the effect of sub- space removal is not merely a consequence of dis- carding dimensions. Subtracting a mismatched lan- guage’s mean, drawn by permuting the language- to-mean assignment, sharply worsens retrieval and drives the own-language share toward saturation, so it is the correct per-language mean that matters, not the act of centering itself. The corrections that work therefore do so because they target language identity specifically.
