Figure 5 provides representative qualitative comparisons of the alter- native policies. We further evaluate how accurately each verification signal predicts the oracle in Appendix C.1 and provide additional ablations on our head in Appendix C.2. Effect of verification tolerance. Table 4 shows how ϵ controls the quality-efficiency trade-off on Lingbot. Increasing ϵ reduces mean denoising steps, with ϵ = 0.20 and ϵ = 0.24 maintaining the four-step generator’s quality while achieving lower Drift. Larger tolerances accept insufficiently refined drafts and degrade quality, highlighting the need to balance early acceptance with further refinement. Effect of verification signals. We adapt two existing selection criteria to token-wise commitment in latent space. Inspired by MotionCache (Xu et al., 2026), we measure temporal differences between adjacent latent frames at the same spatial position. We also adapt RAS’s predicted-noise dispersion signal (Liu et al., 2026) to the channel variance of each predicted clean latent token. Thresholds are calibrated on held-out data to obtain comparable mean denoising steps. As shown in Table 5, both adapted signals yield lower quality than our learned verifier and uniform sampling at similar mean steps. Temporal differences, for example, can reflect both camera-induced changes and intermediate distortions, limiting their reliability as convergence signals (Figure 5). These results support directly predicting each draft’s remaining convergence gap. Comparison with oracle verification. We additionally evaluate an oracle verifier that computes the full-step reference for each chunk using the same initial noise and conditioning, then uses the mea- sured convergence gaps to determine token acceptance. We apply the same tolerance and spatiotem- poral smoothing as the learned verifier. Our learned verifier achieves generation quality comparable to oracle verification without computing the full-step reference at inference (Table 5). 8
