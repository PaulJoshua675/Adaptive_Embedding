As discussed in the introduction, the requirement for logprob access and for per-model manual templates, together with the up-to-10,000 target queries per behavior that their random search consumes, limits applicability to realistic black-box deployment scenarios. 2.2.3 Black-Box Iterative LLM-Guided Methods. Chao et al. [9] developed PAIR (Prompt Automatic Iterative Refinement), which pairs an attacker LLM with an evaluator in a per-behavior refinement loop. Mehrotra et al. [28] proposed Tree of Attacks with Pruning (TAP), which structures the search as a branching tree and discards unpromising branches via a pruning heuristic. Reported ASR for both methods varies substantially with target-model generation: both are highly effective against early targets such as Vicuna and GPT-3.5 and against GPT-4/GPT-4-Turbo (TAP reports over 80% on these closed models), but degrade sharply against recent safety-aligned models such as Llama-2-Chat and Claude (see Table 1). Both methods operate strictly per-behavior, optimize only ASR, lack population diversity, and offer no principled mechanism for exploring multiple attack strategies within a single run. 2.2.4 Black-Box Population-Based and Fuzzing Methods. Yu et al. [41] introduced GPTFUZZER, which applies software fuzzing principles to LLM red-teaming by mutating seed prompts with random operators (75–88% ASR). Liu et al. [25] extended AutoDAN to a lifelong-learning agent, AutoDAN-Turbo, that autonomously discovers and accumulates jailbreak strategies across sessions through a strategy library, achieving up to 97.6% ASR on individual HarmBench targets (ICLR 2025). AutoDAN-Turbo’s strategy-library mechanism is the closest prior approach to REPRO’s Bayesian strategy selection, though it does not employ multi-parent crossover or structured failure analysis. Liang et al. [22] proposed KDA (Knowledge-Distilled Attacker), which distills attack knowledge from multiple teacher attackers (AutoDAN, PAIR, GPTFuzzer) into a single model capable of generating diverse jailbreak prompts, achieving up to 100% ASR on Vicuna and Mistral on HarmBench while maintaining high diversity across attack formats. Zhao et al. [44] introduced DAGR, which structures attack search as a diversified tree with obfuscation at each depth level, achieving up to 99.33% ASR on HarmBench with substantially fewer queries than TAP and PAIR. Samvelyan et al. [34] proposed Rainbow Teaming, which applies quality-diversity optimization to adversarial prompt generation to produce diverse successful attacks rather than a single high-quality one. Liao and Sun [23] introduced AmpleGCG, which trains a generative model over successful GCG-style adversarial suffixes to produce universal and transferable jailbreaks and reports high transfer ASR to closed-source models including GPT-3.5; although AmpleGCG is trained in a white-box setting, its deployment-time transfer results demonstrate that universal suffixes optimized jointly across behaviors can outperform per-behavior optimization, and this observation motivates REPRO’s explicit XFER metric as a first-class optimization objective rather than a post-hoc evaluation. Deng et al. [11] developed MasterKey, whose focus on cross-chatbot transferability aligns conceptually with REPRO’s XFER metric but without multi-objective optimization. REPRO extends the population- based direction with multi-objective Pareto selection, Bayesian strategy scoring via Thompson Sampling, structured failure analysis (Section 3), and semantic crossover as a new recombination operator, none of which appear in these prior systems. 2.2.5 Attack Techniques Incorporated into REPRO’s Mutation Portfolio.
