Despite these advances, the supervisory signal in most systems remains a binary reward at the end of each trajectory, leaving the contribution of search decisions inside long rollouts unidentified. 2.2 Credit Assignment for Search Agents A growing line of work aims to address long- horizon credit assignment through process-level supervision. GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) extends GRPO with anchor-state grouping, computing step- level advantages by matching trajectories that re- visit the same environment state; this mechanism requires reliable state recurrence, which is rare in deep search where retrieved contents and reason- ing histories are open-ended. IGPO (Wang et al., 2026) instead derives dense step rewards from the model’s own probability of producing the correct answer, sidestepping state matching but anchor- ing the signal to internal confidence that can be biased by long, noisy retrieved contexts. A par- allel direction introduces externally grounded su- pervision through learned reward models or rubric decompositions (Zhang et al., 2026b, 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025), providing denser feed- back than outcome rewards but relying on learned proxies whose calibration is itself a research ques- tion, and without producing step groupings that make semantically equivalent decisions directly comparable across trajectories. KnowGPO instead anchors credit assignment to an externally verifi- able representation of progress, the set of satisfied atomic knowledge conditions, and uses this repre- sentation as both the grouping criterion for within- group comparison and the basis of a process reward grounded in the environment. 3 Method 3.1 Problem Formulation We formulate deep search as a multi-turn sequen- tial decision process. Given a query Q and a refer- ence answer y∗, an agent interacts with a retrieval environment over time steps t ∈{1, . . . , T}. At each step the agent produces a reasoning trace and an action at ∈{search, answer} from its policy πθ(· | ht, Q), where ht = (a1, o1, . . . , at−1, ot−1) is the interaction history and ot is the observa- tion returned by the environment (e.g., retrieved documents for search, or a terminal signal for answer). A complete trajectory is denoted τ = (a1, o1, . . . , aT , oT ) and terminates with a binary outcome reward R(τ) = I(ans(τ) = y∗). We build on group-relative policy optimization, which estimates advantages from a group of N trajecto- ries sampled for the same Q and avoids learning a separate value network. The challenge addressed throughout this section is that R(τ) alone provides no signal for distinguishing useful, redundant, or harmful steps inside a long-horizon trajectory. 3.2 Knowledge State A central design choice in KnowGPO is to ab- stract the progress of a search trajectory through the task-relevant knowledge it has acquired, rather than through its surface observations. We realize this abstraction in two steps: constructing a query- specific set of atomic knowledge conditions, and representing each trajectory step by the subset of conditions it has satisfied. Knowledge conditions. For each query Q with reference answer y∗, we construct a finite set of atomic knowledge conditions K(Q) = {k1, . . . , kK}, (1) where each kj is a single-hop factual constraint needed to derive y∗. Intuitively, K(Q) encodes the answer’s logical skeleton: a trajectory cannot reli- ably produce y∗without acquiring every kj. We construct K(Q) through a two-stage pipeline that first identifies the reasoning skeleton implied by Q (with placeholders for unknown entities) and then grounds each placeholder using y∗and a successful reference trajectory. This decoupling produces a trajectory-agnostic knowledge space and ensures three properties: each condition encodes a single fact (atomicity), the conditions jointly support the full reasoning chain to y∗(completeness), and en- tity references remain consistent across the query, the answer, and the search trajectories (grounding consistency).
