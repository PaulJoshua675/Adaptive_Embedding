The circuit processes this sequence incrementally, retaining only a few vectors at a time and grouping several consecutive vectors when a vector cannot be processed alone. Once the null vector supplies a locator σ, the circuit still avoids constructing the whole evaluator ω or the decoded error word. It scans the curve points in small batches called stripes, combines the locator and syndromes only for the current stripe, applies those points’ phase contributions, and clears the temporary data before continuing. The circuit thus has two stages: a locator walk that never stores the matrix, and a space-efficient phase scan. The walk freezes on the rare inputs that need extra work and repairs them at scheduled rounds. Its reversal recomputes most decisions and stores one bit per round. The locator as a null vector. The discovered circuit looks for the locator in a fixed space that is large enough to always contain one. Following the linear-algebra strategy of Skorobogatov and Vlăduţ [48], it uses the span of the 48 monomials ψ0, . . . , ψ47 of weighted degree at most 75. Writing σ = P47 k=0 σkψk, each error point imposes one linear condition P47 k=0 σkψk(Pj) = 0 on the 48 coefficients. There are at most 47 conditions on 48 unknowns, so a nonzero locator always exists. The error points are unknown, so the circuit cannot impose these conditions directly. It replaces them with a condition that it can compute from the syndromes. Define the symmetric 48 × 48 matrix Akk′ = S(ψkψk′). Each product ψkψk′ has weighted degree at most 150, so every entry of A is a linear function of the supplied syndromes. The coefficient vector σ = (σ0, . . . , σ47)T of every locator lies in the null space of A, and almost always the converse holds as well. To see this, let E be the set of error points and let Mjk = ψk(Pj) for Pj ∈E. Then A = M T diag(ej) M, Mσ = (σ(Pj) : Pj ∈E)T. (45) A locator has Mσ = 0 and therefore Aσ = 0. Conversely, when the conditions σ(Pj) = 0 are independent, M has full row rank. The error values are nonzero, so Aσ = 0 then implies Mσ = 0, and σ is a locator. The conditions are dependent with probability ≈8 × 10−8 under the DQI input distribution. Applying A without storing it. The circuit needs only the product Au for a given vector u. Computing it directly would take 482 = 2,304 field multiplications. Let U = P k ukψk be the function with coefficient vector u. Then (Au)k′ = S(ψk′U): each entry is the syndrome of a product of two functions. Every such product lies in the span of the 123 monomials, so its values at 123 suitably chosen curve points R1, . . . , R123 determine it. On this span the syndrome is therefore a weighted sum of point values, S(f) = P123 i=1 ηif(Ri), with weights ηi that are a fixed linear function of the supplied syndromes. Writing Φik = ψk(Ri) gives Au = ΦT diag(η)Φu. (46) Here Φu evaluates U at the points, the diagonal map multiplies each value by its weight, and ΦT returns the weighted values to the 48 coefficients. The maps Φ and ΦT are fixed and linear, so they cost only CNOTs. The only Toffolis are in the 123 pointwise products, each a 15-Toffoli multiplication in F64 [49, 50]. One application of A thus costs 123 × 15 = 1,845 Toffolis. The same pattern recurs below, and we call it evaluation–interpolation: the circuit evaluates at fixed points, multiplies the values pointwise, and maps the products back linearly. Lanczos recurrence. A null vector of A can be found from repeated applications of A. Starting from a fixed seed y0, generate the Krylov vectors y0, Ay0, A2y0, . . .. They all lie in a 48-dimensional space, so they must become linearly dependent: there are coefficients, not all zero, with λ0y0 + λ1Ay0 + · · · + λNANy0 = 0. If the seed is not orthogonal to the entire null space, symmetry forces λ0 = 0. To see this, choose v in the null space of A with vTy0̸ = 0. Symmetry gives vTAry0 = 0 for r ≥1, so the inner product of the dependence with v leaves λ0vTy0 = 0. Then σ = N X r=1 λrAr−1y0, Aσ = N X r=1 λrAry0 = 0 (47) is the desired null vector. In the rare case where λ0̸ = 0, the decoding fails. Used directly, this would require storing every Krylov vector until the dependence appears. The Lanczos algorithm avoids that. It maintains an orthogonal basis w0, w1, . . . whose first r + 1 vectors span the first r + 1 Krylov vectors: it applies A to the newest basis vector and subtracts the components along the existing basis. Because A is symmetric, Awr is already orthogonal to every basis vector except wr−1 and wr, so Awr ∈span{wr−1, wr, wr+1}. (48) Constructing the next vector therefore needs only the current and the preceding basis vectors. A zero result means that the Krylov vectors have become dependent.
