Simulation has become a primary source of robot manip- ulation data, but building simulated environments has long been the bottleneck: each one requires a task de- sign, semantically appropriate assets, a plausible layout, and a training signal, often all specified by hand [38]. Generative simulation removes much of that cost. Cod- ing agents now synthesize scenes, articulations, and task specifications directly [25, 37–39, 43], and language models author the reward functions that accompany them [42, 48]. Environments are no longer scarce. A central challenge remaining is deciding which en- vironments to build next and what supervision will help the policy to learn from them. Existing engines sample variation from hand-specified ranges [29], refine a scene until it is physically valid [39, 43], or allocate budget by per-task success rate [10]. These signals indicate whether an environment is valid and how well the current policy performs, but do not by themselves measure how much the policy improves through training.
