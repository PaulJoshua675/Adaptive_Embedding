Role labels should likewise not be equated with validated medical specialization. [2024], Luz de Araujo et al. [2025]. 2.5 Human–AI Collaboration, Automation Bias, and Clinical Safety Even a strong standalone LLM does not guarantee improvement in a physician–AI team. In a randomized diagnostic trial, access to GPT-4 did not produce a statistically significant improvement in the primary diagnostic-reasoning outcome compared with conventional resources, despite strong exploratory performance of GPT-4 alone Goh et al. [2024]. In a subsequent randomized management trial, physicians using GPT-4 achieved higher scores on patient-care tasks but required more time, and the physician-plus-GPT condition did not automatically outperform GPT alone Goh et al. [2025]. In a 2026 randomized controlled trial, both first-opinion and second-opinion AI workflows improved diagnostic performance relative to conventional resources, yet the collaborative workflow again did not demonstrate automatic superiority over the AI-alone condition Everett et al. [2026]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in 2026 further support the heterogeneity of human–LLM collaboration. For several diagnostic endpoints, LLM-assisted clinicians outperform clinicians alone on average, but effect sizes vary across models, study designs, and case types, while risks of bias and publication bias remain substantial Wang et al. [2026], Chen et al. [2026b]. These studies concern clinician outcomes and human–AI interaction, which are outside the scope of the present benchmark; here, physician ratings are used as an expert reference for the quality of the generated material itself. A separate concern is overreliance on incorrect AI advice. In a randomized vignette study, Jabbour et al. found that systematically biased AI recommendations reduced clinicians’ diagnostic accuracy, and visual explanations did not eliminate this effect Jabbour et al. [2023]. Safety evaluation should therefore account not only for factual or therapeutic errors in an LLM-generated response, but also for the possibility that a plausibly formulated second opinion may shift a physician’s decision in the wrong direction. LLM-generated explanations can themselves influence clinical acceptance: Umerenkov et al. found that explanations increased physicians’ agreement with presented diagnoses while also containing nontrivial errors Umerenkov et al. [2023]. Persuasiveness and clinical correctness should therefore be evaluated separately. 2.6 LLM-as-a-Judge and Physician Expert Evaluation Automated evaluation of free-form medical responses is attractive because physician review is expensive and variable, but the available evidence does not support treating LLM-based evaluation as an unconditional surrogate for ground truth. Croxford et al. reported high agreement between LLM-as-a-judge and human evaluation in the relatively constrained task of clinical summarization Croxford et al. [2025]. Zhou et al. likewise investigated automated expert- level evaluation of medical reasoning Zhou et al. [2026]. More recent studies, however, suggest substantial task- and criterion-dependence. In a multicenter study of real de-identified cases, ratings from more than 400 physicians differed substantially from matched AI evaluators Shi et al. [2026]. Williams et al. compared five LLM judges with clinicians and found that even the best-performing judge reproduced human ratings for only a subset of eleven criteria, while jury-style aggregation produced only limited improvements in agreement Williams et al. [2026]. These findings motivate a more conservative validation framework: physicians remain the primary expert reference, while an LLM judge should be validated against physician ratings separately for each evaluation criterion. Agreement on one dimension, such as factual completeness, should not be assumed to generalize to treatment appropriateness, safety, or uncertainty calibration. 5
