Memory is a limiting factor in training large language models (LLMs), larger models and longer sequence lengths have increased memory requirements. Weights, optimizer buffers and transient activation caches all consume memory. Although a slower device can be compensated for with longer training, a memory limitation is much more difficult to overcome. Offloading storage to the CPU (Rhu et al., 2016; Ren et al., 2021), or even NVMe (Rajbhandari et al., 2021) allows for larger models, but can easily suffer from IO bottlenecks. The dominant methods to tackle this trade off accuracy for storage. Low precision training datatypes such as FP8 (Micikevicius et al., 2022) and FP4 (Chmiel et al., 2025) use fewer bits per value. However, intermediate activations and caches still use higher precision to ensure numerical stability and hardware support is limited to the most modern hardware. Activation quantization has been explored down to 2 bits per value in ActNN (Chen et al., 2021), while other methods exploited the low rank nature of activations (Shamshoum et al., 2025). Optimizer states have been quantized to 8 bits (Dettmers et al., 2022). 3-4 bit weight quantization methods such as GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) and AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) are widely adopted for inference. These methods are designed to minimise accuracy loss, but are not entirely lossless and require careful tuning to avoid accuracy degradation. Lossless compression is an alternative. Gist (Jain et al., 2018) applies lossless compression to ReLU activations, but is not universally applicable. General methods have been developed to take advantage of compression for inference (Zhang et al., 2025; Hershcovitch et al., 2025; Guo and Joshi, 2026; Fan et al., 2026). However, these methods have not been applied for training. Unlike inference, weights and optimizer states are updated every step, and activations must persist between the forward and backward pass.
