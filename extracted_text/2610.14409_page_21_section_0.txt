Measured curvature. On the CIFAR-10 replication checkpoint at the operating γ = 10−4, power iteration on Hessian–vector products gives a largest Hessian eigenvalue µmax of 1.0001 in error coordinates and at least 8 × 104 in state coordinates. Gradient descent at step dt is stable only for dt < 2/µmax: below 2 in error coordinates, which admits the deadbeat step dt = 1, and below 2.4 × 10−5 in state coordinates. The state parameterisation is impractically slow. At dt = 1 the state relaxation diverges within a few sweeps, in float64 as in float32, so the failure is a dynamical instability rather than a precision floor. Below the stability bound it does converge, monotonically and to the same fixed point as the error relaxation, as a shared stationary point requires. But it converges at a measured rate implying a condition number of about 2×107: at dt = 2×10−5, reaching equilibrium takes of the order of 108 sweeps, against one in error coordinates. This cost is a property of the explicit discretisation rather than of the dynamics: a continuous system settles at the rate of its slowest mode, not its fastest, and Goemaere et al. (2026, App. B.5) make the same point, that the decay which makes discrete state updates slow is an artefact of time discretisation. Consequences. The error parameterisation is therefore not just a convenience but what makes the relaxation of this network practical. Three further properties follow from its near-identity Hessian, each measured elsewhere in the paper. The relaxation reaches equilibrium in a single deadbeat sweep from feedforward initialisation (App. F). The implicit-gradient solve of Section 3.2 inverts a matrix already close to the identity, so one conjugate-gradient step is as accurate as fifty (App. H). Finally, float32 suffices: at the operating γ the velocity’s deviation from autograd’s sits below the arithmetic’s own floor (Section 4.1). Part of the reason is that the error parameterisation never forms an error as the difference of two nearly equal states, which in state coordinates costs significant digits of precision in every error. F MEASURING THE VELOCITY CORRESPONDENCE Measurement. The PCN relaxation velocities vPCN and the FFN autograd velocities vFFN are both evaluated in the stated arithmetic, and each saturates at its own floor. The highest is ∼2×10−4, for float32 tensors whose multiplications run in truncated TF32 precision on the tensor cores — the default A100 math mode, under which all our CIFAR-10 training and FID runs were performed. Forcing those multiplications to full float32 lowers it to ∼4 × 10−7, and float64 does not saturate anywhere in the measured range, though it requires patching the adopted architecture’s GroupNorm wrapper, which otherwise casts to float32 unconditionally and would impose a spurious float32 floor. Direction and magnitude. Consider a single interpolant, and let ϕ be the angle between vPCN and vFFN. Resolving vPCN along and perpendicular to vFFN gives components ∥vPCN∥cos ϕ and ∥vPCN∥sin ϕ, so the deviation ∆v = vPCN −vFFN has ∥∆v∥∥= ∥vPCN∥cos ϕ −∥vFFN∥ , ∥∆v⊥∥= ∥vPCN∥sin ϕ. (39) For ϕ ≪1 these reduce to ∥vPCN∥−∥vFFN∥ and ∥vPCN∥ϕ: the parallel component is an error in the velocity’s magnitude, the perpendicular component an error in its direction. Normalising both by ∥vFFN∥gives the relative errors ϵ∥≈ ∥vPCN∥/∥vFFN∥−1 and ϵ⊥≈ϕ, the second using ∥vPCN∥/∥vFFN∥= 1 + O(γ). Since the components are orthogonal, ϵ2 = ϵ2 ∥+ ϵ2 ⊥, (40) where ϵ is the total relative deviation of Figure 2. Figure 3a plots all three: both components are O(γ), with direction carrying roughly two-thirds of ϵ2 across the range.
