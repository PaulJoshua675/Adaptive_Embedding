A team of Gemini 4 Argon agents beats Minecraft 8. Limitations • Perception and navigation through code. Mineflayer tells the agents exactly which blocks and creatures are around them, including blocks behind solid rock and the dragon’s internal state, information that a human player has to see and interpret or cannot see at all, and its pathfinding plugin plans routes around obstacles, which humans do by eye. Playing from pixels with a keyboard and mouse, as SIMA 2 does, remains much harder. • Easy difficulty. Monsters spawn and attack, but deal less damage than on Normal or Hard, and hunger cannot kill a player. • Prior knowledge. Gemini 4 Argon knows a great deal about Minecraft and Mineflayer from its training data. The run tests whether a general-purpose coding agent can turn that knowledge into working programs and team coordination over a day-long task, not whether it can play a game it has never seen. • Many failures along the way. The players died 605 times, mostly to monsters; 22% of programs ended in an error; the fight in the End took nearly 14 hours. • Infrastructure. The world server restarted 6 times during the run, interrupting the game each time. • A single run. We describe a single run, the first launch we tried, which cannot show how reliably the setup succeeds. 9. Responsible development The agents ran in a sandboxed Minecraft world and their in-game programs could control only their own character: they could neither start processes nor write files, and none used a server command. The agents themselves ran on dedicated cloud machines, with the tools they use for software work. A fifth agent, the Observer, followed the session as an independent witness to watch for cheating or specification gaming; neither the Observer nor our manual review of a sample of the logs found any. Every program, message and game event was recorded. Researchers could also watch the run live on a dashboard and pause or stop it at any time, but had no cause to intervene. Studying how teams of agents coordinate in a setting where every step is visible is one way to understand such systems before they are trusted with real-world tasks. The skills required to solve Minecraft such as coordinating a team, recovering from failure, and pursuing a goal for more than a day, are also what agents need to work reliably alongside people.
