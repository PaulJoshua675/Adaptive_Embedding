Ablation Study We evaluate the role of asymmetric conditioning on EdiVal- 10 by varying the inputs provided to the teacher, auxiliary editor, and student, and the length of the training rollouts. The teacher and auxiliary editor receive identical condition- ing in all variants. All variants are trained for 100 student updates. Table 2 reports the quantitative results, and Fig- ure 5 illustrates the conditioning variants qualitatively. Teacher and auxiliary conditioning. Our teacher and auxiliary editor are conditioned on the original image and the compound instruction, anchoring score distillation to the clean source. We compare this design with two alternatives: additionally providing the previous student output to both models, and replacing the original image and compound in- struction with the previous output and current instruction. Both alternatives substantially reduce preservation: consis- tency drops from 3.85 to 2.81 and 2.28, respectively, com- pared with 3.03 for the pretrained base model. Figure 5 illustrates the former variant: as with the base model, the image degrades over the turns, and blotchy noise spreads over the walls and the rug. These results suggest that con- ditioning the score models on intermediate student outputs can reinforce accumulated artifacts rather than correct them. Student conditioning. Our student receives only the pre- vious output and current instruction. Providing the origi- nal image as an additional reference improves consistency from 3.85 to 4.08, but reduces instruction following from 3.38 to 2.80. As shown in Figure 5, earlier edits are undone as later ones are applied: the recoloured stool and ball re- turn to their original colours. Thus, stronger preservation of the source does not necessarily imply successful multi- turn editing. Our asymmetric design maintains both scene consistency and the changes introduced across successive turns. Rollout length. We train on rollouts of 10 edits, the same length as the evaluated sequences. A student trained on 5- edit rollouts still improves over the base model after ten turns (consistency 3.29 vs. 3.03), but recovers only about a third of our gain (3.85) and is significantly worse than our model on every preservation measure. Training on its own outputs over the full length of the sequences it edits at in- ference is therefore important. 6. Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work We introduced a training approach for stable multi-turn im- age editing. Our central idea is that stability should be learned over the entire recursive process, rather than at in- dividual editing steps. We achieve this through asymmet- ric conditioning: the student learns from its own generated editing trajectories, while the teacher remains anchored to the original clean image and the accumulated editing in- structions. This allows us to supervise a long, imperfect sequence of edits through a shorter, clean alternative path, without exposing the teacher to the errors accumulated by the student.
