Every wireless link is already an unintentional measurement of its surroundings. A signal that leaves a base station and arrives at a receiver has encountered several components in an environment: blocked by one building, reflected off another, diffracted around a corner. The environment is, in effect, written into the wireless channel measured at the receiver. If that writing could be read back, the radio infrastructure already deployed at scale could be repurposed as a sensor network, mapping the world through occluded regions, in darkness, and in severe weather, where cameras and lidar degrade or fail outright (Zhao et al., 2018; Dodds et al., 2026). It would also keep maps current when they change fastest: after an earthquake or a flood, the layout of a neighborhood can change in minutes, and the network is often the only sensor still running. Site-specific digital twins, which today require expensive surveys or hand-built geometry, could also be constructed and kept current from the traffic a network already carries (Hoydis et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Testolina et al., 2024). The knowledge of the propagation environment can also be used to optimize communication network throughput (Zhao et al., 2023; Demirhan & Alkhateeb, 2023), and could advance integrated communication and sensing, one of the main pillars of next-generation networks (Akyildiz et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024). Recovering geometry from wave measurements is well studied in acoustics, where room shape is inferred from resolvable echoes and geometric propagation models (Dokmani´c et al., 2013; El Baba et al., 2018); recent learning-based approaches relax some of these assumptions and predict floor- plans directly (Yeon & Choi, 2024; Yeon et al., 2024), but still rely on echo structures that can be attributed to individual surfaces. In the RF domain, scene reconstruction has primarily relied on ded- icated radar sensing systems (Casmin & Oliveira, 2025; Borts et al., 2026), recovering occupancy grids or floor plans from millimeter-wave and ultra-wideband reflections using geometric inference, motion-synthesized aperture, structural priors, or learned generative models (Lu et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2026).
