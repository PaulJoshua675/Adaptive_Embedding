Modern generative models are expensive to train (Strubell et al., 2019) and expensive to sam- ple (Luccioni et al., 2024): diffusion, flow matching and related methods typically produce one image by integrating a velocity field over hundreds of sequential steps, each a full pass through a large network. Sequential work of this kind suits a GPU poorly and a physical system well. A substrate that relaxes to the minimum of an energy performs in one continuous settling what a GPU performs as hundreds of discrete evaluations, and analog neuromorphic devices promise large gains in speed and energy on that basis. Two things stand in the way: finding a generative algorithm whose computation is a relaxation, and removing the backpropagation that training relies on. Energy Matching (Balcerak et al., 2025) is one such algorithm. It generates by gradient descent on a potential Vθ(x), the scalar output of a feedforward network, whose minima are where the data lies. The potential does not change with time, so every step descends the same fixed landscape, which is what a physical system does when it relaxes. But the velocity it follows is −∇xVθ, and in its original formulation obtaining that gradient is a backward pass — once per integration step during sampling, on top of the usual ∇θL backward pass per training step. The remaining obstacle is therefore backpropagation, whose global gradient transport is difficult to realise in analog.
