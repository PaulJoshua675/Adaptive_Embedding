Following prior work, the performance of each split is computed as the macro-average across the tasks belonging to that split, and we additionally report the macro-average performance across the Backward and Real-Time splits in Table 1. The Backward splits of OVO-Bench mostly focus on retrospect to the past and comprise three tasks: Episodic Memory (EPM), Action Sequence Identification (ASI), and Hallucination Detection (HLD). Among them, EPM is particularly well suited for evaluating the memory mechanism introduced by MaLoW, as it directly measures the model’s ability to retain and retrieve information from earlier observations. We further note that HLD is relatively less challenging because all ground-truth responses are Unable to answer. As a result, models can achieve high accuracy on this task by consistently predicting this response, making HLD less discriminative for assessing the effectiveness of long-term memory mechanisms. The trained MaLoW can operate in a fully streaming manner by processing the video history block by block using the cached-forward mode of DeltaNet. (Please see the Evaluation details. in the section C.2) To accelerate evaluation, however, we use the same parallel-forward mode employed during training when evaluating on OVO-Bench and StreamingBench. Specifically, for a question associated with a video prefix [0, T], we treat the interval [0, T −2] as historical context and sample it at 1 fps as input to the hypernetwork. The base VLM then receives the question together with two frames sampled from the current observation window [T −2, T]. For the evaluation on the OVO-bench, we follow the prompt example used in lmms-eval (Zhang et al., 2025a), and for the evaluation on the StreamingBench we follow the prompt example used in the official repository. Baselines Re-Evaluation. Among the baselines in Table 1, we re-evaluate MOSS-VL-Realtime-11B (Wang et al., 2026), VideoChat3-4B (Li et al., 2026), and Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026), as these models are also used as the base VLMs for MaLoW. For the remaining baselines, we report the results from their original papers. For Qwen3.5-9B, we sample video frames at 1 fps with a maximum per-frame pixel budget of 262,144 and use greedy decoding for answer generation. For VideoChat3-4B, we use the official evaluation code and follow the hyperparameters reported in the paper. On OVO-Bench, we sample frames at 2 fps and retain the 32 most recent frames, with a per-frame pixel budget of 4482 and a visual-token budget of 80K. On StreamingBench, we sample frames at 4 fps with at most 4,096 frames, using the same per-frame pixel budget of 4482 and visual-token budget of 80K. For MOSS-VL-Realtime-11B, as neither the paper nor the released repository provides an official evaluation script, we evaluate the model on OVO-Bench and StreamingBench using frames sampled at 1 fps with a maximum per-frame pixel budget of 262,144. These settings successfully reproduce the reported OVO-Bench performance of VideoChat3 and MOSS- VL-Realtime. However, our re-evaluated StreamingBench results are lower than those reported in the corresponding papers. We therefore include both the re-evaluated and paper-reported results in Table 1, with the paper-reported results shown in gray. The Q3.5-2B-MLP is given the current observation (an image) and text instruction and predicts a chunk of four seven-dimensional actions, corresponding to the current action and the following three actions. We learn special tokens as action hidden representations, and VLMs compress the visual observation and the text instruction into the special tokens.
