Theoretical Analysis Section 3 introduced the distance-based uncertainty score in Eq. (3), and Section 4 showed that escalating on that score outperforms random escalation at matched rates. In this section, we now explain the empirical success of our uncertainty-based escalation through a simple probabilistic analysis. To keep the analysis simple, we consider a single decision per task: each task is either handed to the strong agent before execution begins (𝑡esc = 0) or never handed off (𝑡esc = 𝑇). This isolates the initial gate 𝑔0 of LEASH (Section 3.3), with no runtime escalation afterward. We also assume the budget in Eq. (1) is used exactly, so a fixed fraction 𝑝of tasks is escalated, which is what our quantile thresholds target. Let 𝑧be the standardized query representation, 𝑌∈{Pass, Fail} the outcome if the weak agent completes the task without escalation, and 𝑞w(𝑧) = Pr(𝑌= Pass | 𝑧) and 𝑞s(𝑧) the success probabilities when the weak agent and the strong agent, respectively, completes the task. We analyze 𝑢(𝑧), the population version of our initial score 𝑢𝑞in Eq. (3), computed with the true class means and covariance instead of their estimates. Proposition 1 (Optimality of LEASH’s initial gate at a fixed escalation rate). Suppose 𝑧| 𝑌= 𝑦∼ N (𝜇𝑦, Σ) with shared covariance Σ ≻0 and distinct means, and both outcomes occur with positive probability. Let the initial gate escalate a task when 𝑢(𝑧) exceeds the threshold that escalates a fraction 𝑝 of tasks. Among all escalation rules based on 𝑧with the same rate 𝑝: (i) the gate hands off the largest fraction of the tasks the weak agent would fail, i.e., it maximizes Pr(escalated | 𝑌= Fail); (ii) if the escalation gain Δ(𝑧) = 𝑞s(𝑧) −𝑞w(𝑧) is a nondecreasing function of 𝑢(𝑧), and when 𝑞s is constant, the gate also maximizes task success rate. 11
