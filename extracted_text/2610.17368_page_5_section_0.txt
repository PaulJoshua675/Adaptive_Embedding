Improving Tail-Item Representations with Attribute Super- vision. While hierarchical supervision addresses the structure of semantic IDs, it does not explicitly ensure that the learned repre- sentations preserve individual item attributes. This is particularly important for generative recommendation, where users may ex- press preferences for specific attributes such as color, material, fit, or pattern. Semantic IDs are derived from holistic item representations and therefore organize products according to overall semantic sim- ilarity rather than any single attribute. Consequently, an attribute relevant to a user’s query may be distributed across multiple levels of the semantic-ID hierarchy and need not be explicitly preserved by the standard next-token prediction objective. (c) Attribute supervision mainly re- duces color prediction error for torso and tail items with sparse interaction data. Color provides a concrete example of this limitation. In our cata- log, each item is associated with a CIE-LAB color representation, allowing us to quantify perceptual similarity between items. As shown in Figure 3, color information is distributed across the SID hierarchy: the L0 code alone achieves only 37% mean color purity, whereas conditioning on the (𝐿0, 𝐿1) prefix increases color purity to 83%. This indicates that fine-grained color information emerges progressively across the hierarchy rather than being localized to a single SID level. Moreover, a probe analysis shows that color information is present in the model’s hidden representations but is not readily accessible through a simple linear mapping. While the linear probe performs worse than the catalog-mean baseline at all SID positions, a lightweight MLP substantially improves color prediction, achieving a Δ𝐸of 38.5 at L2 compared with the base- line of 56.3. This suggests that attribute information exists in the representation but is not explicitly organized for direct attribute prediction. The limitation is particularly important for long-tail items, which receive substantially less interaction supervision. In our catalog, 15.1% of items occur at most five times in the training data, while 23.8% never appear in a color-specific conversation, leaving them with little or no direct color supervision. As a result, a model may learn to generate plausible semantic IDs while failing to preserve fine-grained attributes needed to dis- tinguish similar items. These observations motivate an additional source of attribute- specific supervision. Rather than relying solely on semantic-ID prediction to implicitly encode every item property, we explicitly en- courage the model’s hidden representations to preserve fine-grained attribute information. Using color as a concrete instantiation, we apply auxiliary supervision to the item’s catalog-derived CIE-LAB representation. As shown in Figure 3, this supervision primarily benefits data-sparse items: the mean top-1 color error decreases from 54.5 to 50.2 for tail colors and from 33.5 to 29.0 for torso colors, while head colors show essentially no improvement (12.5 to 12.6). This suggests that attribute supervision provides complementary information precisely where interaction-based supervision is weak- est. Importantly, the formulation is not specific to color and can be instantiated with other item attributes, such as material, fit, or pattern. 5
