The amortization cache (Section 3.5) functions as a persistent test corpus analogous to a regression test suite, where successful attacks are stored, indexed by category, and replayed against subsequent system versions to detect regressions in safety alignment. The same discovered-attack populations can serve as training data for defensive tooling such as JailGuard [43], which requires diverse, annotated attack examples. Our release (Appendix A) publishes the framework source code, strategy library, seed prompts, judge configurations, and ablation experiment scripts and results, enabling practitioners to reproduce the optimization pipeline and generate their own attack corpora for their specific target models. Unlike methods requiring per-target template engineering, REPRO can be operated by engineers without specialized adversarial-ML expertise. 5.4 Threats to Validity We identify potential threats to the validity of our findings across four standard categories. Because the 100% ASR values reported throughout Section 4 are the paper’s central claim,we discuss the judge dimension first as it is the most commonly raised concern in red-teaming evaluations. 5.4.1 Internal Validity. All ASR numbers in this paper are produced by the Qwen3 judge model, which classifies each target-model response as compliant or refusing against an explicit rubric. Whether the cases Qwen3 classifies as compliant would also be classified as compliant by other judges (the HarmBench evaluation prompt, the Llama-3-70B judge used in JailbreakBench, GPT- 4 as a judge, or human annotators) is a question we partially address through cross-validation (Section 4.1.4) and benchmark-native re-evaluation. On JailbreakBench, Adaptive RS [4] drops from 100% ASR on AdvBench to 78–93% on JBB-Behaviors when evaluated under the JBB-native Llama-3-70B judge; REPRO remains at 100% under both its own Qwen3 judge and the JBB evaluation prompt. This difference reflects a combination of stronger optimization and judge-calibration effects that we cannot fully separate. On HarmBench, white-box attacks with gradient access report 34.5% (GCG) and 0.5% (AutoDAN), while REPRO reports 100%. This gap is partly expected: GCG and AutoDAN produce token-level adversarial suffixes optimized for logit-space effects, while REPRO produces semantically coherent prompts that a semantics-aware judge is more likely to classify as harmful compliance. The remain- der is attributable to judge calibration and potential differences in target-model configuration. The headline numbers should be read as judge-specific. The more consequential form of this threat is not judge disagreement in the abstract but the position of the judge relative to the search. Qwen3-8B both scores candidates during optimization and produces the reported ASR, and the loop halts when it accepts a candidate. A response the judge wrongly accepts is therefore recorded as a solved behavior and never re-examined, so judge error is absorbed into the result rather than showing up as variance around it. The asymmetry we observe in Section 4.1.4, in which Qwen3-8B refuses where the alternative judges accept in the large majority of disagreements, argues against systematic leniency and is the strongest evidence we can offer that this has not happened at scale. It is not conclusive: strictness measured on a stratified sample of archived replies does not preclude a subset of accepted attacks being false positives, and the sample covers five of the 36 targets. We report the result as obtained and mark the false-discovery rate against a search-independent gold arbiter as the open quantity. Partial mitigation through rubric re-scoring, and its limits. We re-scored REPRO’s successful attacks under each benchmark’s published evaluation rubric: the JailbreakBench evaluation prompt for JBB results and the HarmBench evaluation prompt for HarmBench results. Both reproduced the 100% ASR obtained with the Qwen3 judge. Two limits keep this from being a full mitigation.
