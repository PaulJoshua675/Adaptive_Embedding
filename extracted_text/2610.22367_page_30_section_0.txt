The reverse walk then undoes exactly t active steps, and the frozen flag marks which steps are active. The circuit attaches one modular halving of r to each active reverse step: r 7→r/2 if r is even and r 7→(r + p)/2 otherwise. After the reverse walk, r = aw−1, the input w is restored, and no record of t remains. Step-dependent qubit reuse. A naïve layout would give each of the four signed integers f, g, fv, gv its own permanent 256-bit register, consuming 1,024 qubits before the counter, controls, or history stack are included. These registers do not all need their full capacity simultaneously: as the walk progresses, f and g need progressively fewer bits, while fv and gv need more. Each step halves g, and swaps alternate which value plays the role of g. Each step also doubles a coefficient. The packed Euclidean construction of Luo et al. [40] and its specialization in SI II.2 use the same fact: a shrinking remainder and growing coefficient occupy opposite ends of one register, as visualized in Figure S2(a). The discovered divstep circuit applies this idea to f, g, fv, gv using two 256-qubit registers rather than four: g and fv share the register that initially holds w, and f and gv share the other. Within each register, the growing coefficient claims positions vacated by its shrinking partner. The width and position of every value at every step are fixed when the circuit is built. A walk in which a value outgrows its assigned width fails; this event is the integer-size limit in Table S4. Fused coordinate arithmetic. For the remaining coordinate arithmetic, the circuit does not call general multipli- cation, squaring, or reduction circuits. It adds each term of the coordinate formulas straight into the register that needs it. For the square, the coordinate update needs λ2 only while forming Rx. Splitting λ = λ0 + 2128λ1 gives λ2 = λ2 0 + 2129λ0λ1 + 2256λ2 1 (mod p). (58) This expresses the 256-bit square as three products involving only 128-bit halves. The circuit adds them one at a time directly into the coordinate register and clears each temporary product before computing the next. It therefore avoids both a register for the completed square and the second full multiplication of the usual approach, which computes the square, copies it, and uncomputes it. The remaining coordinate products exploit the special form of the prime p. Since 2256 ≡232 + 977 (mod p), (59) the circuit can replace any bits above bit 255 with a small correction to the low bits instead of performing a general modular reduction. The circuit applies this correction while it forms each product, so it never holds a complete product that it must later clear. We require the exact map in Equation (54), including its phase and the return of every ancilla to zero, except on at most a 2 × 10−4 fraction of inputs [55]. The 16-bit-window algorithm of Babbush et al. [8] invokes the point adder 28 times; by a union bound, the failure budget adds less than 0.6% retry overhead. The four divstep walks use 2.5 million Toffolis, 63% of the circuit. The field multiplications around the walks use 1.4 million, 35% of the circuit. The fused square and the coordinate additions use the remaining 0.10 million. The 935-qubit peak occurs during a walk. The algebraic identities behind the recurrence, branch decoders, fused square, and sparse-prime reductions are exact. Failures arise only from the finite stopping-time, integer-size, history, carry, and comparison limits. Reducing modulo the sparse prime adds a short constant, 232 + 977 or a small multiple of it, to the low end of a 256-bit register. In principle the carry from this addition can travel up the whole register. In practice it rarely travels far, because it keeps moving only while the bits above it are ones. The circuit therefore propagates each such carry through only the low 58 bits (60 in the fused square) and drops any carry that would go further. This happens with probability about 2−26 per reduction. The comparison limit uses the same idea: to decide whether a modular addition overflowed, the circuit compares only the top 27 bits of the two operands, which gives the wrong answer only when those bits all agree.
