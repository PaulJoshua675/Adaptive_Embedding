Even as increasingly detailed methods of tissue characterization move toward clinical application, morphology remains the foundation of microscopic pathology (1). Mastering its interpretation requires years of training, building an internal frame of reference against which unseen cases are compared (2). That frame of reference, however, is bounded by the cases an individual has encountered. Each rare entity is, by its nature, seldom seen, yet rare entities collectively make up a substantial part of the diagnostic spectrum. And while any individual pathologist encounters a given entity only occasionally, a department accumulates such cases over years. Once digitized as whole slide images (WSIs), these institutional archives can become a valuable external frame of reference, yet searching through these archives is challenging due to the diverse morphology and size of WSIs (3). Digitization has made histomorphology accessible to artificial intelligence (AI), which is advancing from research toward routine diagnostics (4). Neural networks can learn semantically meaningful representations of tissue directly from data, an approach known as deep learning (5, 6). Rather than learning a single task, foundation models (FMs) use self-supervised pre-training on large corpora of slides to learn general-purpose representations that can be used across downstream tasks (7, 8). While training classifiers is a common way to apply such representations, they require enough labelled cases per class to learn a decision boundary and return a label without morphological evidence behind it (9). Content-based retrieval avoids this by returning cases rather than labels (10-12). We built a search engine based on FM representations, letting pathologists query an institutional archive and explore whether current pathology FMs make this viable for rare entities without any task-specific training. We evaluate our approach on salivary gland tumors as a proof-of-concept, a rare and morphologically challenging entity with an incidence of 5.2 per 1,000,000 person-years in Germany (13) and the WHO recognizing 21 malignant types (14), in addition to secondary tumors (15). While some entities cannot be reliably separated by their morphology (16) and new molecular findings continue to carve out new entities (17), initial histological assessment can guide the selection of ancillary testing. An archive of 137 cases spanning 12 ICD-O morphology categories was embedded with four pathology FMs and retrieval was evaluated using queries selected by a pathologist and by a gated attention-based multiple instance learning (ABMIL) model. Methods Data We digitized 137 salivary gland tumor cases, totaling 974 hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) WSIs, for our test cohort. For training our ABMIL model, we digitized an additional 15 non-tumor cases, totaling 123 H&E WSIs. All scans were acquired at a native magnification of 40× with a resolution of 0.25 µm/pixel (microns per pixel, (MPP)) using Leica Aperio GT 450 DX scanners. ICD-O codes were sourced through the Zentrum für Krebsregisterdaten at the Robert Koch Institute and through our patient information system. Feature extraction Every WSI was screened for artifacts using GrandQC (18). Tissue was tiled into nonoverlapping 512×512-pixel patches at 20× magnification covering 256×256 µm of tissue (0.5 MPP) using PathoPatcher (19). The patch size was chosen to balance morphological detail against index size (20). Patches had to contain at least 50% artifact-free tissue. No stain normalization was applied.
