These trajectories motivate task-dependent iteration budgets, with additional rounds remaining useful where early gains are limited. Candidate coverage and answer selection impose different limits. At N = 9, EMO’s five-candidate oracle coverage exceeds that of nine independent samples in seven of nine settings (Appendix Table 13). Figure 2a illustrates the largest coverage advantage, on Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D. EMO already reaches 35.5 accuracy at K = 1 (N = 3), exceeding BoN with ten samples (33.0). Its dashed oracle curve separates further from independent sampling, reaching 73.7 versus 60.3 at N = 9, despite using five rather than nine candidates. However, the growing gap between EMO’s dashed and solid curves exposes a selection bottleneck: from K = 1 to K = 4, coverage rises from 49.3 to 73.7, while selected accuracy rises only from 35.5 to 37.4. At N = 9, selected accuracy as a fraction of coverage is 50.7% for EMO versus 53.4% for BoN: the advantage comes from broader coverage despite a lower fraction realized by selection (Appendix B.4.4). Figure 2b shows a different pattern on Llama-3.2-3B/MATH-500: at N = 9, EMO exceeds BoN in both coverage (70.6 vs. 66.5) and selected accuracy (60.3 vs. 52.7). The selected-to-covered fraction is also higher (85.4% vs. 79.2%), so the accuracy gap accompanies gains in both candidate coverage and the fraction of covered cases selected correctly. At the other extreme, Llama-3.2-3B/AIME has oracle coverage of only 3.3 and 6.7 on the two editions (Appendix Table 8), so better selection alone would yield limited gains.
