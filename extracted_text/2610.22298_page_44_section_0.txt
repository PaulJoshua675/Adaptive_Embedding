Clinical Syndromes and Involved Mechanisms The principal syndrome is a unilateral, persistent, enlarging breast mass in a prepubertal male, confirmed by the 18-month history with recent enlargement, the palpable 2 cm by 2 cm firm mobile right retroareolar mass in March 2016, and the sonographic finding of a well-circumscribed, hypoechoic, hypovascular nodule without malignant features (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMcpc1503831). The mass is realized through localized proliferation or hormonal stimulation of mammary tissue in the retroareolar region, superficial to the pectoralis muscle. The pathogenetic mechanism that most plausibly underlies this syndrome is localized, asymmetric proliferation of ductal and periductal fibro-glandular tissue in the right breast, consistent with idiopathic prepubertal or early-pubertal gynecomastia; in this child, the mass has progressed beyond the typical small, transient 'breast bud,' which is why surgical sampling is being considered. A lower-probability mechanism is a true neoplasm of the prepubertal male breast—most commonly a fibroepithelial lesion analogous to a juvenile fibroadenoma, and very rarely a ductal carcinoma or other malignancy—in which the same fibro-glandular proliferation is clonal/expansile rather than hormonally driven. This is considered because the lesion is unilateral, >2 cm, persistent for 18 months, and continues to enlarge, features that exceed the usual natural history of simple pubertal gynecomastia. A concurrent syndrome of premature adrenarche is suggested by fine pubic and axillary hair in an 8-year-old with prepubertal 3 mL testes, prepubertal phallus, and bone age of 8 years; this is realized through early adrenal androgen secretion independent of gonadal activation. This adrenarche is significant because it does not explain the unilateral breast mass—systemic estrogen excess would typically produce bilateral breast enlargement and testicular or phallic changes, which are absent—thereby supporting a localized rather than systemic endocrine mechanism. The absence of central puberty is confirmed by prepubertal genitalia and normal bone age, rejecting a hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis-driven process as the cause of the mass. A hormonally active endocrine tumor (for example, an hCG- or estrogen-secreting gonadal or extragonadal germ-cell tumor) producing secondary gynecomastia is explicitly considered and rejected on the basis of prepubertal testicular volume (3 ml) and prepubertal phallus, normal stature and BMI, bone age equal to chronologic age, and absence of any reported features of precocious puberty beyond scant pubic/axillary hair.
