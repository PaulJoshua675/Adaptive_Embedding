Semantic Segmentation. The performance and goal-achievement capability of autonomous or Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) systems, which have become a preferred choice for navigating dangerous environments in recent years, rely strongly on scene understanding. High-level Computer Vision (CV) tasks such as pixel-level semantic segmentation [1, 2] or instance-level object detection [3] have emerged as powerful tools for mapping sensor observations into information and cues that guide decision-making. The main challenge remains robust perception under varying conditions [4, 5]. Semantic segmentation achieves scene understanding by assigning each pixel in an image to a specific class, with classes defined during training. In this way, the entire scene is parsed, and objects can be more easily identified by their shape or distinct attributes. Due to its highly reliable approach, the applications of semantic segmentation extend beyond outdoor scene parsing to do- mains such as medicine [6], indoor environments [7], and even more challenging missions such as survivor localisation [8] in hazy or poorly lit conditions. Usually, high-level CV tasks perform best under near-ideal conditions: consistent illumination that ensures accurate colour balance, without haze, smoke, noise, or other degradations. However, real- world environments rarely provide such conditions, therefore, robust systems must be capable of handling non-ideal sensor inputs [9, 10]. In this study, the focus is on improving low-light semantic segmentation and understanding the steps required to recover lost detail, as well as how to compen- sate for features that cannot be restored [11]. Low-Light Conditions. Light-deficient environments occur as frequently as daylight scenes, mak- ing them not merely an edge case of CV systems but a domain that requires rigorous treatment [12]. What makes low-light images particularly challenging is that limited illumination introduces additional issues beyond the obvious reduced exposure, such as global and local structure loss, noise, artifacts, and prominent colour distortions [13, 14]. There are multiple approaches to im- proving semantic segmentation performance under such conditions, with the main categories being: enhancement-based methods [15] (where a network is applied prior to segmentation to enhance the images), knowledge distillation [16] (where a student network trained on low-light images is con- strained to match the internal representations of a teacher network trained on well-lit data), single- network approaches (where the feature space is analyzed using frequency-domain techniques [17] with a greater emphasis being placed on edge information), and multi-modal fusion approaches [18, 19], in which the limited information in low-light images is compensated by incorporating addi- tional representations.
