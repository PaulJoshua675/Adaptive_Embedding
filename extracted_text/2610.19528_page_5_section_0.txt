Importantly, this optimizes quality under the current translator and policy; it does not assume that semantic similarity is itself the target. 3.3.2 Separate Contrastive Alignment Joint optimization using Eq.(6) may suffer from high variance in credit assignment to the retriever: a single terminal reward must be attributed across many token-level decisions but only a handful of document selections, diluting the gradient signal that reaches the retriever parameters. We therefore introduce a complementary contrastive approach that provides direct document-level supervision. We first train πθ with a fixed retriever. For each query q produced by the trained policy on the training data, we retrieve a candidate set Cq = {d1, . . . , dM} and run controlled translations using one candidate at a time. We score the resulting translations with the same quality function Q and choose the document producing the best translation as d+ = arg maxdi∈Cq Q(ˆyi, y⋆, x). We then fine-tune the retriever using: Lret(ϕ) = −log exp(fϕ(q, d+)/Tc) P di∈Cq exp(fϕ(q, di)/Tc). (7) Unlike conventional contrastive retrieval, the pos- itive document is defined by its measured effect on translation rather than annotated relevance or embedding similarity. These two strategies offer complementary trade- offs. Joint optimization updates the translator and retriever in an end-to-end way, while contrastive alignment requires additional candidate evaluations but provides more direct retriever supervision. We compare and discuss them in Section 4.3. 3.4 Inference At inference time, no reference translation or qual- ity evaluator is required. The learned policy de- cides whether retrieval is necessary, generates a query when needed and incorporates the returned evidence, and produces the final translation. We set a maximum number of retrieval calls and an output-token budget to bound worst-case latency. We report both translation quality and retrieval rate to measure the effectiveness and policy behaviors. 4 Experiments We evaluate SRAT on idiom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation with either offline and online retrieval. We conduct experiment to study three research questions: RQ1. Does a learned selective-retrieval policy outperform prior translation methods and matched policies that never or always retrieve? RQ2. Does aligning the re- triever with downstream translation utility improve translation quality, and when is it most beneficial? RQ3.
