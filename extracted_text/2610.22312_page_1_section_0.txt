Grid-Bench: Can LLMs Predict How Discrete Worlds Change? Zixiang Xu1, Zirui Song2, Wanrong Zheng1, Chiyu Zhang3, Yue Huang4, Yuyang Peng5, Kexin Zhang1, Jinkun Chen1, Xiuying Chen2, Xiangliang Zhang4, Yan Liu1, Laurent Itti1, Frank Shyu3, Xiaoqiang Luo3, Sandeep Pandey3, Xi Liu3, Luke Simon3, Xiyang Hu6, Yue Zhao1 1University of Southern California 2Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence 3Meta 4University of Notre Dame 5University of California, Los Angeles 6Arizona State University Spatial instructions describe how to move objects, transform regions, or modify their surroundings. When several operations are applied in sequence, each acts on a configuration shaped by earlier changes. How accurately can large language models (LLMs) predict the resulting spatial state? We introduce Grid-Bench, an executable benchmark in which eight families of operations transform discrete worlds represented as two-, three-, and four-dimensional grids. Given an initial grid, explicit rules, and an event history expressed as text, a model predicts the complete final grid. Reference execution makes every predicted value checkable, supporting comparisons across spatial operations, history lengths, and workloads. Evaluating six LLMs shows that complete-state accuracy falls sharply as histories grow, with substantial divergence among models that perform similarly on single events. Models with similar exact-match accuracy can nevertheless differ substantially in the number of incorrect cells on shared instances. Even over long histories, some models recover many correct changes beyond what copying the initial state would yield. Grid-Bench makes both complete recovery and these differences in returned states directly measurable across a range of spatial conditions.
