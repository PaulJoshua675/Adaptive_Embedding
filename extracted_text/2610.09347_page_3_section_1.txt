Machine learning has entered genetic programming in two quite different places, and the distinction matters for what follows. In the first, learning is put into the search: the evolved expression remains the answer, and a learned component decides where to look for it. Deep symbolic regression trains a generator of expressions by risk-seeking policy gradients [4]; neural-guided seeding initialises a GP population from a trained model [22]; AI Feynman uses a neural network to detect symmetry and separability and to recurse on subproblems [3]; and graph networks trained on a system are distilled into closed-form expressions after the fact [5]. A large benchmarking effort now places these alongside standard machine learning methods [6]. In all of them the arrangement Koza defined is preserved, because fitness still measures what the expression itself computes. The present paper is not about this family. The distinction matters because the phrase “genetic programming combined with machine learning” now most often refers to it. In the second place, learning is put into the model: as in Section 1, the evolved program gives up being the answer, becoming instead an input to a learner that is refitted for every candidate, with fitness measuring the learner’s output. In classification this is the standard framing of feature construction, from Krawiec’s formulation of it as changing the representation of the input data for a machine learner [10], through feature generation for fault diagnosis [7] and multiple-feature construction on high-dimensional data [8], to multidimensional transformations consumed by a distance based classifier [9, 11]. In regression the same shift appears as multi-gene GP [12], multiple regression GP [13], evolutionary feature synthesis [14], and systems that learn feature spaces or networks of trees using the downstream model’s weights as feedback [15, 16, 23], the same systems already sketched in Section 1. Icke and Bongard combined GP based symbolic regression with deterministic learners with the same motivation [24]. Two features of that second family are relevant here. The first is what it holds fixed. Whatever the domain, the learner is refitted per candidate and never inherited, and the evolved program is scored by proxy, so in every one of these systems the program is a representation, not a solution, though none of them says so. The second is what it varies, and here the variation is narrower than it first appears: the overwhelming majority attach a linear downstream model, whether a least squares combination of genes [12, 13], a linear map over evolved features [14], or a linear or distance based decision rule [9, 11]. By Proposition 2 below, such a stage supplies no order of nonlinearity at all, so in all of this work the evolved features must between them carry the target’s full nonlinear structure. This literature has not overlooked the effect we report; it has been structurally unable to observe it, because it has almost never varied the one component that would reveal it. A third strand attacks the same difficulty from inside the evolutionary loop, not after it. Linear scaling fits a least squares affine map to each individual’s output so that evolution is scored on shape rather than scale [25], and is best read as the degenerate member of the second family above: one evolved feature, one affine learner. Semantic methods instead change the variation operators so that behaviour moves geometrically in semantic space [26, 27], at the cost of rapid code growth [28]. These are not merely empirical proposals; geometric semantic operators come with a conical fitness landscape argument, and semantic backpropagation and library based methods have a developed theory. Our results touch this strand at one point only: stating what an affine post-hoc map cannot contribute locates linear scaling exactly, instead of treating it as merely a weak learner. The measure this paper builds on also comes from within genetic programming. Vladislavleva et al. proposed the minimal degree of a Chebyshev approximation as a complexity measure for evolved models, and used it as a selective pressure inside a Pareto GP to keep solutions simple [18]. Their measure applies to the model and acts as a constraint on it. We use the same notion twice over in the opposite direction: as a property of the target, predicting which downstream learner is required, and as a measurement made on the program a run produced, not a pressure imposed on it. Neither use appears in that literature, and the second is what supplies the evidence in Section 4.2. The experimental instrument likewise has a long history in a different role. Boolean function synthesis is among the oldest GP benchmarks and the even parity family is its canonical hard case [1, 29]. Its role has been diagnostic: because parity admits no useful partial solutions, it reveals whether a proposed mechanism supplies structural search or merely smooths a numerical landscape, which is why it appears as the first hard case in the work introducing geometric semantic GP [26]. In every such use it is a single hard instance, chosen for being hard.
