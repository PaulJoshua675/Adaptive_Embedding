Similarly, labeling a 7-point scale −3 to +3 instead of 1 to 7 shifted re- sponses toward the positive end (Tourangeau et al., 2007). In short, scale sign can influence both the distribution of ratings and the meaning respondents attach to scale points. Rating scale design in LLM prompts. It re- mains unclear whether rating scale design influ- ences LLM-generated scores as it does human rat- ings. Thus, this paper systematically investigates how consistent LLM-generated scores are across rating scale designs. Because LLM outputs are sensitive to changes in prompt formatting, order- ing, and instructions (Sclar et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2025; Zhuo et al., 2024), we hold the prompt fixed within each prompting strat- egy and change only the rating scale it specifies so that differences in scores across rating scale condi- tions can be attributed to scale design. We quantify these differences as numerical and semantic sen- sitivity. In this context, we ask: (RQ1) To what extent do changes in rating scale format, sign, and range produce (a) numerical and (b) semantic scale sensitivity in LLM-generated outputs? 2.2 Variances across Task, Prompting Strategy, and Model We further examine whether three factors moderate scale sensitivity, namely the task, the prompting strategy, and the model. Task. We measure political leaning, sentiment, and toxicity, three constructs widely explored in computational social science (Le Mens and Gal- lego, 2025; Robertson et al., 2023; Rathje et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2024). Political leaning, such as positioning actors or texts along a left-right spec- trum, is a social construct, grounded in political parties, agendas, elites, and symbolic meanings that are socially agreed upon (Bergan, 2012; Boudreau and MacKenzie, 2014). Sentiment is a psycholog- ical construct that reflects individuals’ affective states such as positivity or negativity (Evans, 2002). Toxicity is a socio-psychological construct whose judgment depends on both social norms and ex- pressions of anger or hostility (Sheth et al., 2022; Pandita et al., 2024). We note that political lean- ing and sentiment are bipolar constructs that range between two opposing poles (left vs. right and neg- ative vs. positive), whereas toxicity is a unipolar construct that ranges from the absence of toxic- ity to its strong presence. Given these conceptual differences across tasks, we ask: (RQ2) To what extent does the task moderate (a) numerical and (b) semantic scale sensitivity? Prompting strategy. The prompting strategy may also affect how stable LLM-generated scores are across rating scales. We compare two strategies that do not introduce additional confounding fac- tors and differ in how much guidance they provide: zero-shot and rubric-based prompting strategies. Zero-shot prompting is commonly used in LLM- based text scoring (Gilardi et al., 2023; Törnberg, 2023; Le Mens and Gallego, 2025) and requests a direct rating without explicit evaluation criteria. Rubric-based prompting, modeled on educational assessment, provides detailed coding guidelines for predefined dimensions of a construct. It asks the model to score each dimension and use these assessments to determine a final rating. Rubrics have been shown to improve consistency in LLM- based scoring (Hashemi et al., 2024; Eltanbouly et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026), but whether they also stabilize scores across rating scales is unknown. If scale sensitivity stems from underspecified evaluation criteria, explicit rubrics should reduce it. If it stems from how models map judgments onto numerical scales, it should per- sist regardless of prompting strategy. We thus ask: (RQ3) To what extent does the prompting strategy (zero-shot vs. rubric-based) moderate (a) numerical and (b) semantic scale sensitivity? Model. Model selection has become a consequen- tial methodological choice in computational so- cial science. Models differ in training data, opti- mization objectives, and alignment procedures, and most offer limited transparency into how they rep- resent semantic judgments internally (Kristensen- McLachlan et al., 2025). Considering these dif- ferences, we ask: (RQ4) To what extent does the choice of LLM moderate (a) numerical and (b) se- mantic scale sensitivity? Additionally, we test which measurement design choices most strongly affect the sensitivity of LLM- generated scores by asking: (RQ5) How much of the variation in (a) numerical and (b) semantic scale sensitivity is explained by scale design, task, prompting strategy, and model? 3
