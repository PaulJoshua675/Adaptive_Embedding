A lower error rate indicates that the extracted embeddings contain more language-discriminative information. 4) Speech emotion recognition: We evaluate the emotion branch on speech emotion recognition datasets. For each emotion dataset, we extract utterance-level emotion embeddings from the train, validation, and test partitions using three representations: the frozen emotion teacher, the single-task emotion student, and the emotion branch of the multi-teacher student. For each dataset and each representation type, we train a separate emotion classifier on the training partition of that dataset. The validation partition is used for model selection, and the final performance is reported on the test partition. This dataset- specific evaluation protocol avoids mixing incompatible emotion taxonomies across corpora and allows each classifier to match the number of emotion classes defined for the corresponding dataset. The embedding extractor remains frozen during classifier training. The classifier is therefore used only as a probing model to assess how much emotion-discriminative information is contained in the extracted representations. Performance is reported using unweighted accuracy (UA), which gives equal importance to each emotion class and is therefore suitable for evaluating emotion recognition performance under class imbalance. F. Experimental results In the table of results, we use the following notation. SENSE denotes the semantic teacher, ECAPA-spk denotes the speaker teacher, ECAPA-lang denotes the language teacher, and emotion2vec- base denotes the emotion teacher. SEM-only, SPK-only, LANG- only, and EMO-only refer to single-task students trained with the same architecture as the proposed model but optimized with only one attribute-specific objective. Multi-Attribute denotes our proposed model trained jointly with semantic, speaker, language, and emotion supervision. 1) Semantic attribute: Tables II and III report Recall@1 scores for cross-lingual speech-to-speech retrieval on VoxPopuli and FLEURS corpora. Overall, the Multi-Attribute model preserves semantic re- trieval performance very well and often improves over the SEM- only student or the SENSE teacher. These results indicate that Multi- Attribute supervision does not degrade the semantic space; on the contrary, the additional objectives can act as a regularizer and improve semantic retrieval robustness. 2) Speaker verification: Table IV presents the speaker verification results on VoxCeleb1-O. The ECAPA-spk teacher and the SPK- only student obtain the same EER of 0.899%. The Multi-Attribute model obtains an EER of 0.915%, corresponding to a very small absolute increase of 0.016 points. The minDCF values are also close across the three systems. These results show that joint Multi-Attribute training introduces only a marginal cost for speaker verification, while allowing the same encoder to support several other utterance-level representations. 3) Language identification: Table V reports language identifica- tion performance. The ECAPA-lang teacher obtains the lowest error rate, with 6.58%. LANG-only remains close to the teacher, reaching 6.83%. The Multi-Attribute model obtains 7.02% with its language attribute representation, corresponding to an absolute increase of only 0.19 points compared with LANG-only and 0.44 points compared with the teacher. Interestingly, by combining the language attribute with the semantic attribute generated by our model, we outperform the results reached by the ECAPA-lang teacher, showing that such a multi-attribute speech encoder provides complementary information useful to process some tasks. 4) Speech emotion recognition: We finally evaluate the emotion branch on speech emotion recognition (SER). We compare three representations: the emotion2vec-base teacher, the EMO-only student, and the emotion branch of the Multi-Attribute model. Figure 2 reports Unweighted Accuracy (UA) for each dataset. The radar plot shows that in many datasets, Multi-Attribute matches or improves over both the EMO-only student and the emotion2vec-base teacher, indicating that the emotion branch benefits from the shared encoder rather than being systematically degraded by the additional objectives. Fig. 2. Unweighted Accuracy on speech emotion recognition datasets. The figure compares the emotion2vec-base teacher, the EMO-only student, and the emotion branch of the proposed Multi-Attribute model. Overall, the results across the four evaluation tasks show that the proposed multi-teacher student can learn a unified speech encoder that preserves multiple utterance-level attributes simultaneously. Com- pared with single-task students, the Multi-Attribute model achieves competitive performance on speaker verification, language identifi- cation, and speech emotion recognition, while improving semantic retrieval on VoxPopuli and remaining competitive on FLEURS. These findings support the central hypothesis of this work: semantic, speaker, language, and emotion representations can be distilled into a single shared speech encoder with only limited trade-offs across attributes.
