The difficulty is that the queries which dominate large-N accuracy have hardness xq ≲1/S and are invisible in the sample. We consider: (i) Count ˆxq = 1 S #{Si > s⋆} – unbiased but quantized at 1/S; it returns 0 for the invisible tail and so predicts those queries survive forever. This is exactly the Krichene–Rendle unbiased rank estimator specialized to the top rank, and it is over-optimistic. (ii) Tail extrapolation via extreme-value theory (EVT) (Scheirer et al., 2011; Bahri et al., 2023) – for these queries no sampled distractor reaches s⋆, so count- ing is blind to them. Extreme-value theory states that above a high threshold the far tail of es- sentially any continuous score distribution converges to a universal two-parameter shape, a gen- eralized Pareto. We fit that shape to the highest-scoring distractors we do observe and extend it out to s⋆, recovering a small nonzero hardness. The move is the same one used to esti- mate a never-observed “100-year flood” from the tail of recorded flood heights (full procedure in App. B). (iii) Survival-curve fit – fit the mechanism of Cor. 1, the log-uniform survival model S1(N) = wimm + wc [E1(Na) −E1(Nb)]/ ln(b/a) for the top-rank survival, to the measured survival curve S1(N≤S) and extrapolate. The estimate and its interval. The two families fail on the same object, the hardness mass below the sample floor 1/S, in opposite one-sided ways. Counting and curve fits assign that mass zero, so they can only over-predict, with positive error in every one of our five backbone cases. Tail ex- trapolation instead assigns the mass a fitted value and recovers it. Because mAP integrates marginal positives whose hardness lives precisely in that unobserved tail, we take the tail-aware (EVT) esti- mate ˆRtail as the point estimate. It is accurate across three benchmarks, two distractor pools, and five backbones. The count estimate ˆRcount zeroes the unresolved sub-1/S tail mass, which can only raise the predicted metric—a one-sided bias on that mass, not a deterministic per-sample guarantee; the tail estimate has no matching bias in the other direction, so we treat the gap B = ˆRcount −ˆRtail as an empirical diagnostic—computable from the sample and shrinking as S grows—not a coverage interval. Empirically the truth sits at the tail edge in every case we measure, but a misspecified tail could in principle err either way. Alongside it we report a query-bootstrap confidence interval that captures sampling noise. Judged against this interval, the estimate enters the metric’s own noise from a small sample (Sec. 5). An error analysis (App. C) shows the AP law is exact in the hard- nesses for the realized gallery, so the error is entirely the sampling term. That term concentrates for sample-resolved positives and is governed by extreme-value consistency for the unresolved tail. The law assumes the S distractors are drawn uniformly at random from the pool. This is the one assumption a practitioner can silently violate. A conve- nience sample, such as the first S files of a pool ordered by crawl date, cluster, or upload, is not uniform: correlated images concentrate the score tail and bias the hardness estimate. Hash-based or shuffled sampling is the actual guarantee that the drawn sample represents the omitted pool. The per-shard check—split the sample into feature shards and confirm per-shard accuracy is constant (Sec. 5)—is a diagnostic that catches drift within the drawn sample (visible as shard-to-shard varia- tion), not a proof of representativeness. 6
