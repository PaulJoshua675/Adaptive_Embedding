This paper addresses that gap as an exploratory empirical study: it combines a keyness scatter method that reveals the full vocabulary associated with each outcome with length-controlled cluster-level regressions, using established methods (keyness statistics, Word2Vec, HDBSCAN, conditional and count regression) whose combination is offered as a practical workflow rather than a new analytical framework. 3 Methods 3.1 Data collection Question posts, best/other answers, nested replies, and asker acknowledgements were collected from Yahoo! Chiebukuro via a Selenium-based scraper using the search key­ word “物流” (logistics), spanning April 2004 to July 2026. To avoid the platform’s cap on the number of retrievable search results, the search was partitioned into one-day posting-date windows, each paginated to exhaustion; question URLs were de-dupli­ cated before thread pages were fetched, and each thread page was parsed into its ques­ tion, answers, nested replies, and thanks comments. Posts deleted or inaccessible at crawl time are simply absent from the corpus, and questions still open at crawl time are retained but excluded from the best-answer analyses. The corpus comprises 40,871 ques­ tions and 78,641 answer-tier posts, tagged by structural role: best answer (n = 24,512), other answer (n = 36,314), reply (n = 7,307), and thanks (n = 10,508). Of the 40,871 ques­ tions, 24,767 (61%) received at least one answer-tier post. Temporally, the corpus is not front-loaded despite the 22-year span: yearly question volumes range from 80 (2004) to 3,907 (2011) and remain between roughly 1,500 and 2,400 per year from 2013 onward, with a median question year of 2016 and 36% of questions posted in 2020 or later (Appendix Figure A1). Long posts are split into sentence-bound­ ary chunks (fragments over 200 characters further split), translated, and reassembled — no content discarded. Translation quality is below a commercial system’s, but since the analysis operates on noun-level aggregate statistics rather than sentence meaning, this is expected to act mainly as noise rather than a directional bias, provided errors are uncor­ related with outcome. To verify whether the translations appropriately retained the nouns under analysis, the author—a native Japanese speaker—manually reviewed 50 examples (25 questions and 25 answers) selected evenly from the short, medium, and long length groups.
