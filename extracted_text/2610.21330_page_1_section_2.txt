Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to obtain election-related information, including candidate comparisons and voting advice [2, 18]. Unlike traditional search engines that rank and present webpages, generative engines such as ChatGPT and Gemini retrieve web sources and use their content to generate responses. Existing work on LLMs in political settings mainly follows two lines. One studies political bias and alignment in LLM responses across models and political contexts [5–7, 9, 15]. The other studies whether LLM-generated political messages can influence voters and which factors drive political persuasion [11, 16]. These studies focus on LLM outputs and their effects on users. Much less is known about how external web content shapes political information in generative engine responses. Generative engine optimization (GEO) rewrites web content to improve its visibility in generative engine responses [1, 25]. Ex- isting GEO research typically takes a fixed webpage pool as in- put and studies how rewriting changes page visibility in gener- ated responses [1, 8, 24, 25, 29, 30], providing a natural frame- work for studying this question. To investigate the potential risks of GEO in voting scenarios, which we call VoteGEO, it remains two sets of questions. First, which source channels do generative engines use, how accessible are they to content publishers, and how long do the same webpages remain visible? Second, how does webpage rewriting affect candidate or party exposure in gener- ated responses? Specifically, does specifying a target candidate or party in the rewrite matter, which strategies work across gen- erative engines and elections, and how do voter personas affect baseline exposure and rewriting gains? To answer these questions, we introduce VoteGEO-Bench, a benchmark built on the 2024 U.S. presidential election [7] and the 2026 Japanese House of Rep- resentatives election [19]. After preprocessing, VoteGEO-Bench contains 28,200 election-related query cells across days and engines and 17,251 webpages. We further incorporate 3 categories of voter personas, including demographic identities, partisan identities, and questionnaire-derived profiles constructed from pre-treatment re- sponses of 2,306 U.S. participants [16]. We first analyze which sources generative engines use and how long the same webpages remain visible. We find that many cited sources come from lower-cost channels, including candidate or party websites and open platforms such as Reddit.
