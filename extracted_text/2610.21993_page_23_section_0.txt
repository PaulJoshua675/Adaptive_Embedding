The CKA representation analysis (Section 4.7) corroborates the downstream saturation find- ings: encoder representations converge toward the fully pretrained configuration with increasing data, and this convergence broadly mirrors the performance plateau observed in Table 11. The non-trivial CKA baseline at random initialization reflects the strong spatial inductive bias of ConvNeXt on SAR data, an observation with implications for future architectural choices in SAR SSL. During development of SparK-SAR, several alternative configurations were explored but found to provide no consistent benefit. VICReg regularization (Bardes et al., 2022) increased representation dimensionality but de- graded downstream performance, consistent with a conflict between the generative SparK recon- struction objective and the decorrelation objective of VICReg. Fisher-Tippett-based SAR-specific reconstruction loss (Fisher and Tippett, 1928; Guan et al., 2019) and distillation from a cross-modal optical teacher (DINOv3-style (Siméoni et al., 2026)) produced reduced training losses but no downstream gains, suggesting that representations aligned with optical imagery are not directly transferable to SAR tasks. These negative results highlight the importance of domain-specific objective design for SAR SSL and are documented here to prevent redundant exploration by the community. A dual-branch polarimetric stem processing VV and VH channels separately before 1×1 projection fusion was also evaluated, but did not result in improvement on classification tasks and regressed on dense prediction (−1.44 pp on DFC2020-S1, −0.52 pp on Flood-S1), suggesting that joint polarimetric processing in a shared convolutional stem is preferable for S1GRD MIM pretraining. Flood-S1’s comparatively modest +1.4 pp gain has a natural explanation once we consider what the pretraining objective actually teaches the encoder. Change detection asks the model to reason jointly over a pre-event and a post-event acquisition, but MIM pretraining, single- image by construction, never exposes the encoder to any notion of temporal difference at all. An encoder that has only ever learned to reconstruct one image at a time has correspondingly little temporal structure to transfer. The Flood-S1 ablation (Table 10) shows that the loss functions and fusion strategies we tried at the task head change Flood-S1 mIoU by at most about 0.4 pp (Concatenation fusion with CE or focal loss), so the limitation looks like it may sit upstream, in the pretraining objective itself, which points toward multi-temporal pretraining as the more promising direction for future work rather than further task-head engineering. BigEarthNet-S1 is the one task where SparK-SAR (77.03±0.40) trails, coming in marginally behind both Copernicus-FM (77.9±0.0%) and SoftCon (78.7±0.0%) under frozen evaluation. We note that the Copernicus-Bench dataset wrappers pass geographic coordinates and acquisition timestamps as metadata to Copernicus-FM for BigEarthNet-S1, while DFC2020-S1 passes NaN metadata in lieu of absent coordinate information (Wang et al., 2025b). In contrast, ConvNeXt- RS receives no metadata whatsoever, relying solely on the two SAR amplitude channels (VV, VH) for all tasks. One plausible contributor is that BigEarthNet-S1’s labels come from the CORINE Land Cover map (Sumbul et al., 2019; European Environment Agency, 2020), and European land-cover classes are not spread uniformly: they cluster geographically. Combined with the real coordinates and acquisition times Copernicus-FM receives as input, that clustering gives it a location-based shortcut to lean on that our model, seeing only VV/VH amplitude, simply does not have access to.
