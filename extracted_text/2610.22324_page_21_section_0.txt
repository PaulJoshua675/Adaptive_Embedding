For this dataset, we report (i) the normalized entropy Hnorm = H(p)/ log2 |L|, where p is the pooled label distribution and |L| = 8 is the number of labels on the axis. It lies in [0, 1], and higher values mean ideas are spread more evenly across types. We also report (ii) the total variation distance (TVD) between the model’s label distribution and the human proposals’ distribution, and, (iii) Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) (Lin, 1991). For TVD and JSD, lower values mean the model’s spread of idea types is closer to the human one. Sampling vs Set Level Prompting Gains comes from training. To separate gains from training and set-level prompting, we compare each general model’s set of K = 3 ideas against n = 3 independently sampled single-idea responses (Table 12). Set-level prompting raises distance sharply (0.24–0.53 to 0.79–0.86) but lowers quality for the closed-source models (e.g. win rate 0.33 to 0.21 for Claude Opus 4.8), so utility barely changes (0.34 to 0.38). Our model beats both modes on both axes, with a win rate of 0.50 and a distance of 0.88, so the balance comes from the set-level reward. Table 12 reports the prompted sampled vs set level results. We see that prompting for a set mostly enables the models to increase their diversity/average distance, but this generally comes at a quality cost, indicating a quality-diversity trade off. For example, for Claude, the distance goes up from 0.24 to 0.80, but the win-rate drops from 0.33 to 0.21, even though the overall utility goes up (because of the big jump in distance). But this table is also to show that the major gains in the results for our proposed trained model, come from the reward training and not only the set-level prompting.
