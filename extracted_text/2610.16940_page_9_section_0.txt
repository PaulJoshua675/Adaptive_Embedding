The held-out results (Table 5) show a different pattern: with a shallow query tree (nq ≤512), dual-tree’s construction overhead is reduced but so is its pruning leverage. 5.3 Base Parameter Analysis Beygelzimer et al. [7] recommend base b=1.3 based on empirical evaluation. For RUSTKNN’s dual-tree all-NN (Table 6, Appendix A.4), our data do not support it as a default. Three patterns emerge. First, coarser bases win: the optimum is b=2.0—the value used in the original analysis—on four of the seven datasets in Table 6 at k=1 and five at k=10, and b=3.0 on the two smallest (glass and ionosphere, n ≤351), where shallower trees reduce the traversal overhead that dominates at small scale. Across all twelve datasets (Appendix A.4), b=1.3 is optimal in only one of 24 (dataset, k) cases. Second, the optimum rarely shifts with k: in Table 6 only mnist moves (1.3 at k=1, 2.0 at k=10), and there the optimum is shallow: base 2.0 is 10% slower than 1.3 at k=1 and within 1% of it at k=10. Third, the default’s cost is real where it matters: b=1.3 is 1.57× slower than the optimum on covtype at k=1 (1.51× at k=10) and 1.11–1.17× slower on corel and pendigits; it is within 2% of the optimum only on optdigits at k=1 and on mnist. We therefore recommend sweeping the base rather than adopting 1.3 unchecked: a sweep over b ∈{1.3, 2.0, 3.0} costs seconds on most datasets (roughly 10–15 minutes per base on mnist). We do not generalize this beyond dual-tree search; the per-implementation curves in Appendix A.4 show how the other implementations respond to the base. Full base sensitivity curves appear in Appendix A.4. 5.4 All-NN vs. Held-Out Divergence The all-nearest-neighbor benchmark, in which query and reference sets coincide, is the standard evaluation in KNN literature [7, 8]. This setting can enable some memory and algorithmic opti- mizations since the query and reference sets can use the same cover tree. However, most practical workloads involve held-out queries against a pre-built index. Table 5 (Appendix A.2) shows held-out performance diverges from all-NN rankings. Ranking divergence. The clearest case is mlpack’s two query modes. In the all-NN benchmark they are within about 2× of each other on every dataset, and dual-tree is the faster of the two on five of them (Table 3); held out, mlpack-Dual is 7.8× slower than mlpack-Single on pendigits and 250× slower on covtype at nq=512 (Table 5). An evaluation that used only the all-NN benchmark would never see a gap of that size. The best RUSTKNN mode changes in the same way: on mnist, single-tree search wins the all-NN benchmark at k=1, but held out at nq=512 dual-tree search is 1.8× faster than single-tree (8.4 vs. 15.1 s; Appendix A.2). And on covtype, the C++ reference ties RUSTKNN’s best mode in all-NN at k=1 (13.0K vs. 13.6K ms, intervals overlapping) but is 1.5× faster held out at nq=512 (53.0 vs. 81.1 ms, intervals disjoint). This divergence is systematic: in all-NN mode the query tree is the reference tree, so dual-tree and batch methods amortize their tree-building and expansion costs over all n points; held out, the query tree is shallow and that amortization is small. Where our implementation loses. Table 5 also contains a result worth stating plainly rather than glossing: on covtype held-out—arguably the most practically relevant cell in the paper, being the one dataset with both large n and moderate d—the C++ reference is fastest at every nq, and RUSTKNN’s best mode (dual-tree) is 1.2–1.5× slower. RUSTKNN-Single is also slower than mlpack-Single at nq ≥128 (176 vs. 142 ms at nq=512). But single-tree is the wrong RUSTKNN mode here: RUSTKNN-Dual is 2.2× faster than RUSTKNN-Single at nq=512 and 1.75× faster than mlpack- Single. Both facts are the same lesson from opposite directions—an implementation evaluated in only one query mode gets misranked, whichever way the misranking happens to fall. 9
