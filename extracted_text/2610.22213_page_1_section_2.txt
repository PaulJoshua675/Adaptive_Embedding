Coordinate descent methods have found wide applications in large-scale optimization problems in machine learning (ML) [62]. One such representative algorithm is randomized block coordinate descent (RBCD), which iteratively updates a randomly chosen coordinate block along the negative direction of the corresponding partial gradient while keeping the other coordinates fixed. This scheme is easy to implement and highly efficient for large-scale optimization problems, where even the simplest full- dimensional operations are rather expensive [47]. In the past decade, RBCD has been improved in various directions, such as momentum acceleration [33, 41, 47, 48], variance reduction [25–27], and distributed optimization [52, 64]. The popularity of RBCD has motivated extensive studies on its optimization guarantees [42, 47, 51], which reveal how the trained model would behave on training examples. However, in ML we are more interested in the generalization behavior of the learned model on testing examples [53]. To this end, a central topic in statistical learning theory is the generalization gap, which measures the difference between training and testing [28]. Specifically, the algorithmic stability approach is used to analyze the generalization of RBCD with coordinate-wise updates in convex settings [61, 63]. For nonconvex problems, the literature on stochastic gradient descent (SGD) shows that meaningful stability bounds typically rely on very small step sizes [28, 32], which can limit the optimization performance within a realistic computational budget. As an alternative, the uniform convergence approach does not require convexity assumptions [22, 44] and allows for standard polynomially decaying step sizes [36]. However, existing work based on this approach primarily focus on SGD [35, 38], whereas the generalization of RBCD remains largely unexplored in nonconvex settings. In this paper, we establish generalization guarantees for RBCD in nonconvex settings. We derive high-probability bounds on the gradients of population risks for general nonconvex objectives, and on the excess population risks for gradient-dominated objectives.
