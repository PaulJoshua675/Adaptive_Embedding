R is explicit: it is stated openly, and others can check it. Move 37 was a stone on the board in full view, and the rest of the game put it to the test. 2. There is no record of the route to R that a person can follow step by step. Here the machines differ. • When Deep Blue beat the reigning world champion Garry Kasparov in 1997, it searched six to eight moves deep for each side, scoring 200 million positions a second with rules that people had programmed into it (Graepel 2026). There were far too many steps for anyone to read, but each one applied rules written in human terms. • AlphaGo worked differently. Its neural networks supplied hunches about which moves looked promising and which positions looked won, and its search tested those hunches by reading ahead through possible replies. The search kept a record: a game tree with thousands of branches, each a possible future, in which every move and position carried its networks’ judgments (Graepel 2026). The tree can be walked, but the judgments on it come from networks that give no reasons. • A large language model typically keeps no such record. The chain of thought it writes out looks like deliberation, but research shows that it is often constructed after the fact, “reaching an answer by one route but reporting another” (Graepel 2026; see §7.3). Condition 2 is therefore a matter of degree: Deep Blue’s route was long but readable, AlphaGo’s was recorded but rested on its networks’ hunches, and an LLM’s is neither recorded nor reliably reported. 3. No one yet has the tacit understanding of R (the feel for why it works) needed to extend it, teach it, or judge what it is for. When Move 37 was played, it had almost no roots in human play. AlphaGo’s own policy network, trained to predict the moves of strong human players, gives a measure of this: it estimated the chance that an expert would play the move at roughly one in 10,000 (Graepel 2026). Polanyi’s dictum implies that a rootless result is not yet complete knowledge. If explicit knowledge must be tacitly understood and applied, a result that nobody understands in that way is true but idle: it can be cited, but not used as knowledge is used. Condition 2 is the one a substrate can change, as the three machines show; AlphaGo’s game tree is the kind of record that the second layer of §8.1 is meant to keep. Conditions 1 and 3 belong to the result and to the community respectively. Pritchard draws the same line between knowing and understanding: a student can know Pythagoras’ theorem on the testimony of an expert teacher while lacking any grasp of why it holds or how to apply it, and it is understanding, which involves that grasp, that education aims at (Pritchard 2023, 195–197). Ten years after Move 37, Go shows this line at work, with the AI in the teacher’s place. Professional players now train by copying AI moves as closely as they can, “even when the machine’s thinking remains mysterious to them”, and a Go professor observes that “top-tier players haven’t yet been able to deduce the general principles behind AI moves” (Kim 2026). A rootless result is known in the first way: it can be checked or accepted, but no one yet understands it well enough to extend, teach or judge it. 7.2 A worked case The Jacobian counterexample satisfies all three conditions at the moment of its announcement.
