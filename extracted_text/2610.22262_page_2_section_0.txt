In contrast, the order-2 Wasserstein PD distances, which accumulate mismatch over all persistence pairs, favor the GAN on 155 and 158 fields. This pattern is consistent with the sharper GAN better matching the aggregate distribution of ground-truth birth–death pairs while incurring larger individual discrepancies and preserving the merge hierarchy less consistently. Neither conventional fidelity measures nor any single topological distance provides a complete account of reconstruction quality. These observations motivate the central question of this work: can a fidelity-oriented CNN be fine-tuned toward persistence- relevant scalar structure while retaining strong conventional fi- delity? We approach this question through a loss study rather than a single proposed loss. Starting from the pretrained CNN, we fine- tune with differentiable auxiliary losses that target different aspects of scalar structure: scalar speed, gradient magnitude, soft high- speed level sets, high-speed local maxima, and fixed-pair supervi- sion, which uses the Topology ToolKit (TTK) [30] offline to locate the endpoints of ground-truth persistence pairs and penalizes er- rors in the predicted values and persistence at those fixed locations. None of these losses computes a persistence diagram or merge tree of the prediction; PD and MT distances are used only to evaluate the trained models. Every configuration is compared with a matched control that is fine-tuned identically (same initialization, data, optimizer, and schedule) but with the reconstruction loss alone; we call it the stan- dard fine-tuning control. Differences from this control therefore measure the effect of the auxiliary losses rather than that of contin- ued fine-tuning. We trained and report 16 configurations in total, in- cluding all eight combinations of the speed, gradient, and level-set losses, which allows the contribution of each term and their interac- tions to be estimated. The study identifies gradient supervision and fixed-pair supervision as complementary signals, and we use their combination as the final objective. The final objective lowers all three PD distances relative to both the pretrained CNN (by 25–31% in the mean) and the matched con- trol (by 32–35%), whereas standard fine-tuning on its own makes all three worse; all three PD distances are lower than the CNN’s on 150 of the 168 benchmark fields. The final model retains about half of the PSNR gain of standard fine-tuning. Mean MT distance also improves, but MT gets worse on 59 of the 150 fields whose PD distances all improve, indicating that PD and MT measure comple- mentary information. This work provides a controlled proof of concept for topology- inspired supervision in scientific SR. Its principal contributions are: • a characterization of descriptor-dependent disagreement in wind-field SR, showing that pretrained CNN and GAN recon- structions are ranked differently by fidelity, physics-oriented, merge-tree, and even different persistence-diagram distances, which motivates topology-aware supervision of the fidelity- oriented CNN; • a family of lightweight, differentiable topology-inspired losses for fine-tuning an SR network, culminating in an objec- tive that combines gradient-magnitude supervision with fixed- pair supervision at TTK-identified ground-truth persistence- pair endpoints; • a controlled comparison with fine-tuning on the reconstruc- tion loss alone, showing that the persistence improvements are not explained by continued fine-tuning; • a fully reported loss study of 16 configurations with facto- rial analyses, showing that gradient supervision produces the largest PD improvement among the scalar-field losses but does not improve MT agreement, whereas fixed-pair super- vision gives the largest and most consistent joint PD and MT improvement; and • an evaluation with three PD distances and MT distance, us- ing a validated PD-distance implementation, showing that PD and MT improvements are frequently decoupled at the level of individual fields and therefore capture complementary in- formation. The remainder of the paper reviews wind-field SR and scalar- field topology, describes the data and controlled fine-tuning design, introduces the auxiliary losses and the loss study, and evaluates their effects using fidelity, physics-oriented, PD, and MT measures. 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 2.1 Wind-field super-resolution and evaluation Stengel et al. [28] introduced the PhIRE framework for large-factor SR of climatological wind and solar data using CNN and GAN models. Subsequent work has explored transformer, diffusion, and generative approaches for wind-field downscaling and renewable- energy applications [29, 21, 6, 8]. Complementary work on con- strained climate downscaling has shown that physical consistency constraints can be built into neural SR models, motivating evalu- ation beyond visual or pointwise fidelity [14]. At the same time, prior studies have documented disagreement between pointwise nu- merical errors and visual, spatial-frequency, or distributional as- sessments [15, 2]. Such disagreement is especially consequential for scientific fields, where sharper output can represent either re- covered physical structure or model-induced artifacts. We therefore evaluate not only PSNR and SSIM, but also wind-power-density (WPD), spectral, gradient, exceedance, component-structure, and topological measures. Beyond meteorological downscaling, super-resolution has been studied directly for scientific vector-field visualization. Here, spa- tial SR estimates a higher-resolution field from a coarser one at the same time instant, whereas temporal SR reconstructs missing inter- mediate time steps. Guo et al. [12] proposed SSR-VFD for spatial SR of vector fields, An et al. [1] joint space–time SR, and Han and Wang [13] temporal SR of unsteady vector fields; these methods aim to reconstruct vector-valued data in a form useful for down- stream visualization and analysis. Our setting is complementary. The SR network still reconstructs the complete wind vector [u,v], and topology-inspired supervision is applied to the derived scalar wind speed in order to study persistence and merge hierarchy. 2.2 Scalar filtrations and topological descriptors Let s : Ω→R denote a scalar wind-speed field on a spatial do- main Ω. Its topology can be studied by sweeping a scalar threshold through the field. The sublevel and superlevel sets are S− τ = {x ∈Ω| s(x) ≤τ}, S+ τ = {x ∈Ω| s(x) ≥τ}. (1) As τ changes, connected components appear and merge only at crit- ical values. Sublevel filtrations emphasize low-valued basins; su- perlevel filtrations provide the dual view of high-valued peaks and regions. Several of our auxiliary losses target high-speed regions and local maxima, which are most intuitive from the superlevel view, in which a prominent maximum gives birth to a high-speed component. All reported topological descriptors, and the persis- tence pairs used by fixed-pair supervision, instead use the sublevel convention of our TTK pipeline. The two views are dual: in a sub- level filtration of a two-dimensional field, a local maximum does not create a component but closes a cycle [9]. Under our sublevel convention, maxima therefore appear as death endpoints of finite saddle–maximum pairs (D1), one of the two pair families used in our evaluation (Sec. 5).
