FidLIF supports full-sequence parallel training and step-by- step incremental inference. Moreover, the learned neuronal dynamics parameters in other methods are usually uncon- strained, while FidLIF enforces 0 < ρt < 1, preserving the leaky dynamics of LIF neurons. FidLIF is highly competitive. On sequential CIFAR10, FidLIF’s best individual run reaches 91.25%. On sequential CIFAR100 FidLIF reaches 66.42%. On CIFAR10-DVS it attains 84.13%, and on SHD, which has 80–140 time steps, FidLIF reaches 94.38%, showing that a maximum of three iterations remains effective even on sequences of such length. A central advantage of FidLIF is that the same trained neuron supports both full- sequence parallel and step-by-step incremental form. We evaluate each trained model in both execution forms without retraining or fine-tuning. As shown in Table 2, the serial ac- curacy closely matches the parallel accuracy. The remaining gaps mainly reflect the discrepancy between the parallelly recovered dynamics and exact step-by-step serial dynamics. In multi-layer networks, such neuron-level discrepancies can accumulate across layers, while task-dependent activation distributions also differ from the Gaussian currents used in the single-layer fidelity experiment. Overall, a maximum of three iterations is sufficient for complex networks on real benchmarks, enabling models trained in parallel forward passes to be deployed for serial inference, which is essen- tial for real-time edge applications. Effect of the Maximum Number of Iterations. We also examine whether training benefits from more iterations. Ta- ble 3 compares maximum iterations K = 3 and 5 on the two sequential CIFAR benchmarks. Accuracy differs by less than 0.3%, indicating that the trajectories recovered by a maximum iteration budget of K = 3 are already enough for training. Nevertheless, K can be increased when additional iterations are required, with negligible additional memory cost.
