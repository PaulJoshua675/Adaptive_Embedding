Recent dense retrieval systems increasingly incor- porate reasoning through query rewriting, decom- position, reranking, and reasoning-conditioned em- beddings (Qin et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Asai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Muennighoff et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Lan et al., 2025), moti- vated by the observation that semantic refinement can improve retrieval for complex queries. However, existing reasoning-based retrieval sys- tems generally treat additional reasoning as benefi- cial, applying it without distinguishing whether further refinement helps or hurts retrieval qual- ity. In practice, retrieval-oriented reasoning often exhibits diminishing returns. As a result, longer reasoning trajectories do not always improve retrieval quality, and can even harm re- trieval discrimination, increase inference latency, and introduce unnecessary computational cost. To understand how retrieval utility evolves dur- ing reasoning, we analyze retrieval specificity across intermediate reasoning steps on BRIGHT. As shown in Figure 1, retrieval specificity improves on average during early reasoning steps, suggesting that additional refinement can help construct more discriminative retrieval representations. However, this improvement is highly heterogeneous: variabil- ity increases rapidly with reasoning depth, indicat- ing that different queries benefit from substantially different amounts of reasoning. The fraction of queries that have already passed their optimal rea- soning step increases and exceeds 50% by Step 10.
