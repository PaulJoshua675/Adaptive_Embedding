Learning from global performance feedback without transmitting error derivatives through neural circuitry is a central challenge in biologically plausible credit assignment. Backpropagation (BP) solves this problem computationally, but its conventional implementation relies on coordinated forward and backward computations, precise layer-specific error signals, and corresponding feedback pathways whose biological realization remains debated [1, 27]. Biological synapses, in contrast, exhibit spontaneous fluctuations and remodeling, while synaptic plasticity can be regulated by neuromodulatory signals associated with reward and behavioral outcomes [28–31]. These properties motivate learning mechanisms that exploit local stochastic exploration and globally available evaluative signals to guide synaptic adaptation. Weight perturbation (WP) offers a particularly direct approach to this problem. Synaptic weights are transiently perturbed, and the resulting change in task performance determines the direction of subsequent plasticity [2, 3]. Each synapse requires only information about its own perturbation and a shared scalar feedback signal. The learning rule operates without an explicit differentiable model of the network, backward error propagation, or symmetric feedback weights. WP therefore provides a model-free candidate for biologically motivated learning, connecting stochastic synaptic plasticity with gradient-based optimization [8, 12, 28]. Perturbation-based learning has also been combined with competitive Hebbian plasticity in sparse excitatory neural networks [25], illustrating its compatibility with additional biologically motivated learning mechanisms. The principal obstacle is the statistical efficiency of high-dimensional credit assignment. WP infers a parameter update from scalar performance differences, introducing substantial variance into the estimated learning direction. Consequently, convergence can be considerably slower than gradient-based training [11, 12].
