Inputs are noisy, and the agents involved often hold different, sometimes conflicting objectives. Our results show that a model’s competence is shaped by the opponent it faces, and how both sides adapt as the interaction unfolds. Traditional benchmarks fail to capture this, as they test one model at a time against a fixed correct answer. This makes game inventories a natural fit for evaluating models in decision-pressured, interactive settings that they are actually deployed to in practice. Moreover, because game states are generated dynamically through interaction, games are more resistant to the training-data contamination that increasingly undermines static benchmarks [23]. That said, games are not immune to saturation. Even without contamination, the discriminative signal can erode as models approach equilibrium play (strategy profiles from which no player can gain by unilaterally deviating). More specifically, under standard no-regret learning, adaptive play converges to the set of coarse correlated equilibria (CCE), a superset of Nash equilibria [20]. In two-player zero-sum games such as Colonel Blotto, every equilibrium yields the same expected payoff, so once models play near-equilibrium strategies, none can outperform another and their results become indistinguishable. In contrast, in general-sum games the set of CCE is typically large and contains outcomes with very different payoffs. This suggests favouring general-sum games when building inventories meant to remain discriminative. 6 Conclusion and Future Work We extend game-theoretic evaluations and define strategic selection as a repeated opponent- conditioned meta game layered on top of the base game. Initial evaluations across 5 games and 10 models show that LLM strategic capability varies substantially across games and models, leaving considerable room for a selector to exploit. By adapting a range of selection methods, we report that the existing methods do not transfer equally well even against a simple fixed-model opponent, and that a plain bandit algorithm can surpass structurally heavier pre-trained encoders and LLM reflection methods. Subsequent runs against a selector opponent reveal that simple adaptations like discounted and adversarial bandit algorithms are not able to capture the non-stationary dynamics of opponent-conditioned strategic selection, and fail to outclass the plain sampler. Overall, the results highlight the challenges of strategic model selection, and motivate a selector design that can extract and learn from signals across 3 different dimensions: model capability, usage cost, and strategic disposition, in noise-heavy interactive settings. Our investigation was scoped to the analysis of 5 games. However, to better understand agent-to-agent interplay and the underlying safety and risk factors, it is important to consider games from a range of fields, including economics, social sciences, and operations research. We believe this choice should reflect both the measurable effect of the game and the underlying practical application of the selector. We also relied on explicit parsed game actions as a communication method between the models, which underestimates both the strategic capabilities (e.g. persuasion), and the associated risk factors in deployment. A more realistic setting should capture agent-to-agent natural language interactions [27]. We leave such free-form interactions to future work. Finally, high-stakes repeated interactions (e.g. negotiations, auctions) involve a risk factor not captured by expected gain. This can include catastrophic losses, regulatory penalties, or reputational damage. Capturing the variance in realised gain to account for involved risk is another future direction. For example, a risk knob (β ∈R: β = 0 is risk-neutral, β > 0 risk-averse, β < 0 risk-seeking) could allow users to better align the selector with their deployment policies.
