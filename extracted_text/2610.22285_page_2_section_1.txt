Learning from Imperfect Teaching Reward learning from human feedback typically assumes the teacher is optimal or noisily optimal [10, 11], but human input is routinely suboptimal. Some methods build richer models of the human, capturing systematic or structured deviations from opti- mality [12, 13]. Others treat suboptimality as localized failures of supervision rather than uniform noise: the robot reasons about how well its hypothesis space explains the human’s input and flags what it cannot account for [14, 15, 16], or detects from the teaching data which features were well- supervised versus underspecified [3, 4, 17]. Rather than resolving the diagnosed gap by requesting more data, we use the diagnosis to drive active preference queries, shifting resolution onto the robot. Active Preference Elicitation Preference queries, which ask the human to choose between trajec- tories, are a low-effort channel for reward learning. The robot can select each query to maximize information gain, minimizing the comparisons the human must answer [6]. Prior work extends this loop in various ways: warm-starting the reward belief from demonstrations [8], enriching queries to ask which features make a trajectory preferable [7], and using language models to select queries and ease answering [18]. From Biyik et al. [8] we inherit the initialization of the reward belief from demonstrations. But rather than treating all features alike, we shape that belief to encode where teaching was reliable, letting the information-gain criterion target the underspecified features. 2
