Among the retriever based methods, we evaluate a training-free zero-shot model that uses BGE-m3 embedding model at its core. We employ BAAI/bge-reranker-v2-m3[5] off the shelf with no task adaptation; it establishes how much of the task is solved by a general-purpose reranker and how far the vocabulary is from its pre- training distribution. The trained cross-encoder fine-tunes the same checkpoint on our 13, 319 training documents for 10 epochs with batch size 32, learning rate 2 × 10−5, linear warm-up over the first 10% of steps, weight decay 0.01 and maximum sequence length 512, with early stopping. The descriptor side is the bare preferred label rather than the enriched label text used for retrieval; the enriched form was tried first and performed worse, as the concatenated aliases and scope notes dilute the pair signal the cross-encoder relies on. The decision threshold is selected on the validation split. TAGHN (w/o CE) is our own system with the cross-encoder attention channel removed (§4.3), reported along with the baselines to separate the contribution of the graph from that of the pairwise signal folded into it. All systems are trained on the same 13, 319 documents, evalu- ated on the same 1, 660 test documents against the same active vocabulary of 6,198 descriptors, and scored with a single evaluation script. 5.5 Evaluation metrics We report precision, recall and F1-score aggregated by different means to capture the highly skewed distribution of subjects. • Micro: Pools every document, subject before aggregating. Since every prediction counts, it is dominated by head labels • Macro: Computes precision, recall and F1-scores for each subject and averages over them. It is a tail-sensitive view and provides performance across label frequency. 6 Results and discussion The table 4 reports the performance on the test set according to different aggregated metrics. Significance is assessed by a paired approximate randomization test with Holm-correction applied to the table. The TAGHN model outperforms all other systems in three out of six metrics namely macro precision, recall and F1 and each of these are statistically significant with every baseline. On micro F1-score, TAGHN reaches 0.487 which is in close competition with the XR- transformer. Against the strongest reranker baseline, a fine-tuned bge-m3 cross encoder, the TAGHN model (without incorporating the cross-encoder) scores a +4.6 increase in micro F1-score while maintaining the macro F1-scores. Combining the complementary signal from the cross-encoder, we are able to show a significant increase of +4.4 points on micro F1 and +3.3 macro F1-score over TAGHN without the cross-encoder. Similarly, we can observe an improvement of +9 micro F1 points and +3.4 macro F1 points over fine-tuned bge-m3 cross-encoder model. Compared to XMLC specialised model such as bonsai, TAGHN adds a +9.3 micro F1 and +16 macro F1 points. In terms of macro F1, TAGHN is ahead of the XRTransformer by +10.2 points. We report the test-set labels binned by their frequency on training dataset. Consistent perfor- mance across label frequency is an essential requirement of the subject indexing, because the task is often characterized by long tails. TAGHN leads the mid-frequency bins, reaching 0.388 on 6–20 (728 labels) and 0.438 on 21–100 (663 labels), and it attains the best aggregate macro-F1 of 0.380 over all 2,011 test-gold descriptors ahead of the graph reranker without the cross-encoder (0.347), the trained cross-encoder (0.346), Xrt (0.278), and Omikuji (0.220). The Xrt is significantly stronger on frequent labels, taking 101–500 (0.571 vs. 0.540). It also leads above 500 occurrences (0.660 vs. 0.464, p=0.009), but this bin contains only eight descriptors, which limits how small a p-value the randomization test can produce. It also degrades sharply into the tail and scores 0.000 on unseen labels by construction, having no parameters for descriptors absent from training. TAGHN concentrates its advantage on the 6–100 range, which covers 1,391 descriptors against only 118 above 100, the region where the bulk of real indexing work happens. On the 70 unseen descriptors, trained cross-encoder (0.288) re- mains the strongest system and is significantly better than TAGHN (0.114). While, zero-shot bge-m3 model (0.195, p=0.124) scores bet- ter than TAGHN, it is not statistically significant. On the 1–5 bin, the difference between the trained cross-encoder and TAGHN is not significant (0.286 vs. 0.278, p=0.705), and we regard the two systems as tied. The configuration that maximizes aggregate F1 is thus not the configuration that best serves the extreme tail. 6.2 Head-to-head comparison Aggregate metrics can conceal whether a system wins broadly or wins by a big margin on a few documents.
