The model receives W0, rules, and all three events as text and predicts the complete W3. The benchmark covers eight operation families on two-, three-, and four-dimensional grids, with instances varying in history length and within-family workload. Each instance provides an initial grid, explicit rules, and an ordered event sequence as text; the model predicts the complete final grid after all supplied events in a single response. The rules specify how each event selects locations and changes their values in the current configuration. A simulator executes the full event sequence to compute an exact reference state. Comparing every predicted value with this reference establishes whether the whole state is correct and reveals the extent and location of errors when it is not. Figure 1 illustrates the reference states produced by a movement history in two and three dimensions. We evaluate six large language models under a shared protocol and find that exact-match accuracy declines sharply with history length. Models with similar single-event accuracy separate over longer histories, and even similar rates of complete recovery can accompany quite different incorrect predictions. At eight events, Kimi K2.5 and MiniMax M2.5 have similar exact-match accuracy, yet their median wrong-cell counts are 14 and 67 on the 84 shared instances where both return valid incorrect states. Grid-Bench brings diverse spatial operations into a common, executable framework for predicting and eval- uating complete worlds. The accompanying six-model study examines when models recover the final state across event histories and spatial conditions, and what they get wrong when recovery fails. 2
