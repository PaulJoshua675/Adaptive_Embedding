Strategic reasoning in multi-agent systems (MAS) hinges on de- termining what outcomes groups of agents (termed coalitions) can bring about through their interactions. In many multi-agent settings, especially safety-critical systems, properties of interest go beyond plain reward signals and include temporally extended goals such as reachability/liveness (e.g., eventually reaching a tar- get region or completing a mission), or safety invariants (e.g., al- ways avoid unsafe regions, or never violate constraints) [4, 23]. Similar objectives are naturally captured by temporal logics [38] which provide precise languages and semantics to specify and ver- ify such properties. Languages relevant to specification of strate- gic properties include Alternating-Time Temporal Logic (atl [3]), Dynamic Coalition Logic [22], Probabilistic Alternating-𝜇Calcu- lus [47], Game Description Logic (gdl [16]), and See-To-It-That (stit [7]). However, such logics typically resolve non-determinism by restricting to coalitional modalities which aim to universally and unilaterally (inevitably) force a property to be true. On the other hand, rational synthesis [20, 52] aims to find strate- gies that are self-enforcing through stable equilibria, instead of quantifying against all possible behaviour (including irrational be- haviour) by other agents who have their own objectives. Therefore, rational synthesis is typically less conservative than adversarial synthesis. Settings we consider can be represented formally as (general- sum) stochastic games, which model multiple decision-making agents that interact over time in a dynamic environment with probabilistic behaviours. Each agent repeatedly selects an action, and the combination of agents’ non-deterministic choices and the environment’s probabilistic transitions determines the evolution of the game. Stochastic games have been applied in domains as diverse as cybersecurity [60], epidemics [29], auctions [39], and routing in stochastic traffic networks [59]. We use an expressive specification style in which each agent has its own probabilistic temporal objective as a logical formula inter- preted on finite traces. Finite traces have a natural correspondence with reinforcement learning (RL) as tasks are often trained and executed episodically. Rationality is captured by imposing that the learned joint strategy profile satisfies a specific solution concept (e.g., (𝜀-)Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium), where each agent’s payoff reflects the preferred direction of its satisfaction probability. Our approach emphasises learning a single committed strategy profile, allowing tractability by not requiring an explicit transition model of the underlying stochastic game which gener- ally grows combinatorially with respect to the number of agents involved. Contributions. Our contributions are as follows: (i) We formulate a learning-based rational-synthesis objective for finite-horizon sto- chastic games in which each agent has a probabilistic ltl𝑓objective and utilities encode the preferred direction of satisfaction probabil- ities; (ii) We compile per-agent ltl𝑓objectives into deterministic reward automata and show that terminal automaton rewards have expectation equal to satisfaction probability; (iii) We provide theo- retical analysis for the reward-automaton encoding, discounting effects, finite-sample satisfaction certification, and finite-horizon equilibrium existence and convergence; (iv) We evaluate satisfac- tion and strategic stability on 8 benchmark environment instances.
