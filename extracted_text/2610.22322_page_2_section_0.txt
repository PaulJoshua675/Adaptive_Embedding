This perspective echoes Minsky’s Society of Mind (Minsky, 1986), which explains intelligence through the organization and interaction of many individually limited components. Large language model (LLM) agents now exhibit strong individual reasoning capabilities, yet agent swarms remain limited in their ability to organize their own participation and actions toward a shared objective. Existing multi-agent systems typically place models within predefined interaction struc- tures: a manager delegates to workers, a verifier checks a generator, or agents communicate accord- ing to a prescribed workflow (Hong et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). Even when the participating agents are trainable, the orchestration policy that determines which agent acts next often remains fixed throughout training (Feng et al., 2026). This separation overlooks the coupling between individual capabilities and swarm orchestration. Which agent acts in a particular state shapes not only the swarm’s trajectory, but also which agents receive experience and learning signals. As agents improve, effective patterns of participation can change in turn. An agent that should act early in training may become unnecessary later; an action that was previously unhelpful may become valuable after downstream agents improve. Training individual agents under fixed orchestration therefore optimizes only one side of this coupled process. We instead ask: Can decentralized agent swarms jointly learn to act and self-orchestrate from experience? Learning self-orchestration in decentralized agent swarms poses a joint orchestration and credit- assignment problem. At each turn, agents must determine who should act. When task feedback becomes available, the swarm must also determine how to train the agents whose actions shaped the trajectory. These decisions are coupled: participation determines which agents acquire experience, while learning changes which and how agents should participate in the future. We therefore introduce market-based post-training (MPT), a framework that enables decentralized agent swarms to learn both how to act and how to self-organize their participation. Following Qi et al. (2026), agents compete for opportunities to act through bids that reflect their willingness to participate in the current decision. An economic incentive structure connects these participation decisions with credit assignment, using collective outcomes to improve both action and bidding behavior. As agents become more capable, the swarm learns when to rely on each member, allowing individual capabilities and self-orchestration to evolve together without a central coordinating agent. We evaluate MPT on embodied crafting, robot navigation, and deep research. Comparisons on these benchmarks show higher training rewards than monolithic post-training, while further anal- ysis reveals that agent participation evolves during learning. To isolate the contribution of self- orchestration, we compare coordination strategies using the same post-trained action policies. On Iron Pickaxe, MPT achieves a 63.4% relative improvement in mean episode reward over the strongest alternative, Graph-of-Agents. We further show that both bidding and action benefit from learning, with their joint use yielding the strongest performance. A credit-assignment ablation shows that learned bids also provide more effective action-training signals. We also examine the reusability, scalability, and robustness of MPT. Trained swarms adapt more rapidly than the single-agent baseline to new crafting objectives. Increasing the inference-time pop- ulation improves pass@3 across crafting milestones without additional training, supporting the scal- ability of learned self-orchestration. Larger populations also better tolerate occasional agent unavail- ability. These results suggest that learned self-orchestration supports both reuse across objectives and flexible deployment with varying agent populations. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We introduce MPT, a framework for jointly learning action policies and self-orchestration in decentralized agent swarms, allowing patterns of participation to adapt as agents improve. (2) We evaluate MPT on embodied crafting, robot navigation, and deep research, demonstrating gains over monolithic post-training. We further show the benefits of learned self-orchestration, learning both bidding and action, and using learned bids for credit assignment. (3) We demonstrate the reusability, scalability, and robustness of trained agent swarms: they adapt faster to new tasks, while larger inference-time populations improve performance and robustness. 2
