Together, these propositions explain why shifting the codebook can adapt LCT to different input scales at runtime. 5.2 Allocated rate and scaling with tensor size For a tensor of N elements comprising full blocks, let S be the symbols per stream, F the reserved bits per stream, T the number of raw tail exponents, and K the number of overflowing streams. Each tail exponent occupies one byte and each affected stream requires nine metadata bytes. The core rate is bLCT = 8 + F S + 8 T N + 72K N , ρLCT = 16 bLCT . (4) The first two terms are the regular payload; the last two account for raw overflow tails and overflow metadata. A smaller F reduces the fixed payload but can increase the number of overflowing streams K and raw tail length T, reducing the compression ratio. This assumes full blocks and excludes padding and allocation overhead. Appendix B.2 gives a more detailed analysis. 6 Experiments 6.1 Encoding and decoding bandwidth First, we evaluate LCT’s encoding and decoding performance and compare it with existing methods. Table 4 compares the time to encode and decode a 1 GiB Gaussian tensor using LCT, SplitZip, DFloat11, ZipNN, and three nvCOMP codecs, LZ4, ANS, and Bitcomp. Appendix A.1 gives implementation details. LCT encodes fastest, with 2.38× the throughput of SplitZip, the next-fastest encoder. SplitZip decodes slightly faster, but LCT has a lower combined encode–decode time (4.967 versus 8.159 ms) and a higher compression ratio (1.455× versus 1.333×). The nvCOMP LZ4 and Bitcomp configurations slightly expand the input (0.998×), while nvCOMP ANS achieves 1.258× compression. All three encode and decode more slowly than LCT. DFloat11 and ZipNN achieve higher compression ratios, but their encoding times are over two orders of magnitude longer than LCT’s. Next, Figure 3 evaluates the throughput for varying input sizes. LCT scales well with input size, with peak encode throughput reaching 395.5 GiB/s. Table 8 in the appendix extends the comparison to NVIDIA V100, A40, and H100 GPUs. LCT remains the fastest encoder, while SplitZip remains the fastest decoder. 6.2 Fused pointwise operators Table 5 compares fused operators with two baselines using Gaussian BF16 input tensors of 1 GiB each. The naive path decompresses all operands before computation and returns the requested output format. The dense baseline is the standard PyTorch implementation, without any compression. Appendix A.2 gives the protocol. For dense output, LCT is 1.66–1.72× faster than the naive path. Operations with one compressed operand also slightly outperform the dense baseline since compression reduces the size of the tensor, and hence memory traffic.
