We added regular-expression negation detection and temporal cue matching to discount past-tense and resolved mentions. This substantially improved precision over the floor but was brittle. Hand-written patterns missed paraphrases and complex sentence structure, and over-aggressive negation occasionally suppressed true positives. Stage 2: Hybrid NLP. We layered three further components onto the rule-based system. Clinical-form and template filters remove boilerplate and blank-field matches, severity gates down-weight “mild,” “controlled” and “resolving” language, and simple cross-temporal handling was added. At equal overall accuracy to the rule-based model, this recovered recall that the stricter negation rules had removed, so the two systems sat at different points on the same precision–recall frontier rather than one dominating the other. Stage 3: Iterative single-LLM prototype. Before the full ground truth existed, we prototyped a three-pass LLM approach on a 32-patient subset to test whether contextual reasoning could move beyond the rule-based frontier. It established feasibility but used a different evaluation set with no paired ground truth, so it is not part of the six-method benchmark. Stage 4: Single-LLM and the dual-LLM consensus gate. Once the full ground truth was available, we evaluated a single clinical-NLP-expert LLM, whose accuracy (72.7%) edged above the rule-based frontier (70.9%) although the difference was not resolved at 189 cells. Adding a second, differently prompted domain-specialist model raised the question of how to combine the two outputs. We tested both aggregation rules on identical model outputs. An OR-gate (permissive, positive if either agent says Yes) maximizes sensitivity but readmits false positives, while an AND-gate (consensus, positive only if both agree) favors precision. The consensus gate gave the best overall accuracy and the most run-stable operating point, and was adopted. Rejected variant: a broader definition of the target. Having fixed the gate, we tried loosening what the prompt counts as a positive. Both agents were instructed to score any documented diarrhea as present, dropping the exclusion for diarrhea that was mild or already controlled with medication, on the reasoning that a symptom under treatment is still a symptom. This raised recall as intended but added far more false positives than it recovered true ones, so overall accuracy fell well below the adopted design and the change was reverted. Note S7 quantifies the same effect on the reported grid as versions V1 and V4. What the pairwise tests do and do not establish. Table S11, panel B, reports all 15 pairwise McNemar exact tests on the identical 189-cell paired ground truth. Every context-aware method significantly outperforms the regex floor (p < 0.0001, and after Holm correction). The comparisons above the floor are directionally consistent with the accuracy ordering, with the consensus model winning each head-to-head disagreement, for example 24 discordant cells to 14 against hybrid NLP, but none reaches significance (p = 0.11–1.0). This reflects limited power rather than an absence of effect. The consensus-versus-NLP gap rests on a net of roughly ten cells of 189, and confirming a difference of that size at 80% power would require on the order of 550 annotated cells, about three times the current ground truth. The final rerun sharpens the same reading. On the refreshed corpus and the corrected anchor the gate reaches 79.6% and the stronger single agent 79.2%, differing on two of 183 cells (Table S10, panel A).
