Hannagan Design choices specific to ImageNet. Under the official ImageNet checkpoint the synchronization memory uses random pairing: rather than forming the full D × D matrix, a fixed set of neuron pairs (i, j) is drawn once at initialization and only those entries are tracked, so the action and output slices are long vectors rather than a dense matrix. The checkpoint tracks 8196 pairs for the output slice and 2048 for the action slice, against D = 4096 neurons. The sampling is broad rather than selective: a neuron takes part in 4.0 output pairs on average and 98% of neurons appear in at least one, so the readout draws on the population as a whole rather than on a privileged subset. None of this affects any state-space quantity used here. What is known about how the CTM forms decisions. The original CTM paper reports several findings on ImageNet-1K (Darlow et al., 2025) at a budget of 50 ticks. Most images pass a 0.8 certainty threshold within ten ticks with a long tail out to fifty, so the budget could in principle be halted early, as in adaptive-computation-time recurrent net- works (Graves, 2016); the logits are unusually well calibrated without post-hoc adjustment; and reading the prediction at the most certain tick beats reading the current one beyond roughly fifteen ticks, while averaging logits does worst—so the model passes through low- certainty, sometimes incorrect states before settling. That certainty rises—equivalently, that output entropy falls—along the thought in most cases is therefore already known, and is in fact built into the CTM’s objective. The attention trajectory over image features also emerges through training, and visual inspection shows diverse, multiscale dynamics with low-frequency traveling waves across a learned feature map arising with no driving signal—a phenomenon with a long history in cortex (Muller et al., 2018). One further property of the released ImageNet checkpoint bears on what follows: its learned decay rates are negligible over the 50-tick budget, so Sout is in effect a running average of the state’s co-activations, a deterministic quadratic functional of z1, . . . , zt. We therefore analyze the state zt, where the recurrence’s learned per-tick maps and its fixed points live. 3. An attracting, non-normal fixed-point curve What governs the state’s motion? One way to study recurrent network dynamics is to locate the fixed points of the recurrence and linearize there (Sussillo and Barak, 2013)—we hold the memory context At fixed, which turns the tick into an ordinary map zt+1 = f(zt; At) on state space. We reconstruct that map exactly (a single reconstructed step reproduces the model’s next state to < 10−6) and iterate it: it converges at 3055 of 3072 image–tick pairs from 256 images (residual < 10−6; App. A), and at every one of these fixed points z⋆the Jacobian J, read by autograd power iteration, has spectral radius well below one (ρ(J) = 0.15 median, 95% interval 0.14–0.16, max 0.95). By Banach’s theorem, applied in an adapted norm (App. A), the fixed point is locally unique and attracting: the state has one well-defined target at each tick. Beyond the neighbourhood the theorem covers, 3456 restarts from random states and from other images’ states all converged to the same fixed point, and we found no second basin (App. A). The map is nevertheless strongly non-normal: although every eigenvalue decays, the largest singular value exceeds one (σmax(J) = 3.2 median, up to 74), so it briefly stretches a few directions even while contracting overall (Fig. 2a).
