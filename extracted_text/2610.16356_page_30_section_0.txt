(2016) in ABC and Nasution and Kurniawan (2018) in TLBO. In contrast, algorithms that emphasise rapid convergence, such as HHO, may settle prematurely into suboptimal solutions, producing less distinct spatial clusters. This problem also justified the choice of using rapid progressive dives in HHO when clustering the green infrastructure vulnera- bility in Indonesia, as done by Sumargo et al. (2024). Moreover, this observation is consistent with the ”no free lunch” theo- rem, which suggests that no single algorithm universally solves all problems optimally (Yang, 2014; Nasution and Kurniawan, 2018). Each optimisation algorithm offers distinct competitive advantages; for example, TLBO excels at avoiding local optima at the cost of higher computational demand, while other algorithms may rapidly converge but risk local optima traps. Conse- quently, selecting an algorithm from naspaclust depends on the user’s pri- orities: whether efficiency or clustering quality is paramount. Furthermore, to mitigate the risks of unintended cluster formation, careful initialisation is recommended using parameters such as randomN in FGWC is recommended. On the other hand, the practical implications of these findings for policy- making are significant. Policymakers must carefully select algorithms align- ing with their specific analytical objectives, considering both clustering ac- curacy and spatial coherence. Algorithms such as TLBO or ABC, which demonstrate robust spatial performance, are preferable choices. In addition, policymakers should consider clustering methods in groups or validate clus- ter solutions using expert judgment to enhance reliability and ensure robust policy applications from clustering spatial analyses. 30
