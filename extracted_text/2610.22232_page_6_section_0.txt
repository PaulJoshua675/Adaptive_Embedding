Table 2 presents a com- parison between WaveLLM and traditional, learned, and LLM-based methods. Table 2 reports WaveLLM rates alongside rates quoted from prior work. Table 3 combines our iWave and WaveLLM mea- surements with runtime figures quoted from prior work; Tables 4 and 5 report measurements from our setup. WaveLLM with a 3B backbone yields lower rates than both P2- LLM and the visual-prompt method, even though both employ 8B backbones. On Kodak, WaveLLM achieves 2.51 bpsp versus 2.83 bpsp for both LLM competitors, a reduction of 11.3%. On CLIC.pro and DIV2K, it reaches 2.24 and 2.27 bpsp versus 2.35/2.25 and 2.51/2.29 bpsp, respectively. WaveLLM is also competitive with CALLIC [20], a strong learned method that uses content- adaptive per-image fine-tuning. Among strong non-LLM baselines, WaveLLM also improves over JPEG XL, the strongest traditional codec in Table 2, and the lossless iWave codec in the matched 5/3 setting, by 0.36 and 0.43 bpsp on Kodak. Exact reconstruction was verified on all reported test sets, with no observed value-range overflow or out-of-range input or label indices. 4.2.2 Computational Cost. Table 3 reports runtime and computa- tional cost on Kodak. Because hardware, implementation details, and model sizes differ across papers, we interpret these numbers qualitatively. WaveLLM requires about 2.6 × 107 kMACs/pixel and takes 65.1 s to encode and 152.8 s to decode each Kodak image in our implementation. Although its 3B backbone is smaller than the 8B backbones used by prior LLM-based methods, end-to-end run- time remains hardware- and implementation-dependent. WaveLLM parallelizes its 13 subband tasks across 8 RTX 3090 GPUs, and its peak memory of about 16.2 GB per card allows the full pipeline to fit on 24 GB consumer GPUs. This implementation also benefits from patch-wise parallel processing and an optimized arithmetic coder. By contrast, the iWave baseline follows serial pixel-wise in- ference and is measured on a single RTX 3090 in our environment. The complexity and timing figures for Delétang et al. are quoted from [8] rather than measured by us, as the original model weights are not publicly available. These measurements describe the current implementation; runtime reduction remains future work. 4.3 Analysis of the Proposed Design 4.3.1 Why the Wavelet Domain. To test directly whether the cod- ing domain matters for LLM-based entropy modeling, we imple- ment two alternative variants under the same general paradigm: a pixel-domain variant that performs next-token prediction on ras- terized RGB values, and a residual-based variant that encodes the residual between the original image and a lossy reconstruction, using the global lossy image and the corresponding local recon- struction patch as visual prompts. Table 4 summarizes results across all three benchmarks. Under the same Llama-3.2-3B backbone and next-token entropy-modeling framework, the wavelet-domain for- mulation is consistently the strongest. This comparison is especially relevant because WaveLLM is, to the best of our knowledge, the first LLM-based lossless compressor formulated directly in the wavelet domain. The pixel-domain variant is already competitive, indicating that the value interface and autoregressive LLM framework remain ef- fective beyond the wavelet setting. However, the wavelet-domain formulation outperforms it by 0.21, 0.11, and 0.17 bpsp on Ko- dak, CLIC.pro, and DIV2K, respectively. The residual-based variant yields higher rates under our implementation, and its total rate includes both the lossy base bits and the coded residual bits. We do not introduce an explicit parametric density model in the residual domain, which may limit how fully the model capacity is used in that setting. Overall, the wavelet-domain formulation performs best among the tested variants. 4.3.2 Wavelet-Domain Backbone-Scale Analysis.
