The first step therefore costs more than the two that follow, although a further loss of up to 0.14 cannot be excluded, and for an individual model the upper bound is as large as 0.25. Our initial hypothesis was that problem definition would be the bottleneck: an agent given a symptom or a wrong solution would build the wrong system. For the leading models, the data do not support this. One interpretation is that once these models must search for constraints at all, they also discover what the sponsor actually needs. For weaker models, the steps beyond L1 remain costly: across L0–L3, GPT-5.5 scores 0.51, 0.15, 0.04, and 0.06, and Claude Haiku 4.5 scores 0.11, −0.11, −0.30, and −0.25. The fully specified level yields a result of its own: even with every rule provided, no model exceeds 0.82 on the same 20 tasks. Knowing the facts does not remove the need for engineering. 5.3 Recovering Key Facts Does Not Ensure Effective Delivery Finding 3 Leading models obtain most of the facts held by stakeholders yet deliver only 0.60–0.70 of the reference uplift; stakeholder contact costs further reduce their scores. The grader records which key facts an agent obtained from stakeholders, which allows us to separate information discovery from system building. At L3 the three leading models ask 10.0–12.3 questions per task and obtain 72– 85% of the key facts. Their normalized business uplift is 0.60–0.70. Figure 5a shows the pattern across all thirteen configurations: more facts are associated with better systems, but the leading group sits at the far right of the plot and still well below the reference solution. In the 73 task–model cases where one of the three obtained every key fact, the normalized business uplift averages 0.66 and reaches at least 0.8 in 27 of them (37%).
