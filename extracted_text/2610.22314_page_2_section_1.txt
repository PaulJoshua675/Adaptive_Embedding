The World Health Organization (2025) estimates that more than one billion people worldwide live with a mental health condition. Conditions such as anxiety and depression are among the leading causes of disability, straining individuals, families, and healthcare systems in countries of every income level. Faced with this burden and a shortage of mental health professionals, people are increasingly turning to LLM-based chatbots for mental health support (McBain et al., 2025; Stade et al., 2026). Nevertheless, LLMs frequently generate nonfactual information, struggle to enforce appropriate bound- aries, validate maladaptive thoughts, and exhibit sycophancy by over-conforming to the user. These vulnerabilities may foster over-reliance and even so-called “AI psychosis”, in which interactions with chatbots contribute to the development or intensification of users’ delusion-like beliefs (Guo et al., 2024; American Psychological Association, 2025; Shen et al., 2026; Diel et al., 2026). Consequently, recent benchmarks have been developed to track the progress of LLMs in mental health interactions. However, privacy regulations and ethical concerns severely limit the availability of therapy transcripts, making it difficult to construct realistic evaluations. As a result, existing benchmarks primar- ily assess clinical knowledge through question-answering tasks (Zhang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Na, 2024; Lai et al., 2023) or single-turn response generation (Arora et al., 2025; Malik et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). Advancements in LLMs have significantly improved simulation, turning synthetic conversations into a viable replacement for static knowledge benchmarks and a valuable complement to human evaluation (Yao et al., 2025; He et al., 2026a). When it comes to patient simulation, LLMs are used to probe high-risk behaviour, such as suicide risk detection (Bentley et al., 2026), harmful responses un- der adversarial audits (Weilnhammer et al., 2026) and delusion reinforcement (Yeung et al., 2025), and to evaluate everyday, non-crisis support (Pombal et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026), which makes up most real use (Stade et al., 2026). However, these benchmarks employ patient simulators that can produce unrealistically articulate, forth- coming and self-reflective responses, and focus on individual sessions rather than repeated sessions with the same patient (Louie et al., 2024; Pombal et al., 2025; Hoang et al., 2026). This can limit opportunities to test how counsellors respond to hesitation, partial disclosure and resistance, or how counsellors use context from earlier sessions when patients return with new or unresolved concerns. This paper introduces MindEval2, a benchmark for evaluating AI mental health support of everyday, non-crisis use. Figure 1 presents the overall architecture, including its two main components. The first is MHQR (§3), a taxonomy developed by PhD level psychologists consisting of 14 rubrics that a counsellor turn can exhibit, each graded by severity; clinical psychologists use it to annotate counsellor turns, and we evaluate several LLMs as judges on their annotations. The second is MindSim (§4), a patient simulator that role-plays users of an AI mental health app across several sessions. Each patient is drawn from a clinically grounded profile whose presenting problems resemble, in rate and nature, that of real users. Additionally, every reply is preceded by a tool call that commits a move from a set of patient moves, increasing the steerability of patients towards diverse behaviours. Patients return for later sessions after a gap in which their situation may have changed, with a memory summarising previous sessions. We benchmark several state-of-the-art proprietary and open-weight LLMs (§5), and find that recent models have made substantial progress on safety-critical failures such as distortion reinforcement, with frontier models exhibiting only minor issues in that area. However, all models struggle to calibrate guidance, preferring to keep asking exploratory questions instead of providing concrete guidance to the patient. Furthermore, across multiple sessions, counsellors that build on previous sessions im- prove, whereas others keep asking about what the patient has already shared. This suggests that further progress in AI mental health support depends less on more capable models than on an ecosystem that better guides patients throughout their journey. 2
