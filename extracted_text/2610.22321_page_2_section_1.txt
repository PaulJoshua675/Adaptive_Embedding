However, evaluation methodologies have not kept pace with this evolution: the richer audio-visual content produced by joint models has made consistency evaluation more demanding. Current eval- uations largely reuse metrics from earlier simple and unidirectional tasks, where one modality was fixed and only a limited relation such as lip–speech or sound–action timing needed to be checked; accordingly, they often evaluate an audio-video clip as a whole and rely on simple predefined re- lations such as semantic similarity or temporal synchronization. Multi-event composition makes such clip-level evaluation unreliable because different event relations are entangled; for example, ImageBind-based metrics (Girdhar et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) encode the au- dio and video within a clip or temporal window into a shared embedding space and measure their overall similarity, which can remain high when dominant semantics match despite a local event inconsistency. Multi-layer audio mixture further complicates evaluation: metrics such as Synch- former (Iashin et al., 2024) predict an audio–video temporal offset from the mixed soundtrack with- out separating its layers. When physical sounds are mixed with voice-over or background music, sounds that should align with visible actions are evaluated together with audio that does not require such temporal correspondence, which can lead to an inaccurate consistency score. Complex cross- modal relations further challenge predefined matching rules; for example, AV-Align (Yariv et al., 2024) matches audio onsets with visual-motion peaks and may therefore penalize a valid off-screen sound that lacks direct visual evidence. Therefore, we need a more general audio-video consistency evaluator that can handle diverse event relations across different generation paradigms, particularly in modern joint generation. Moreover, we believe that such an evaluation model can serve as a training signal to directly improve generation models, closing the loop of “generate–evaluate–optimize.” To meet this need, we propose an event-centric evaluation perspective, in which the audio and video of each generated clip are first disentangled into individual events and analyzed independently before the final judgments. Under this perspective, we first separately extract and analyze audio and visual events, then cross-validate their relations across modalities, and finally aggregate the resulting judgments from coarse to fine. We formulate this final consistency assessment as a coarse- to-fine, three-level consistency metric. Global narrative harmony assesses whether the audio and video form a coherent scene and narrative. Event-level temporal synchronization establishes event relations, checks event presence and temporal alignment, and marks audio events without visual evidence. Fine-grained attribute alignment verifies the attributes of matched events and assesses whether marked audio is plausibly an off-screen sound, voice-over, or background music. By disentangling individual events, distinguishing different audio sources, and explicitly validating their cross-modal relations, this metric can separately assess overlapping events, handle audio layers with different roles, and accommodate valid asymmetric relations, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Based on this event-level perspective, we implement the above three-level metric as a trainable audio-video reward model, named AVC-Reward, initialized from Qwen3-Omni-Thinking (Xu et al., 2025).
