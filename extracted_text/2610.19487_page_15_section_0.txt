B. Projection and mechanistic non-identifiability Our information-theoretic formalism separates two consequences of coarse-graining. It allows us to discern information loss from changes in apparent interaction or- der. Data processing inequality guarantees that under no projection the available predictive information could in- crease; however, it does not constrain the interaction or- der at which the surviving dependence may manifest. A strictly third-order dependency may disguise as pairwise interaction under projection, with partial or complete in- formation preservation depending on the map (Fig. 1). Along the branch-to-soma cascade, the direct fraction F increases while available information decreases and the total-entropy fraction R falls (Fig. 2). This denominator trap concerns the fraction of surviving information un- der coarse-graining captured by the direct family, not the total-entropy fraction R. Residual Ihigher contraction is mathematically guaranteed under the closure condition of Eq. (14); without it, no description of universal in- teraction order is meaningful (Fig. S1). This traces the boundary of effective theories for neural systems: inte- grating out degrees of freedom renormalizes the orders of interactions as well as their strengths. Eliminating a me- diating variable generates higher-order couplings among those that remain, while merging variables into a collec- tive coordinate can instead absorb an interaction into the limiting definition of that coordinate. Apparent interac- tion order is therefore a property of the retained coordi- nates and descriptive measure, rather than an invariant of the generative mechanism. A recent work shows that fitting a maximum-entropy model while constraining only a selected set of sum- mary statistics is equivalent to projecting the empiri- cal sampling distribution onto that restricted family27. Therefore, any higher-order or nonlinear interactions that covaries with the constrained statistics cannot be represented explicitly; instead, it will be absorbed into the low-order parameters. This misattribution— what they call “feature leakage”—would be particularly pro- nounced in sparse, unevenly sampled binary data. When input sampling is sparse and biased, rare input com- binations and biased sampling would cause different nonlinear/higher-order mechanisms to project onto the low-order statistics, so they look like mere shifts in mean firing or pairwise covariance. To isolate this effect, they use a procedure that keeps each neuron’s conditional re- sponse distribution (the probability of each output given a specific input pattern) unchanged and only alter the input-state weights used in the fit, so any change in at- tribution must come from the sampling geometry. An example would be replacing empirical sampling weights with product-marginal or balanced weights. Because the conditional responses are unchanged, it would fol- low that that any change in the fitted attribution must stem from the altered sampling geometry. In their ex- amination of hipocampal recordings, the median frac- tion of explanatory power attributed to first-order terms falls from 0.75 (empirical) to 0.68 (product-marginal) and 0.46 (balanced). One can then conclude that the ap- parent low-order explanatory power depends on which input states were over- or under-sampled rather than the system harboring genuinely low-order generative structure27. Our construction unifies two apparently conflicting ob- servations — that a low-order fit can be predictively ad- equate under the observed state distribution11 and that the same fit can fail to identify the underlying condi- tional mechanism27 — by showing they probe different arguments of the same object. The “direct fraction” is a function of three variables: i) the distribution over input states, ii) the measurement map that defines the observed coordinates, and iii) the model family used for fitting. One analysis computes the direct fraction at the empiri- cal value of all three (distribution, observed coordinates, and chosen model), concluding predictive adequacy un- der those conditions11.
