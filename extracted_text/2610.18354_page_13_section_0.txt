[8], we first define a descriptive method that characterises signal and noise levels across games. Fix a game G and let S denote its set of models under evaluation, with j, k ∈S ranging over the pair of models compared in G. For a model s ∈S, let τ = 1, . . . , N index its seeds, where a seed is a single independent evaluation run. Let vG,s,τ be the bounds-normalised score on seed τ of G played by model s. Let vG,s denote the mean of vG,s,τ over all N seeds, and let vG denote the mean of vG,s over all models s ∈S. Noise, signal, and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) are then noise(G, s) = Stdτ(vG,s,τ) vG,s , signal(G) = maxj,k∈S |vG,j −vG,k| vG , SNR(G) = signal(G) noise(G) . (3) Here Stdτ(·) denotes the standard deviation taken over the seed index τ, and noise(G) denotes noise(G, s) averaged over every model s ∈S. Noise is a coefficient of variation, a model’s own seed-to-seed spread relative to its mean. Signal is the largest gap between any two models’ means, relative to the game’s overall mean. SNR is high when models are well separated relative to how much a single model’s score jitters across seeds, and low when the two quantities are comparable. B.2 Number of Seeds Given the SNR, we want to determine the minimum number of seeds N that lets us report statistically significant outcomes. This is a trade-off between cost (the number of model inferences we run) and reliability (our statistical confidence in the reported results). Decision accuracy. Heineman et al. [8] suggests calculating a decision accuracy metric to determine the number of seeds N needed to resolve the pairwise gaps between models. Let P denote the set of model pairs compared in G, let n denote a candidate (small) batch size, and let N ≫n denote a large reference batch size. Let v(n) G,s denote model s’s mean bounds-normalised score over n seeds.
