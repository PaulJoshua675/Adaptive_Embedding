The sampling rule also has a useful marginal interpretation. Define Gp(n; τ) := Up(n + 1; τ) −Up(n; τ), n < K. These marginal gains are nonincreasing in n. To see this, write Zi := qp(Yw,i), Rn := max{tp −τ, Z1, . . . , Zn}. Then Gp(n; τ) = E[(Zn+1 −Rn)+ | P = p]. Since Rn+1 ≥Rn and a fresh weak response has the same distribution at every step, the expected improvement from one additional sample cannot increase with n. Thus (18) can equivalently be viewed as increasing the planned number of weak samples while the expected marginal gain exceeds β, and stopping once it falls below β. Claim 4. Suitable prices recover the constrained optimum. Let A(σ, ϕ, π) denote expected accuracy, and define W(σ) := E[σ(P)], S(σ, π) := E[π(Hσ)]. For prices β, τ ≥0, consider the priced objective A(σ, ϕ, π) −βW(σ) −τS(σ, π). Claims 1–3 characterize a policy that maximizes this objective. Under the standard randomized-policy formulation with finite K, the set of achievable triples (W, S, A) is convex and compact. Therefore a supporting-hyperplane argument gives prices β, τ ≥0 and boundary randomization, when necessary, satisfying (16) and (19). It remains only to verify that these conditions imply constrained optimality. Let (σ∗, ϕ∗, π∗) be the resulting policy, and let (σ, ϕ, π) be any other feasible policy. Since the former maximizes the priced objective, A(σ, ϕ, π) ≤A(σ∗, ϕ∗, π∗) −βW(σ∗) −τS(σ∗, π∗) + βW(σ) + τS(σ, π) ≤A(σ∗, ϕ∗, π∗) + β(Bw −W(σ∗)) + τ(Bs −S(σ∗, π∗)) = A(σ∗, ϕ∗, π∗), where the last equality follows from complementary slackness. Hence the stated sampling, selection, and routing policies are jointly optimal. 29
