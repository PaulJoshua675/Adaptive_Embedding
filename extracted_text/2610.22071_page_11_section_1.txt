Byte and hierarchical models. Models operating below the subword level differ in both representation and alloca- tion of computation. ByT5 uses bytes in a text-to-text trans- former (Xue et al., 2022), CANINE downsamples character inputs (Clark et al., 2022), and MambaByte uses a selective state-space model (Wang et al., 2024). MEGABYTE sepa- rates local and global computation with fixed patches (Yu et al., 2023); SpaceByte places global computation at “space- like” positions such as UTF-8 leading bytes, whitespace, and punctuation (Slagle, 2024); BLT selects patches using entropy (Pagnoni et al., 2025). Learned segmentation takes several forms: dynamic token pooling learns boundaries or supervises them with subword or entropy targets (Nawrot et al., 2023), MrT5 learns deletion (Kallini et al., 2025), and H-Net learns chunking (Hwang et al., 2026); AU-Net instead builds an autoregressive hierarchy over rule-based word splits (Videau et al., 2025). MAGNET routes each byte sequence through a boundary predictor for its script, with per-script compression targets chosen to make segmen- tation granularity similar across scripts (Ahia et al., 2024). FLEXITOKENS allows a range of boundary rates during adaptation (Owodunni et al., 2026a), while Fast BLT and dynamic multi-byte prediction target decoding efficiency (Kallini et al., 2026; Owodunni et al., 2026b). These ar- chitectures treat compression and boundary placement as explicit design choices. Our analysis concerns the infor- mation that an encoding exposes to the boundary predictor, particularly when supervision is inherited from a subword model. The distinctions we study, such as a dependent mark versus a letter after the same base, arise within one script and usually within one 256-codepoint page, so routing by script, as in MAGNET, does not supply them. The study of entropy-based patch allocation at matched patch counts by Vera Zúñiga (2026) addresses a complementary question, and SOMBRERO scores boundaries by how strongly chunk starts concentrate on bytes that are hard to predict (Neite- meier et al., 2026); our audit uses grapheme integrity as the boundary criterion. Transfer and encoding. Bolmo transfers a pretrained model into a byte architecture through distillation (Minix- hofer et al., 2025), whereas zero-shot tokenizer transfer predicts embeddings for a replacement tokenizer (Minix- hofer et al., 2024). H-Net similarly distills Llama 3.2 3B into a byte-level model around its frozen backbone, super- vising the routing module with the parent’s token starts and noting that these boundaries cannot always be predicted online (Hwang et al., 2026, Appendix E.2). Inference-time methods instead expose a tokenized model as a byte- or character-level distribution without training (Phan et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2025a; Hayase et al., 2026); byteifica- tion trains local modules and a boundary predictor around the parent transformer. Concurrent work adapts H-Net- style dynamic byte chunking to a frozen subword model, pulling chunk embeddings toward averaged subword em- beddings and adding part-of-speech tagging supervision to guide word-level boundaries (Kumar et al., 2026). Vocab- ulary expansion based on hidden-state similarity can con- solidate multi-token characters in a frozen model (Churchill & Skiena, 2026). Closer to our boundary question, Haltiuk (2026) reports nearly inherited Bolmo segmentation, pro- poses capability-preserving boundary transfer, and asks which shared alphabet to use. We give cluster-aligned tar- get constructions and analyze their predictability at limited lookahead. Alternative encodings provide another way to change the representation presented to a tokenizer or model.
