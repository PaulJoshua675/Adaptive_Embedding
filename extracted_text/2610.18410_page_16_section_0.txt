A Graph Construction and Evaluation Dataset A.1 Graph Construction The corpus is a fully synthetic professional network of fictional candidates, organisations, resumes, employer rosters, and textual evidence. Generation is deterministic under a fixed seed and does not involve a language model: every fact is sampled from predefined source pools and then rendered into text by fixed templates. Because the graph is built before any question exists, no answer information can enter its construction. Candidate identity. A candidate’s name is a first–last pair drawn from two flat pools of 512 and 458 entries. A used_names set rejects any repeat and the draw is retried, so every person in the dataset has a distinct full name before the identity-collision checks performed at ingest. With roughly 2.3 × 105 available combinations and a few hundred candidates per graph, rejection is rare. Uniqueness is what allows the human-readable entity:person:<Name> identifier to serve as the canonical key: two candidates sharing a name would otherwise collapse into a single node and silently merge their evidence. What is generated. Each candidate is assigned a primary sector sampled uniformly from eleven, which governs certifications and supplies the default source for employer and role attributes. A candidate receives one or two education records, between one and four jobs, zero to two projects per job, zero to three certifications, and zero to two endorsements. Education degrees are sampled with weights 50 % Bachelor’s, 35 % Master’s, and 15 % PhD, and universities come from a global pool that is not sector-scoped. Project descriptions reuse a subset of the skills attached to their job, so project evidence is correlated with the job rather than sampled independently. Controlled cross-sector bridges. With probability 0.2 a job’s role attributes—title, skills, responsibilities, tenure, salary band, team size, and project name—are drawn from a different sector, selected through a fixed affinity map, while the employer remains tied to the candidate’s primary sector. This prevents the graph from collapsing into isolated sector-specific clusters and produces known bridges between otherwise sector-oriented regions, such as technology roles inside healthcare organisations.
