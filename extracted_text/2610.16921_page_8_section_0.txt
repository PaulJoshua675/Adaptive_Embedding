Under the TSE-RL setting, the same pretrained backbone is split into the TSE-RL agent. The agent learns a policy to perform time-window optimization by selectively discarding or retaining windows and marking them as Onset, Sustain, or Offset. The agent then aggregates the probabilities of retained windows for classification. All exper- iments are independently repeated ten times, and performance is reported as mean accuracy and standard deviation across the ten runs. Statistical significance of performance improvement between TSE-RL and its corresponding Baseline-full counter- part is assessed using a paired test over the ten runs. Hyperparameters. Unless otherwise stated, we use shared hyperparameters across datasets and backbones. The history encoder G operates on the most recent k = 5 windows and is implemented as a standard Transformer encoder with one layer, four attention heads, hidden size dh = 128, dropout rate 0.1. The encoded sequence is temporally mean-pooled and linearly projected to obtain a vector hn ∈Rdh, dh = 64. The FSM-constrained policy network receives the concatenated vector zn = [fn; hn; ρn] and is realized as an actor–critic multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 64 units. The reward and discount factor follow Section II-A. Training follows the three-stage scheme. In supervised pre- training, each backbone B is trained end-to-end on full win- dows using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 10−3, batch size 64, and 300 epochs. After convergence, B is split into F and D, and their parameters are frozen. In policy learning, only the history encoder G and the FSM-constrained policy network are optimized by PPO with learning rate 3 × 10−4, PPO clipping parameter 0.2, generalized advantage estimation parameter λ = 0.95, and a total of 4×104 environment steps. In joint fine-tuning, F are unfrozen and updated together with G and the policy network using a smaller learning rate of 3 × 10−5 for 2 × 104 additional environment steps. Configuration. Experiments were implemented in Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.3.1 and ran on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA TITAN V GPU and an Intel Xeon Gold 6230 CPU (2.10 GHz). C. Within-subject cross-session classification in simulated online experiment Tables II and III report within-subject cross-session accu- racies. In all four datasets, TSE-RL consistently improves the performance of the Baseline-full models for most back- bones, with some gains being statistically significant. On BNCI2014001 (Table II), TSE-RL yields improvements for EEGNet, FBCNet, ATCNet, M-ShallowConvNet, and CSPNet, with average accuracies increasing by about 2–3 percentage points. Only TSLANet exhibits a slight degradation, and on this dataset it has both the lowest accuracy and the largest standard deviation among the eight backbones. Since the re- wards are computed from the window-level confidences, such a backbone provides a noisy learning signal for the policy. On BNCI2014002, BNCI2015001 and OpenBMI (Table III), TSE-RL improves every backbone, including TSLANet, and the benefit persists on the much larger OpenBMI dataset. D. Cross-subject classification in simulated online experiment Table IV summarizes cross-subject performance on BNCI2014001. Strong cross-subject variability makes this set- ting considerably more challenging, and Baseline-full models drop substantially relative to the within-subject case. Nev- ertheless, TSE-RL still improves average accuracy for most backbones, with statistically significant gains for EEGNet, FBCNet, ATCNet, mAtt, and CSPNet. TSE-RL is therefore compatible with a wide range of MI backbones across both cross-session and cross-subject decoding.
