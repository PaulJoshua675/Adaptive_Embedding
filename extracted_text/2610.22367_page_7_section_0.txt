In a naïve reversible implementa- tion, coherent control and cleanup can introduce enough overhead to obscure the advantages of an efficient classical method. Our Berlekamp–Massey and divstep construc- tions show how jointly choosing the algorithm, represen- tation, and uncomputation can recover those advantages. These results suggest that some apparent costs of quan- tum arithmetic reflect limitations of existing reversible constructions rather than of the algorithms themselves. Finding such constructions requires reasoning about choices whose benefits depend on one another. An arith- metic saving may become useful only after the associated control and uncomputation costs have also been reduced. A candidate design must therefore be carried through sev- eral unresolved mathematical and implementation prob- lems before its resource advantage becomes apparent. The framework is bounded by the choices that define each task. One is the objective that ranks circuits: we use the TQ-cost for a uniform comparison, but other ob- jectives are equally reasonable. In practice, the relative importance of qubit count, depth, routing, and measure- ments depends on details such as hardware constraints, decoder performance, and error-correction architecture. The scoring function can be replaced with a hardware- appropriate combination of these resources, but deter- mining that objective remains a matter of application and hardware judgment.
