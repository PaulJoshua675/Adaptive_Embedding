Multilingual dense retrievers map queries and doc- uments from many languages into one vector space, so that relevance rather than language decides what is retrieved (Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). The intent is met unevenly. The same model re- trieves well for some query languages and fails badly for others, as documents in the query’s own language, or in English, fill the top ranks and push relevant foreign-language documents out (Yang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Amiraz et al., 2025). What separates the languages that fail from those that succeed is not visible in their scores. Scoring a language against gold labels shows how often it retrieves the right documents, but not why it misses them. To see why, we ask the same question in several languages and compare what each retrieves. The retrieved sets diverge, and how they diverge is diagnostic. We call this cross-lingual disagreement and make it our object of study. It requires no relevance labels and char- acterizes failed language by its departure from suc- cessful languages rather than by a single number. Disagreement is diagnostic because it separates two explanations that a single score cannot. A language may fail because the model represents it poorly, or because the languages interfere only once they share an index. The two call for dif- ferent fixes, better training in the first case, a ge- ometric correction in the second, but from gold scores alone, they look identical. Prior work does not separate them: monolingual and cross-lingual retrieval are typically measured on different col- lections, confounding the effect of mixing with a change of items, while audits quantify how unequal the mixed ranking is (Wang et al., 2026), and mit- igations act on the output space, transforming the index (Jang et al., 2026), removing language iden- tity post hoc (Kim et al., 2026), projecting out a language subspace (Xie et al., 2022), or correct- ing hubness (Lample et al., 2018; Sakhawat et al., 2026). Each presupposes which explanation holds rather than testing it. Disagreement can be read at three points, each answering a different question. Among languages retrieving only within their own documents, it asks whether they behave alike in isolation. They do: every language retrieves competently and orders concepts much as the others do, so representational quality is not the binding constraint.
