Multi-agent LLM collaboration. A growing body of work organizes LLMs into groups that debate, critique, and vote, reporting gains in rea- soning and factuality over single models (Du et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024). These systems rest on a cooperative premise: more agents and more rounds surface and filter errors. Even without an adversary, discussion can erode correct answers through conformity and the loss of shared facts (Zhang et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2026). We study the adversarial case, in which the same channel is used to inject a false claim. Adversarial influence and misinformation in agent groups. Persuasive adversarial debaters lower collaborative accuracy (Amayuelas et al., 2024; Kraidia et al., 2026), persuasion can override truth in debate (Agarwal and Khanna, 2025; Wynn et al., 2025), and the damage has been related to the number and share of deceivers in a group (Wu et al., 2026). Misinformation spreads further in densely connected agent networks (Yu et al., 2025), persists once it enters agent communities (Ju et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026), and can be induced by contriving agents that post only true fragments (Hu et al., 2026). Our attacker is deliber- ately simple: it edits no weights and only argues for one fixed false claim in the conversation. Our main difference lies in what we measure. These studies mostly evaluate what the group outputs (messages, votes, consensus, or accuracy); to our knowledge, none pairs each agent’s public stance with its pri- vate answer turn by turn, which is what reveals that most infection is never voiced. Public compliance and private acceptance. So- cial psychology separates public compliance from private acceptance (Deutsch and Gerard, 1955; Kelman, 1958). Majorities mainly produce com- pliance, that is, public agreement without pri- vate change (Asch, 1956), whereas minorities shift private judgment more than public behavior (Moscovici, 1980; Wood et al., 1994). LLMs con- cede to users (Sharma et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2023), abandon facts they know under multi-turn persuasion (Xu et al., 2024) and conflicting ev- idence (Xie et al., 2024), and conform to peers (Weng et al., 2025; Soffer et al., 2026; Bito et al., 2026). Studies that do separate private from public answers in LLM groups mostly report the compli- ance pattern: agents agree in the discussion but keep or return to their own view in private (Baltaji et al., 2024; Ghaffarizadeh et al., 2026; YS, 2026), or withhold dissent they privately hold (Mansuri et al., 2026).
