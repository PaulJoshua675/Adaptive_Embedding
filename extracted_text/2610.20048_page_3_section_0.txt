Reading the Neighborhood: Text-Attentive Graph Reranking for Automated Subject Indexing 3 Problem formulation 3.1 Task: subject indexing Subject indexing in libraries is usually done by referring to a con- trolled vocabulary, i.e., a set of established authority descriptors. We use the STW Thesaurus for Economics2, which is developed and maintained in-house at ZBW – Leibniz Information Centre for Economics3. It contains around 6,000 descriptors that are cat- egorized into seven subthesauri and linked by broader/narrower relationships in a polyhierarchy. The thesaurus is updated every year by a board of subject indexing experts and published on the web as linked open data in SKOS. Librarians tasked with subject indexing will identify and assign matching descriptors from the thesaurus to a publication first, and then possibly cover remaining salient aspects with free keywords, which will become candidates for additions to the next edition of the thesaurus later on. While some thesauri contain a number of descriptors in the order of 20,000 or more, the label set size for the thesaurus used in this work contains a little over 6,000 labels. This in combination with the variable number of labels for each record still qualifies our subject indexing use case comfortably as a so-called extreme multi-label text classification (XMLC) task. XMLC is also characterized by the existence of “long-tail“ labels, i.e., labels that do not occur very frequently in the available training data. A few “head“ labels appear on the majority of the documents, but the vast majority are “tail“ labels that might only appear once or twice in the entire training data. Traditional text classification treats labels as independent strings and has no notion of how two labels, e.g., construction cost and real estate, relate to each other. Subject indexing, however, operates on taxonomy/ontology. The labels are not independent; they are deeply interconnected concepts governed by strict semantic rules. 3.2 The thesaurus as a heterogeneous graph We assign descriptors as subjects for the document. These descrip- tors are taken from the STW, which covers economics along with its neighboring business and social-science disciplines. The the- saurus is published as linked open data using the SKOS vocabulary. Each descriptor is a skos:Concept and the relationship between the descriptors is expressed by SKOS semantic properties. Exploit- ing SKOS relationships gives us the ability to treat the labels as a structured graph and not just a flat label list. We represent the thesaurus as a directed edge-typed graph 𝐺= (𝐿, 𝐸), where 𝐿= {ℓ1, . . . , ℓ|𝐿|} is the set of descriptors (labels) and 𝐸is a set of typed edges. Each edge is a triple (ℓ𝑖, ℓ𝑗,𝑟), 𝑟∈R = {bt, nt, rt}, (1) where ℓ𝑖is the source concept, ℓ𝑗the target concept, and 𝑟the relation type connecting them. The triple (ℓ𝑖, ℓ𝑗, bt) denotes that the ℓ𝑗is a more general parent to ℓ𝑖. In other words, it is the hierarchical parent of ℓ𝑖. Similarly, triple (ℓ𝑖, ℓ𝑗, nt) denotes that the ℓ𝑗is a narrower and a specific term to ℓ𝑖. The BT and NT relation types are strict inverses of each other. While the BT/NT edge induces hierarchy in the thesaurus, the related edge represents an associative, otherwise unspecified, semantic relationship between the labels. The three different types of edges make the graph heterogeneous in nature. Beyond relations on the descriptor level, our thesaurus also has a coarser, superordinate classificatory layer. The descriptors are grouped into a set of subject categories, which in turn are organized in seven sub-thesauri. The subject categories are arranged in a hierarchical structure with BT, NT relation edges between them as well and are therefore also part of the graph. Following the broader-term links upward from any descriptor eventually reaches a top-level category. This means every descriptor can be seen to naturally inherit semantic context from its parent category. And since the thesaurus is a poly-hierarchy, any descriptor with an overlapping meaning can belong to more than one subject category and therefore be connected to more than one of the seven sub- thesauri at once. The document text is represented as a 1024-dimensional vector using a single frozen domain-adapted BGE-M3[15][5] model. Similarly, the subject categories are encoded into the same embed- ding space using their preferred labels. For descriptors, the label text consists of the preferred, alternative, and hidden label text. It is concatenated with definition and scope notes whenever avail- able. This enriches the label representation while projecting it onto the embedding space. Because this encoder is trained contrastively so that semantically related texts are close to each other, cosine similarity in this space approximates semantic relatedness for both document–concept and concept–concept pairs. This shared space is what allows a document to be mapped directly against individual candidate descriptors.
