Persistence diagrams. A persistence diagram records birth– death pairs D(s) = {(bi,di)}n i=1, (2) where the persistence |di −bi| measures the scalar interval over which a feature remains present. Points close to the diagonal b = d represent short-lived features, whereas points farther from it rep- resent more persistent structure. PDs compactly preserve paired critical values but not the complete ancestry describing which com- ponents merge into one another, nor where in the domain a feature lies. We compare diagrams with three distances. The bottleneck dis- tance dB(D1,D2) = inf γ sup p∈D1 ∥p−γ(p)∥∞, (3) where γ ranges over matchings between diagram points, including matches to the diagonal, is controlled by the single largest discrep- ancy under an optimal matching. We report both W2,∞(L∞ground norm, matching the bottleneck ge- ometry) and W2,2 (Euclidean ground norm); the two differ only in the ground norm, not in the outer order. Because “W2” is used for both conventions in the literature, we always write both indices. Because dB measures the worst-case mismatch and W2,q the ac- cumulated mismatch, they can rank reconstructions differently: a field that matches the bulk of the diagram well but misplaces one prominent feature can have a low Wasserstein and a high bottleneck distance, and vice versa. Merge trees. A merge tree retains the connected-component evolution together with the hierarchy of merge events. Leaves rep- resent extrema at which components are created, internal nodes represent saddles at which components merge, and edges encode scalar intervals between events. Consequently, an MT can distin- guish fields that have similar birth–death pairs but different ances- try, branch order, or connectivity [36, 25, 24]. Recent work emphasizes that descriptor choice and distance choice both affect scalar-field comparison. Wasserstein formula- tions support merge-tree geodesics, barycenters, and representa- tion learning [24, 23, 33]; complementary approaches study stable, geometry-aware, branch-independent, and deformation-based com- parisons [4, 35, 32, 31]. These developments motivate treating PD and MT evaluations, and different distances on the same descriptor, as related but distinct evidence rather than interchangeable notions of a single “topological quality.” 2.3 Topology in neural learning Persistent topology has been incorporated into neural models through differentiable layers, regularizers, and task-specific losses. Br¨uel-Gabrielsson et al. [5] demonstrate differentiation through persistence for reconstruction and generative-model regularization, while Hu et al. [16] and Clough et al. [7] use differentiable topo- logical losses to reduce connectivity or shape errors in image and volume segmentation. Moor et al. [22] preserve multiscale connec- tivity in autoencoder latent spaces, and Kazeminia et al. [17] use a topological inductive bias to improve multiple-instance learning under data-scarce conditions. Explicit optimization has also been investigated for scalar-data simplification [18], and efficient persis- tence computation is an active topic in scientific visualization [11]. Learned representations have been proposed for efficient merge- tree comparison; Qin et al. [26] approximate MT similarity with graph neural networks and topological attention. The critical-value component of our fixed-pair supervision is closely related to the topology-aware interpolation framework of Kissi et al. [19]. Their training proceeds in two phases. The first combines reconstruction and gradient losses with a critical-value loss that constrains predicted values at the birth and death vertices of the target persistence pairs identified by TTK. The second keeps the reconstruction and critical-value terms, drops the gradient term, and adds a Wasserstein-type persistence-diagram loss: at each itera- tion the prediction’s diagram is recomputed with TTK and matched to the target diagram, prediction pairs without a counterpart are pushed toward the diagonal, and the matched birth–death cost is differentiated with respect to the predicted values at the prediction’s critical vertices. Our critical-value term LCV closely follows their first-phase constraint. We differ in three respects. First, we add a persistence term Lpers that directly constrains the scalar contrast between the two endpoints of each supervised pair. Second, we do not recompute or re-match the prediction’s persistence diagram during training: supervision is confined to fixed ground-truth end- point locations and, unlike their second-phase loss, cannot remove spurious pairs that the prediction creates elsewhere. Third, we fine- tune a pretrained wind-field SR network rather than train a time- conditioned scalar-field decoder. The PD and MT distances we re- port are therefore evaluation measures that our training procedure never computes. A related line of work preserves topological and other features during lossy compression, for scalar fields [34], vector fields [20], and as a general augmentation of existing compressors [10]. These methods share our motivation of retaining analysis-relevant struc- ture under an information-reducing transformation, but the prob- lems differ: a compressor has the original high-resolution field at encoding time and can enforce guarantees on the decoded field, whereas SR receives only a coarse observation and must infer unre- solved structure. We therefore treat topology-preserving compres- sion as methodological context rather than a directly comparable baseline. 3 DATA AND EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 3.1 Data source and PhIRE-compatible preprocessing Wind fields are drawn from the NREL WIND Toolkit national grid- ded dataset (NREL, now the National Laboratory of the Rock- ies, NLR) and accessed through the Highly Scalable Data Ser- vice (HSDS). The upstream variables are 100 m wind speed and wind direction, which are converted to horizontal vector compo- nents [u,v] using the standard meteorological convention. We use a Mid-Atlantic spatial crop centered near 39.5◦N, 75.0◦W and fol- low the PhIRE wind-data organization in which raw high-resolution vector fields are downsampled to form paired lower-resolution in- puts and higher-resolution targets.
