1.1 Performance Gains in Interacting Dyads Bahrami et al. (2010) provide us with a paradigm and experimental results to test our model of collective performance gains. They showed pairs of participants sets of Gabor patches (si- nusoidal grating patterns with a Gaussian blur) in two consecutive intervals. Participants were tasked with identifying which interval, the first or the second, contained a contrast oddball. Individual responses were collected on each trial. When responses differed, participants con- ferred with each other to reach a joint decision. Individual and joint decisions across a range of contrast differences were fit with psychometric curves representing the perceptual sensitivity of each member and the dyad. The study showed that some dyads’ discriminability exceeded the perceptual sensitivity of their best member. Furthermore, the authors showed that this dyadic advantage was modu- lated by differences in individual member discriminability: Dyads composed of members with approximately similar perceptual sensitivities exhibited the greatest dyadic gain, and this gain diminished as the disparity between individual ability increased. The authors argued that this inverse linear relationship is consistent with the hypothesis that dyads communicated their level of confidence in their guesses to produce joint decisions (Bang et al., 2014; Fusaroli et al., 2012). There is an open question about the process by which dyads shared their confidences during joint decision-making. On the one hand, communication may only serve to integrate guesses after individual decision-making has terminated. In this case, the dyadic advantage would reflect the same baseline statistical facilitation also leveraged by wisdom of crowds. On the other hand, individual decision-making may continue during communication, and interaction between the decision processes themselves may produce an emergent benefit beyond what is attained by statistical facilitation. These two theoretical alternatives are illustrated in Figure 1b. In the present study, we investigate these alternatives by modeling dyads performing a classification task and testing if interaction or aggregation better accounts for the Bahrami et al. results. The question is whether or not interaction may benefit collective intelligence by enabling group performance gains above those that are explainable by statistical facilitation. 1.2 Computational Efficiency in Interacting Collectives In addition to performance gains, interaction may also benefit collective intelligence by enabling groups to accomplish a task more efficiently. For example, recent findings showed that the force output of weaver ants increased superlinearly with group size such that force per ant nearly doubled when they coordinated as groups to pull and fold leaves into nests (Stewardson et al., 2025). Alternatively, in humans, "social loafing," where effort per individual decreases with increasing group size, may grant collective efficiency if group performance is maintained or even increases despite the drop in individual effort. This is exactly what was found in a simulated real-world crisis mapping scenario: As group size increased, collective performance increased, effort per individual decreased, and collaboration across members increased, suggesting that interaction can distribute effort across human teams (Mao et al., 2016). At the same time, while some studies show that the cost of interaction, as measured by response time, can outweigh benefits to accuracy (Almaatouq et al., 2024; Straub et al., 2023), other studies show that interaction can help groups respond more quickly and more accurately. For instance, one study found that group interaction led to more accurate decisions in monetary policymaking compared to individual decisions without an increase in response time (Blinder & Morgan, 2005).
