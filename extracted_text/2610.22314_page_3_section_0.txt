Patient-Ψ (Wang et al., 2024) conditions generation on a cognitive model based on Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) principles, an approach several other works have built upon (Qiu et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2026). Nevertheless, a recurring finding is that unconstrained patient LMs are too insightful and too cooperative, adopting the therapist’s framing without pushback or even proposing coping strategies or rational interpretations (Naous et al., 2026; Hoang et al., 2026). Post-training these models (Liu et al., 2026) or prescribing a high level trajectory (Qiu et al., 2026) are possible solutions to mitigate these behaviours, but the former needs conversational data, while in the latter a trajectory fixed in advance cannot dynamically respond to the therapist. Our simulator brings these ideas together: each patient is grounded in a detailed profile, and behaviour is steered by moves (e.g., disclosing, deflecting or disagreeing) inspired by dialogue-act design and also applied to counsellor LMs (Baldo et al., 2026). Mental Health Benchmarks. Existing evaluations fall into (1) knowledge benchmarks; and (2) conver- sational benchmarks, of which some are single response benchmarks, and other multi-turn interactive benchmarks. Knowledge benchmarks test clinical facts and reasoning through multiple-choice items or short form responses, often derived from licensing examinations (Zhang et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025). Single-response benchmarks elicit one reply to a fixed prior context and score it, either with automatic rubrics or with panels of clinicians (Malik et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). Because the context is fixed, these benchmarks do not measure how a generated reply affects subsequent patient responses and counsellor behaviour. Interactive benchmarks mitigate this limitation by having a simulated patient converse with the model under test, with the resulting transcript being scored (Lee et al., 2024; Pombal et al., 2025; Bentley et al., 2026; Weilnhammer et al., 2026). However, to our knowledge, none of these frameworks evaluate a model across multiple sessions with the same patient. MindEval2 extends interactive eval- uation to this longitudinal setting and, rather than scoring the transcript as a whole, annotates every counsellor turn with the issues it exhibits and their severity, similar to MQM for machine translation (Lommel et al., 2014). 3 MHQR: Mental Health Quality Rubrics LLM judges in open-ended settings usually provide a single holistic score (Zheng et al., 2023), which makes them hard to interpret and act upon (Ye et al., 2024). Consequently, this has led to a shift in the evaluation of open ended generation tasks to rubric-based taxonomies (Arora et al., 2025; Goel et al., 2025; Akyürek et al., 2026; He et al., 2026b; Shi et al., 2026). Rubrics decompose response quality into explicit criteria which can be assessed individually, making judgements interpretable and actionable. We follow the same paradigm in mental health by introducing Mental Health Quality Rubrics (MHQR), a rubric-based taxonomy for evaluating counsellor LM responses in everyday, non-crisis mental health support conversations developed by our clinical team. MHQR evaluates a single counsellor LM turn in the context of the preceding conversation (Figure 17) and consists of three main components: (i) a taxon- omy of 14 clinical issues, (ii) a turn-level severity scale, and (iii) a scoring model to compute aggregated quality scores. 3.1 Clinical Issues The core of MHQR is a taxonomy of 14 clinical issues summarized in Table 1. Our annotation guide- lines, together with complete definitions of all clinical issues, can be found in Appendix A. Each issue is defined as a rubric criterion describing a specific failure mode of a counsellor LM turn. The issues are grouped into six top-level categories: Distortion Reinforcement, Factual & Contextual Fidelity, Guidance Calibration, Boundaries, Delivery, and a catch-all category for clearly broken responses. Framing evaluation as structured error annotation rather than a direct assessment has also led to consid- erable improvements in machine translation2 by introducing multi-dimensional quality metrics (MQM) (Lommel et al., 2014). Large-scale studies found that structured MQM annotations by professional lin- guists are markedly more reliable and more discriminating than the holistic crowd ratings used previ- ously (Freitag et al., 2021).
