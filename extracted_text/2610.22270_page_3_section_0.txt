Because YOLO26 does not distinguish between bus subtypes, the dataset includes full-size buses, microbuses, and vans. III. MATERIALS AND METHODS A. Dataset The dataset was constructed from approximately 1,460 hours of continuous video recorded during June and July 2026. Recordings were acquired with a single fixed camera mounted at a height of 5 m and positioned 30 m from Circuito Interior, one of the main arterial roads of Mexico City. The camera was oriented toward the roadway, providing a consistent view of passing traffic, including public transport buses operating on multiple routes. 1) Bus Detection: Given the large volume of recorded video, manual annotation of the full videos was impractical. The videos were therefore preprocessed using the YOLO26 object detector [10] combined with a multi-object tracker. Only detections belonging to the bus class were retained, and a single image was stored for each tracked vehicle to avoid redundant samples of the same bus. To prevent truncation of the vehicle body, each bounding box was expanded by 10% before cropping. This process yielded a total of 7,972 cropped images of public transport vehicles. Because the detector does not distinguish between subtypes, this set includes buses, microbuses, and vans, all of which were classified as bus by YOLO26. Fig. 2 shows examples of the resulting images. 2) Ground-Truth Annotation: Although the proposed clus- tering approach is fully unsupervised, ground-truth labels were required to evaluate its performance. To this end, all 7,972 images were manually annotated. These labels were used exclusively for evaluation and were not involved at any stage of feature extraction or clustering. Two hierarchical binary labels were assigned to each image: is bus and is RTP bus, where the latter is a subset of the former. The is bus label indicates whether the crop contains a full-size bus and was introduced to assess the precision of the YOLO26- based preprocessing stage, given that smaller vehicles such as microbuses and vans were also detected under the bus class. The is RTP bus label identifies vehicles belonging to the Red de Transporte de Pasajeros (RTP), Mexico City’s public bus network, which constitute the target class of this study. B. Bus Clustering The objective of this stage is to group the detected vehi- cles into clusters that correspond to semantically meaningful categories, such as bus routes and vehicle types, without relying on labeled data. The proposed pipeline consists of three steps: (i) extraction of complementary morphological and color descriptors from each image, (ii) early fusion of both descriptors into a single weighted feature vector, and (iii) unsupervised clustering of the fused representations. The following subsections describe each step in detail. 1) Feature Extraction: Buses operating on different routes often share similar body structures but differ in their paint schemes, whereas vehicles of different types (e.g., full-size buses, microbuses, and vans) may exhibit similar colors while differing substantially in shape. To capture both sources of variation, two complementary types of features were extracted from each image: deep morphological features and handcrafted color features. Morphological features: These features were extracted us- ing DINOv2 [7], a family of Vision Transformer (ViT) [11] models trained through self-supervised learning. Unlike text- guided approaches such as CLIP [12], which depend on paired image and caption data, DINOv2 learns visual representations from images alone. Its training objective combines an image- level self-distillation loss, computed on the class token be- tween a student and a teacher network observing different crops of the same image, with a patch-level masked image modeling loss inherited from iBOT [13]. A key property of DINOv2 is that its features perform well as frozen, general-purpose descriptors, without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. Its authors report strong results with simple linear classifiers and nearest-neighbor retrieval across a wide range of benchmarks, including fine-grained vehicle recognition on Stanford Cars [7]. These properties make DINOv2 well suited to an unsupervised setting such as ours, in which no labeled data are available for fine-tuning and the cluster structure depends directly on the geometry of the embedding space. In this work, we employed the publicly available facebook/dinov2-small checkpoint, which corresponds to the ViT-S/14 variant of DINOv2 distilled from the ViT-g/14 teacher.
