Following these findings, a suite of selectors is run against fixed but unknown models (i.e. opponent is a fixed LLM) to evaluate their potential as strategic selectors. Finally, these selectors are deployed against adaptive opponents to simulate the non-stationary dynamics of real-world settings. We note that reported results should not be compared across (Tables 2 to 4), as a method’s performance is a function of the specific opponent pool it faced which varies across settings (e.g. the runs against adaptive opponents do not include fixed models). 4.1 Design Full details on the models, games, and selector implementations are given in Appendix C. Models and Games. The model portfolio draws on 10 open-weight LLMs across four families (Granite, Mistral, Llama, and Qwen) ranging from 3B to 70B to capture varying capability levels, usage costs, and dispositions. The game inventory captures 5 classical game-theoretic scenarios (Colonel Blotto, Cournot Duopoly, Stag Hunt, Vickrey Auction, Chicken Game) spanning zero-sum to cooperative dilemmas. Experiments are run through OutplayArena2, which owns the base game rules, turn sequencing, and scoring. Selectors. A suite of selectors spanning traditional and adversarial bandit samplers [2, 29], con- temporary LLM routers [5, 26], and an LLM self-reflection framework [21] is then incorporated to draw on this portfolio. Adapting selectors from a range of different backgrounds plays an important diagnostic role in understanding the strengths and weaknesses of each method as strategic selectors. Additionally, simple opponent-aware full-information selectors are evaluated post-hoc to measure the selection headroom. Number of Rounds and Seeds. Some strategic benchmarks [28] evaluate models across hundreds of games. To test whether such broad evaluations rest on a consistent signal, we quantify the per- game signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and find that the evaluation signal is inherently game-specific. A power analysis on single-round fixed-model matchups shows that the share of detectable pairwise comparisons stabilises at N ≈150 seeds in every evaluated game, with Cournot Duopoly separating models more cleanly than any other game (Appendix B.3). In our experiments, fixed-model matchups are single-round T = 1 with N = 300 seeds. Selectors learn online and require repeated play, so we run T = 300 rounds per seed with a single seed (N = 1) to keep the inference cost tractable. Note that the fielded model’s and fixed opponent’s context is limited to the current round. This ensures that each round is an independent draw of the opponent’s play. 4.2 Results Strategic Capabilities Vary Across Models and Games. Table 2 reveals that strategic competence is uneven and game dependent. For example, the Llama models are top performers in Cournot Duopoly but fail to make the podium in Chicken Game. In addition, the substantial performance variance among models within individual games highlights an opportunity to adopt a selector. Figure 2a underscores that model scale does not reliably translate into strategic capability. Across our evaluations, we find only a weak positive correlation between model scale and utility (Pearson’s r = 0.37), and a moderate negative relationship when accounting for the usage costs (r = −0.54). Upon a closer look, we also see that smaller models have game-specific usability. For example in Vickrey Auction, due to its high rationality (i.e. always bidding the true value), the Granite4.1 3B outperforms other much bigger models that range from 24B to 70B parameters. Finally, Figure 2b supports our framing “choosing a model is choosing a strategy”. For each model, we score strategic dispositions from its in-game actions, such as the dare rate in Chicken Game or the produced quantity in Cournot Duopoly. We describe each model by a vector of its seven disposition scores, and project these vectors onto their principal components (Appendix D.1). We find that even same family models can differ sharply in disposition. For example, Granite4.1 8B dares in 96% of Chicken Game rounds while its 3B and 30B versions never dare. Families still show a general coherence, as Qwen3.5 and Llama models compete aggressively in Cournot Duopoly, whereas most Granite and Mistral models cooperate.
