This changes both the ac- cessed rows and their consumer, providing a bound- ary test for the results on hashed memories. Sensitive rows. Most PLE rows have per-row kurtosis 3.3–3.5, moderately above the Gaussian value of 3. The BOS row and the rows of about 100 frequent tokens have heavy tails. These rows are read in a few sensitive layers, and they dominate the loss. At 2.0 bpp without rotation, QTIP gives a worst-domain KL of .106 on E2B and .162 on E4B (Table 26). Keeping the BOS row and the top-100 rows in bf16 costs 0.006 bpp and lowers these to .027 and .028. This gives similar worst-domain KL to QTIP with rotation (.033 and .029). On E2B-it, rotation is better (.126 against .140). These token- indexed tables therefore require a different treat- ment of sensitive rows from the uniform, unrotated representation in Section 3. In the tested hashed ta- bles, protecting sequence-start rows changes aggre- gate KL by at most 1% on DeepSeek and LongCat (Table 20 in Appendix E.3). Predicting model sensitivity. Table statistics alone did not predict this effect. Before measur- ing E4B-it, we fixed a prediction of the rotation KL ratio from per-layer kurtosis. The prediction was .21, and the measurement was .877. The table-side part was right: the MSE ratio without and with ro- tation was 1.74 predicted and 1.78 measured. The step from MSE to KL failed. When only layer 10 is quantized without rotation, KL is .121 on E4B and .004 on E4B-it. The two layers have similar statistics, so the gap lies in model sensitivity. We found this explanation after the prediction missed, so it is post hoc. A second prediction, also fixed be- fore the measurement, gave the correct decision for both gemma-3n models and the correct magnitude for neither. Layer-level norm adjustment. On PLE, a layer- mean norm adjustment on top of QTIP’s global scale changes worst-domain KL in both directions. It raises it by 6.3% and 9.0% on E2B-it and by 6.0% on E4B without rotation.
