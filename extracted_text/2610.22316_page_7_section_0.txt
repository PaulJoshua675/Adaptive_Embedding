RQ1: Can a steering agent improve task per- formance, and can reinforcement learning strengthen its steering intelligence? RQ2: Does the learned steering capa- bility generalize to out-of-domain tasks, transfer to unseen executors, and support controllable intervention frequency? RQ3: Can steering improve a multi-agent system, where goal drift arises from collaboration among specialized roles? 4.1 Experimental Setup We follow the experimental setting of ASearcher (Gao et al., 2025), which trains on ASearcher-Base-35k and evaluates on seven retrieval-augmented QA benchmarks of multi-hop reasoning and single-hop retrieval with a local Wikipedia retrieval server. We report F1 (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) following the same protocol, with Avg. denoting the mean over the seven benchmarks. For the executor, we use a Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct search agent (Qwen et al., 2024), which shows basic search ability but is prone to goal drift. Given the difficulty of the steering task, we use the stronger Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) as the backbone of the steering agent. Further details are described in Appendix A. 4.2 Effectiveness of Steering (RQ1) To study the effectiveness of the steering agent, we compare it with planning and verification, which use the same supervisor backbone (Qwen3-8B) to decompose the goal before execution and to review the goal completion afterward. Table 1 reports F1 on the seven in-domain benchmarks, together with the corresponding steering cost and execution cost, i.e., steering actions (#Act.) and executor turns (Turns) per task. We adjust the fast path threshold (Equation 4) to keep the average number of steering actions around one per task for a fair comparison. Zero-shot steering brings the largest gain among the three supervision forms and leaves executor turns nearly un- changed. In comparison, zero-shot planning and verification help less or even degrade performance and lengthen execution. These results show that intervening during execution supervises the executor more effectively than plan- ning ahead or reviewing afterward. To examine the improvement brought by reinforcement learning, we apply the same learning algorithm (Section 3.3) to the steering, planning, and verification agents. RL improves all three supervision forms over their zero-shot coun- terparts. Among them, steering benefits the most. RL raises the gain from 1.84 to 6.66 points and reaches the strongest results consistently on all seven benchmarks, with the fewest steering actions and executor turns. These results show that reinforcement learning effectively unlocks the steering intelligence, enabling the steering agent to mitigate goal drift. Regarding the learning algorithm, we additionally compare online reinforcement learning with an offline alternative, where filtered behavior cloning (BC) (Chen et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 2023) imitates the sampled steering actions that improve the task outcome. Filtered BC falls behind our online algorithm, further validating the necessity of online reinforcement learning. 7
