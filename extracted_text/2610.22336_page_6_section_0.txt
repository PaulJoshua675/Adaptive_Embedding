(2.6) Under the protocol of the pilot clinical trial [52], every patient receives abiraterone at enrollment, and treatment is reinitiated whenever PSA returns to or above its pretreatment value. We impose both require- ments on every policy as a safety constraint, which we refer to as the forced-treatment rule. In terms of the observation (2.6), we require at = 1 at t = 0 and at every visit with yt ≥0, and impose no restriction on at at any other visit. Accordingly, the policy class Π in (2.1) consists of the history-dependent policies satisfying this constraint. We define progression as the first visit t ≥1 at which the tumor burden reaches 1.2B0, i.e., exceeds its pretreatment value by at least 20%, which is the threshold adopted in [5,15,45]. Since treatment can only be adjusted at visits, the tumor burden may reach 1.2B0 during a treatment break while the tumor is still partially sensitive to treatment. Such progression is referred to as premature progression in [15], where the threshold for pausing treatment is derived from the regrowth of the tumor between two consecutive visits to prevent it. Given that premature progression exposes the patient to a tumor burden that would be regarded as progression under treatment, we count progression irrespective of whether treatment is administered. In contrast, progression is declared only when the tumor grows under treatment in [5], which concerns intermittent androgen suppression instead of abiraterone and adopts the model of [45] (see Appendix D). We define the progression-free time Gπ(c) ∈{0, 1, . . . , T} as the number of months completed before progression. Correspondingly, we model progression as an absorbing state with zero reward, and set the reward at every visit t before progression to rc(xt, at) = I  Bt+1 < 1.2B0 , where (St+1, Rt+1) = Fα,β(xt, at). Hence the cumulative reward is given by Gπ(c), and J(π; D) = Ec∼D [Eπ [Gπ(c)]]. It should be noted that AT50, implemented as in Section 3.1, is a feasible solution of (2.1), whose two thresholds are fixed in advance rather than obtained by solving (2.1). By applying RL to (2.1), we instead search over the entire class Π for a policy that adapts the treatment to the whole history of PSA measurements and treatment decisions. 2.3 Robust Contextual MDPs In clinical practice, a policy learned by solving the contextual MDP (2.1) is subject to uncertainty beyond the variation across patients, which we model through the population distribution D of contexts. In par- ticular, the treatment decisions are made based on the measured PSA, which is affected by analytical and biological variation [35], and serves only as an indirect measure of the underlying tumor burden even when measured exactly [6]. The response of a patient to the treatment may also deviate from the nominal model in Section 2.2. Indeed, the key parameters of the nominal model are estimated from a small cohort of pa- tients and are hence subject to considerable statistical error, while the patient population and the disease dynamics may shift between learning and deployment [13], or even during treatment. For instance, tumors may develop new mechanisms of resistance to abiraterone during treatment [1]. Since the learned policy tai- lors its decisions to the PSA trajectories generated by the nominal model, its performance may deteriorate substantially in the presence of such uncertainty. In view of this, we seek policies that are robust against both errors in the observation and shifts in the patient dynamics. To this end, we model the errors in the observation as random, and account for shifts in the patient dynamics through the lens of distributionally robust optimization (DRO) [2, 3, 10, 17, 27, 37], in which one seeks the best worst-case performance over an ambiguity set of distributions. Following the game-theoretic view of distributionally robust MDPs [29, 32], we formulate robustness to such shifts as a Markov game between the controller and an adversary acting on the transition kernel. 6
