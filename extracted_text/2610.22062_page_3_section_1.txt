Rett syndrome is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder affecting approximately 1 in 10,000–15,000 live female births, caused primarily by loss-of-function mutations in the MECP2 gene [1, 2]. The condition is characterized by an initial period of apparently normal development followed by a period of regression, with loss of acquired hand skills, development of stereotypic hand movements, gait abnormalities, and loss of spoken language [2], followed by a period of stabilization without further regression of skills. In March 2023, trofinetide (Daybue) became the first FDA-approved treatment for Rett syndrome [3], following earlier phase 2 efficacy data [4] and a pivotal Phase 3 trial demonstrating improvements on the Rett Syndrome Behaviour Questionnaire (RSBQ) and Clinical Global Impression–Improvement (CGI-I) scale [5]. However, the trial captured only a subset of treatment-relevant phenotypes over 12 weeks, and real-world treatment response across the full spectrum of Rett syndrome features remains incompletely characterized. BACKGROUND AND SIGNIFICANCE Monitoring treatment response in Rett syndrome is challenging. The condition manifests across multiple domains, including motor, cognitive, behavioral and autonomic function, each potentially documented by a different specialist in narrative notes. Manual chart review of the resulting note volume can quickly become prohibitively labor-intensive. Electronic health record (EHR) phenotyping has therefore turned to automated methods, from concept-extraction systems [6, 7] to rule-based and portable phenotype algorithms [8, 9]. But traditional rule-based natural language processing (NLP) [10, 11] struggles to distinguish active symptoms from historical, anticipatory, and negated mentions within complex narratives [12, 13], a difficulty long noted in adverse-event detection [14, 15]. Large language models (LLMs) perform strongly on clinical text and medical benchmarks [16, 17, 18, 19], offering the contextual reasoning to interpret narratives, but a single LLM applied directly to clinical extraction can perform poorly [20]. In our data, a single model produced both false positives, over-interpreting routine documentation, and false negatives. The appropriate architecture depends on task complexity. Simple extraction tasks may suit a single well-prompted model [21, 22]. Our task spans many phenotypes and requires judging, within each note, whether a finding is active at that visit rather than historical, negated or anticipatory, so it is complex enough to benefit from a multi-agent verification design in which one model evaluates or critiques another’s output [23, 24]. We therefore use two independently prompted models and require agreement before making a positive call, prioritizing precision over recall. We present a dual-LLM consensus pipeline that extracts 18 treatment-response phenotypes from clinical notes of 108 patients with Rett syndrome receiving trofinetide. The 18 comprise 4 adverse effects, 13 Rett clinical features and medication refusal, and each is classified at every visit as documented present or absent (Methods). The pipeline was developed and evaluated on an expert-annotated ground truth for the diarrhea phenotype, then applied to the remaining 17 phenotypes with only the phenotype definition substituted. Our objective was to determine whether contextual LLM reasoning, combined with a consensus requirement, can extract multi-domain treatment-response phenotypes from routine clinical documentation at a scale that manual review cannot reach. We focused on one major adverse effect, diarrhea, to develop the pipeline, and we report that process in full.
