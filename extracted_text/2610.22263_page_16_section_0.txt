Choosing the covariance between the reward and the weighted score as the estimand recovers this invariance, and the covariance reduces to the policy gradient when the ratio is not regularized. At the sequence level, the estimand is a single covariance by definition; at the token level, it is an expected sum of conditional covariances but can also be written as a single covariance (Proposition 14). Hence, the two classes of covariance estimators in Section 3.1 apply at both levels. The covariance perspective clarifies the roles of baselines and score centering. Both perform centering in covariance estimation, but they differ in the choice of the mean used for centering. The group-mean baseline uses the sample mean and yields the sample-mean estimator, which coincides with RLOO and, up to a multiplicative factor, with Dr. GRPO (Proposition 10). With a sample mean, centering the reward, centering the score, and centering both are all equivalent (Proposi- tion 5). Score centering uses the population mean instead and yields the known-mean estimator, which is unbiased for any constant baseline (Proposition 8) and is defined even for a single sample. The two estimators have different variances (Proposition 9). The known-mean estimator has strictly smaller variance if and only if its baseline is sufficiently close to the variance-optimal baseline b⋆, and it can become arbitrarily worse as the baseline moves away from b⋆. Whether both forms of centering should be used simultaneously depends on whether importance sampling is applied at the sequence level or at the token level. At the sequence level, score centering is redundant once the group-mean baseline is used, regardless of whether the ratio is regularized (Proposition 11). Consequently, the population mean of the sequence-level weighted score, which is intractable to compute, is not needed. At the token level, while score centering is far more com- putationally tractable, it is no longer optional. Without score centering, the group-mean estimator is biased for the token-level estimand; its expectation contains an extra term (Proposition 15) that vanishes under standard importance sampling but is nonzero in general when the ratio is regularized. These results suggest the following practice. With sequence-level importance sampling, the group-mean baseline alone suffices, and score centering should be omitted. With token-level impor- tance sampling and a regularized ratio, score centering should be applied, either together with the group-mean baseline or with a constant baseline; the latter has smaller variance only if the baseline is sufficiently close to b⋆, which is prompt-dependent and must be estimated from multiple rollouts. Finally, because both families of estimators degrade as the training–inference mismatch grows, it remains useful to reduce the mismatch at its source, e.g., through matched kernels, batch-invariant computation, and consistent arithmetic precision across the training and inference engines.
