TF-OPD needs no training, gathers its statistics on text the models generate, and is fit to match the specialists’ outputs. We connect the two lines. For a single linear layer, asking the fused weight to reproduce each teacher’s output on that teacher’s inputs is the distillation objective restricted to the layer, and it is the least-squares problem that RegMean solves: once the second moments of the inputs are known, no optimization is left to run. TF-OPD gathers those moments where on-policy distillation gathers its data, on rollouts. Each specialist answers a few hundred training problems from its domain, the answers are filtered, the specialist reads its kept answers once with teacher forcing while forward hooks accumulate a Gram matrix per layer, and each layer is then solved exactly. Nothing is back-propagated. On Olmo 3 7B this yields a generalist without a single gradient step: one model that keeps most of what each specialist gained on five mathematics and two code benchmarks and that is, on average, more accurate than either specialist alone (Figure 1). Three findings explain when it works. First, the filter carries the result. A minority of the mathematics specialist’s rollouts run to the length limit in a repetition loop: few among the answers, several times more among the tokens. With them the closed form is no better than task arithmetic on AIME25, and removing them alone closes the gap to the specialist scored in the same format. Second, the token-level fit is not what fails: the unfiltered model already follows the specialist almost token for token, so hard problems are decided by which inputs the statistics weigh. Third, rollouts written by the student, the on-policy data of trained distillation, do not improve the closed form. Reinforcement learning also moves weights so little that the 16-bit release format distorts a fused update, which makes the formats of storage and evaluation part of the method. 2
