Four Gemini 4 Argon agents entered a newly generated Minecraft survival world with one instruc- tion: defeat the Ender Dragon. The run tests whether a team of general-purpose coding agents, not built for Minecraft, can pursue one goal for more than a day, through thousands of programs and hundreds of failures, while coordinating with its peers. Each agent controlled a player through Mineflayer, an open-source JavaScript library for Minecraft bots, and communicated with its team- mates through the game’s chat. Twenty-seven hours after their first program, the team defeated the dragon. Reaching the end of Minecraft from a random spawn requires a long sequence of dependent steps across three dimensions (Fan et al., 2022): gathering resources and crafting tools in the Overworld, collecting blaze rods and ender pearls in the Nether, locating an underground stronghold, and destroying the ten crystals that heal the dragon in the End. Prior work in Minecraft has trained policies on gameplay (Baker et al., 2022; Hafner et al., 2025; SIMA Team, 2025) or paired language models with game-specific planners, curricula, and skill libraries (Wang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Altera.AL et al., 2024). Without pre-assigned roles or a prescribed plan, the four agents divided the work among themselves, kept shared notes of coordinates and lessons, recovered each other’s items after deaths, and wrote 2,783 JavaScript programs to complete the game. 27 h 14 min from the first program to the dragon's defeat 2,783 programs written and run by the four agents 63,313 distinct lines of JavaScript 229 messages between teammates in the game's chat 605 deaths along the way 2. How the agents play Each player is driven by our general-purpose Gemini 4 Argon coding agent, connected to a standard Minecraft server through Mineflayer and its pathfinding plugin. Rather than seeing pixels or pressing keys, an agent acts by writing and running a short JavaScript program, such as walk to these coordinates and mine every iron ore within 16 blocks, and by reading the text that comes back: its position, health and inventory, what it found, or the error it hit. It then writes the next program. Each program runs until its task finishes or reaches a time limit chosen by the agent; the median program ran for 33.5 seconds. Over the run, each player had a program running about 40% of the time; the rest went to reading results, thinking and writing the next program. Mineflayer lets a program read a player’s position, health, inventory and surroundings, and order basic actions such as walking to a point, digging or placing a block, crafting an item or attacking. It does not know how to play: what to do, and in what order, was up to the agents. 2
