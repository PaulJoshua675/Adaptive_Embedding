Full held-out scaling curves appear in Appendices A.2–A.3. 5.5 Beyond Cover Trees: A KD-Tree Under the Same Harness The factors we isolate above are cover-tree-specific in their details, which raises the question of whether the methodological finding—that implementation choices can dominate algorithmic ones— holds for other index structures. To test this without rewriting the study, we added a median-split KD-tree inside RUSTKNN, reusing the identical kernel, candidate structure, distance counter, and timing code, so it runs the same five feature-flag ablations. We also benchmarked kiddo, an in- dependent Rust KD-tree crate, under the same 20-allocation protocol, separating the effect of the codebase from that of the index. Full tables are in Appendix H. The better index reverses with dimension. On all-NN at k=1, the KD-tree is 13× faster than single-tree cover trees on corel (254 vs. 3,248 ms; same-allocation values from Table 11) and 23× faster on covtype (4,465 vs. 100,590 ms), because axis-aligned box pruning is highly effective at moderate d. At d=784 that pruning collapses—the KD-tree issues 594M distance computations— and it becomes 12× slower than the single-tree cover tree (4.09M vs. 334,487 ms): a textbook curse-of-dimensionality crossover, measured with no cross-codebase confounders. The same algorithm in a different codebase differs by up to 9×. Our KD-tree and kiddo im- plement the same algorithm, yet kiddo is 9× faster on pendigits (7.8 vs. 72 ms) while being 2.9× slower on covtype and 2.0× slower on mnist (8.31M vs. 4.09M ms): each wins on different data. A practitioner comparing “KD-tree vs. cover tree” would reach the same verdict with either library here, but not the same magnitude: on covtype the KD-tree beats the best cover-tree mode by 3.1× with ours but by only 7% with kiddo—precisely the confounding this paper argues against, now shown outside cover trees.
