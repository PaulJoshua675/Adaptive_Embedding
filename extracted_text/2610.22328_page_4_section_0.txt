Su- pervising the model on these rollouts is challenging even when a ground-truth editing sequence is available. Since an edit can have many valid outcomes, the model’s output at a given turn may differ from the corresponding ground-truth image, even if the edit was applied correctly. As a result, subsequent edits in the rollout are applied to images that differ from those in the ground-truth sequence. Therefore, the ground-truth images cannot serve as paired targets for supervising these edits. Inspired by Self-Forcing [17], which addresses a sim- ilar challenge in autoregressive video generation, we use score distillation to supervise our student model Gθ, which becomes our stable editor after training. We compute the score-distillation signal using a pretrained flow-matching editor vϕ that serves as the teacher. A key challenge is that the student’s intermediate out- puts may contain accumulated errors, making them unreli- able inputs for the teacher. To address this, we introduce asymmetric conditioning of the student and teacher through compound-edit supervision. While the student applies edits sequentially to its own outputs, the teacher is always con- ditioned on the original image and a compound instruction combining all edits up to the current turn. This allows the teacher to provide a training signal that does not inherit the errors accumulated along the student’s trajectory. 4.1. Self-Generated Editing Rollouts We generate editing rollouts by repeatedly applying the stu- dent editor Gθ to its own outputs (left part of Figure 2). Each rollout starts from a source image I0 and a sequence of E instructions c1, . . . , cE, with E = 10 during training. We describe the dataset of instruction sequences in Section 5. At turn e, the editor applies the next instruction ce to the completed output from the previous turn: Ie = Gθ(Ie−1, ce), e = 1, . . . , E. Thus, after the first turn, the editor operates on its own gen- erated images, as it does at inference. We implement the student with FLUX.2-klein-9B, an image editor that operates in four denoising steps. We de- note the complete four-step editing process by Gθ and a single model prediction within this process by vθ. We use the convention where σ = 1 corresponds to pure noise and σ = 0 to a clean image, with the predicted velocity point- ing toward noise. At a denoising step with noise level σ, let xσ be the current state. The model’s prediction provides an estimate of the clean output: ˆx = xσ −σvθ(xσ), where, for brevity, we omit the dependence of vθ on the noise level, the previous image, and the current instruction. For a given training rollout, we consider an edit turn e ∈{1, . . . , E} and a denoising exit step s ∈{1, . . . , 4}. We first obtain Ie−1 by applying the preceding e −1 ed- its, each using the full four-step denoising process without tracking gradients. At turn e, we execute the denoising pro- cess up to step s, where we compute the clean output esti- mate ˆxs e. Gradients are propagated only through the model’s prediction at this step, without backpropagating through earlier denoising steps or edit turns. Throughout the roll- out, we pass the edited images directly between turns in the model’s latent space, avoiding repeated decoding and en- coding. 4.2. Training Asymmetric Conditioning. Having obtained the clean- output estimate ˆxs e at turn e, we now define the training signal. This signal should encourage adherence to the edit- ing instructions, preservation of relevant image details, and generation of realistic, artifact-free images. We obtain this signal from a pretrained flow-matching image editor vϕ, which serves as our teacher. Specifically, we evaluate its score on a noisy version of ˆxs e at noise level τ, denoted by zτ(ˆxs e). Unlike conventional text-to-image score distilla- tion, our editing teacher is conditioned on both an image and an instruction. It can therefore guide the output toward results that satisfy the requested edit while preserving rele- vant details of the conditioning image. The remaining question is how to condition the pre- trained editing teacher vϕ. Its conditioning image should serve as the source of the edit, while its text input should describe the intended change from that image to ˆxs e. A nat- ural choice is the previous output Ie−1 and the current in- struction ce, which are also the inputs to the student Gθ at turn e. However, beyond the first turn, Ie−1 is generated by the student and may already contain artifacts. This creates a distribution shift for the teacher, which was trained on clean images. Moreover, since the teacher is expected to preserve details of its conditioning image that are not affected by the instruction, it may also preserve these artifacts rather than correct them. We examine this conditioning choice in the ablation study in Section 5.4. Instead, we condition the teacher on the original clean image I0 and the accumulated instructions he = (c1, . . . , ce). This allows the teacher to score the result of multiple edits as a single compound edit, without relying on potentially corrupted intermediate images. For example, a sequence of four editing instructions can be treated as a single compound edit: Student: “add handbag” −−−−→ “make it red” −−−−→ “make it leather” −−−−→ “add a gold buckle” −−−−−→ Teacher: “add a red leather handbag with a gold buckle” −−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−→ To implement this supervision, the teacher vϕ evaluates a noisy version of the student’s clean-output estimate ˆxs e, denoted by zτ(ˆxs e), and produces the velocity prediction vϕ(zτ(ˆxs e); I0, he, τ) used for score distillation. 4
