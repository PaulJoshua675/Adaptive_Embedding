The question How long is the coast of Britain? did more than introduce a memorable fractal1. It exposed a dependence that ordinary measurement language tends to hide: the answer belongs jointly to an object and to a ruler. A coarse ruler crosses bays and omits bound- aries that a fine ruler must follow. The resulting lengths disagree without either measurement being erroneous. The point neuron is neuroscience’s coarse ruler. From early threshold-unit models onward, a large branch of theoretical neuroscience has represented a cell by a small number of state variables and an input–output rule2–6. This abstraction underlies theories of recurrent dynam- ics, chaos, balanced excitation and inhibition, and popu- lation computation, and has proved extraordinarily pro- ductive despite suppressing the spatial structure of the cell. A related statistical tradition asks how much of pop- ulation activity can be captured by low-order dependen- cies alone. In small groups of cells, pairwise maximum- entropy models reproduce surprisingly rich collective ac- tivity from first- and second-order statistics7. That ade- quacy is conditional: it is expected in small, sparsely ac- tive populations whatever the underlying interactions8, it gives way to explicit higher-order terms in larger pop- ulations under natural stimulation9, and it changes with which cells the model retains10. The analysis is careful about what is measured. Its inferred weights are func- tional dependencies among recorded cells, not necessar- ily synapses, and unexplained variability can combine higher-order dependence, temporal dependence, unob- served inputs, and stochasticity. We propose a scale-theoretic fromalism. The success of a direct model at the resolution of a population record- ing characterizes an effective relation among the variables that survive measurement and projection. It does not imply that the cellular computation realizing that rela- tion is itself direct, additive, or single-stage. This dis- tinction matters because a neuron is not, at finer resolu- tion, a single computational degree of freedom. Dendrites contain spatially distributed nonlinear subunits12–14, and the input–output transformation of a detailed neuron can require a multilayer artificial network for accurate func- tional emulation15,16. Thus, the population-level and dendritic descriptions are not competing accounts of the same object; they are descriptions at different obser- vational scales, connected by a many-to-one projection. The question is therefore not whether the reduced model is useful, but whether predictive adequacy at one scale warrants identifying its effective dependencies with the computation that produces them at another. The coastline analogy is deliberately epistemic. We do not claim that every dendrite possesses an exact frac- tal dimension over an unlimited range. Neuronal ar- bors do exhibit scale-dependent geometric organization and constrained branching17–19, but geometry is not the premise of the argument.
