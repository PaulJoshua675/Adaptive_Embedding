As shown in Table 4, MindSim receives higher mean ratings than MindEval and Patient-Ψ at both the turn and conversation levels. In terms of iden- tified issues, annotators highlight writing style and narration as the main weakness of Patient-Ψ, whereas for MindEval, its weaknesses lie mostly in accepting the counsellor’s suggestion too read- ily (a problem also identified by the authors). Our simulator was seldom criticised for form, but it was pointed out that register came across some- times as “performative ambivalence”.5 Real con- versations were flagged for having patients that do not answer the counsellor or that are hard to follow, but annotators often took this incoherence as a sign of a real person (“some things do not make sense but that makes it ring truer”). Surface-feature distances. Following PSI-Bench (Hoang et al., 2026) and User-Sim Index (Zhou et al., 2026), we compare the surface form of the patient turns of each simulator with those of real users. For every conversation, we compute seven features of the patient’s turns: word count, words per sentence, the coefficient of variation of the word count across turns, lexical diversity (MTLD), and the rate of PSI- Bench’s absolutist, depressive and non-fluency markers per 1,000 tokens. We then compare, for each feature, the distribution of per-conversation values of each simulator with that of the real sessions using the Wasserstein-1 distance (expressed in standard deviations of real usage). For simulated sessions with the production counsellor, the PSI point estimates are 0.44 with Sonnet 4.6 and 0.47 with DeepSeek, compared with 0.54 for MindEval and 0.92 for Patient-Ψ (Table 5). Across all systems, word count CV represents the largest gap: human users alternate response lengths far more than any system, which aligns with the known low style variability of LLM user simulators (Zhou et al., 2026). Conversation dynamics. Beyond single turns, a realistic patient should also follow the course of a real conversation. Following PSI-Bench (Hoang et al., 2026), we label every patient turn as a problem state- ment, a transition, change talk or filler. Figure 3 indicates real users keep talking about their problem throughout the session with change talk only being present between 5 and 10% of turns. Our simulator follows the same course, with a share of problem statements within 7 points of real usage on average (9.5 with DeepSeek) and change talk at 6% (8% with DeepSeek) at the end of the session. The baselines instead follow the scripted therapeutic arc, with patients moving from their problem to transition and change talk early in the conversation.
