A LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK OS-Helix is an initial step toward native GUI/code hybrid computer use, with remaining limitations centered primarily on computational and API resource constraints. First, desktop environments are difficult to scale comprehensively: even an application–artifact desk- top infrastructure spanning diverse applications cannot cover the full diversity of real user machines, private artifacts, application versions, and personalized configurations. Future work should further expand the application and artifact ecosystem and study broader randomization of system states and user configurations while preserving reproducibility. Second, automatic verification remains imperfect. Unlike software engineering tasks, where gold final states can be obtained deterministically, automated CUA task synthesis under limited resources cannot reliably capture ground-truth terminal states—tasks may therefore be subject to feasibility risks, and the resulting evaluators carry intrinsic brittleness. Concretely, rule-based evaluators re- liably cover many file-backed tasks but require careful construction and often depend on specific application versions or export formats; more fundamentally, even when an agent’s execution is se- mantically near-correct, inconsistent strictness in file-attribute checks can reduce the score to zero. Rubric-based partial scoring, adopted in several recent works including ours, partially alleviates this issue but does not resolve it entirely. VLM-based evaluators extend coverage to textual out- puts and visual outcomes, yet may miss fine-grained discrepancies; more critically, due to their inherent brittleness, existing RL engineering practice typically excludes VLM-based scoring from the reward signal altogether, concentrating construction effort on rule-based evaluators—which in turn constrains the types and diversity of tasks that can be incorporated into training. Future work should develop stronger evaluator-generation, repair, and validation pipelines, and systematically study cross-evaluator agreement as well as robustness to reward hacking. Third, our agentic RL experiments are subject to certain limitations imposed by available computa- tional resources. RL training was conducted only on the dense 27B model with relatively conserva- tive hyperparameter settings; the total number of training steps was likewise constrained, precluding a systematic investigation of the scaling behavior of agentic RL. For OS-Helix-35B-A3B, despite its strong performance in SFT experiments, the Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture introduces non-trivial additional challenges in training-inference consistency and optimization stability dur- ing agentic RL, making it difficult to support this training pipeline reliably under current resource conditions. We regard a more thorough exploration of RL training dynamics and scaling laws, to- gether with a stable extension of Step-balanced GSPO to larger MoE computer-using agents, as an important direction for future work. Beyond scaling, we believe the community is now at an inflection point where recursive self- improvement (RSI) deserves serious consideration. As CUA models attain a meaningful level of general computer-use capability, the model itself can serve as its own teacher: rolling out interaction trajectories on existing tasks, providing the training signal for the next iteration, and progressively improving through this self-bootstrapping loop. Rather than relying solely on human demonstra- tion or externally curated data, such a paradigm allows the agent to continuously generate its own training trajectories with growing quality as capability improves and feed them back into subse- quent training rounds. We view this self-evolving, teacher-free training paradigm as one of the most promising long-term directions for advancing CUA agents. Finally, many existing desktop benchmarks were originally designed for GUI-only agents, with task design, reference solutions, and evaluation functions all implicitly centered on visual interaction paths. Although their tasks can often be completed with hybrid strategies, existing evaluators may not capture every valid GUI/code solution. Furthermore, using success rate as the sole metric is overly coarse: it neither distinguishes whether the agent selected the more efficient modality nor captures potential safety risks inherent to hybrid execution, such as unintended side effects of code execution or inadvertent system-level operations. A particularly promising complementary direction is the migration and adaptation of existing general-purpose agent benchmarks such as GDPval (Patwardhan et al., 2026) and Agents’ Last Exam (Sun et al., 2026b) into GUI environments. These benchmarks are inherently designed around authentic artifact manipulation and complex reasoning tasks, and thus naturally encompass diverse environment states and authentic artifacts.
