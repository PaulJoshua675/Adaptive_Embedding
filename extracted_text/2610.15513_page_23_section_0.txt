Under a single environment, XFER (Equation 3) reduces to ASR, so we report only ASR throughout. We report Wilson 95% score intervals alongside ASR, computed over the number of behaviors in each benchmark (50 for AdvBench, 100 for JBB-Behaviors, 200 for HarmBench). One caveat applies to reading them: a Wilson interval models 𝑛independent draws at a fixed success probability, whereas our protocol optimizes each behavior until the judge accepts a candidate. The intervals should therefore be read as sampling uncertainty over the benchmark’s behavior set, that is, what the figure might look like on a different draw of similar behaviors, rather than as a confidence statement about the method’s success probability on a fixed budget. We additionally report iterations to convergence as a secondary efficiency metric. In the transfer experiments (Section 4.3), ASR is computed over a single shared attack evaluated against all training behaviors simultaneously, rather than over per-behavior attacks. 4.1.4 Judge Cross-Validation. All REPRO results in this paper use a single Qwen3-8B judge model for scoring target responses. Our choice of Qwen3-8B was informed by two considerations. First, prior work on LLM-as-a-judge has established that GPT-4 achieves the highest agreement with human raters on safety-related judgment tasks [45], and our own earlier work on faithfulness evaluation in red-teaming [3] conducted a systematic comparison of judge models with full statistical testing and found that Qwen3-8B agrees closely with GPT-4 on safety decisions while being substantially cheaper to run at the scale of our experiments, which involve repeated re-scoring of large candidate populations across multiple benchmarks and target models. Second, the baselines we compare against were originally scored by their authors using different judges (GPT-4 for PAIR and TAP, Llama-3-70B for JailbreakBench baselines, the HarmBench classifier for HarmBench baselines), which raises a natural question: could REPRO’s reported success rates be an artifact of judge leniency rather than attack effectiveness? To answer this directly, we re-scored a stratified random sample of 365 archived target replies drawn from two pools: (i) 165 pairs sampled across the five AdvBench-50 target models retained for the query-cost experiment (Table 12) at 33 pairs per target, and (ii) 200 pairs sampled from the JBB-50 gpt-4o-mini ablation run (Section 4.4) with the same stratification protocol. Both sub- samples use a 70/30 positive/negative split within each stratum to ensure both decision sides are represented, with seed 42 for reproducibility. The two replacement judges are GPT-4 as a high- capability API judge and Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct as a capability-comparable local judge. All three judges were invoked through the same API path with the original rubric and 2000-character reply window pinned from the experiment code, so only the judge model varies across the three raters. Cross-validation on additional targets used in the comprehensive model coverage evaluation (§4.2) would require re-running those evaluations with reply archiving enabled and is left to future work. Agreement is substantial across all three judges. Table 5 reports the inter-rater agreement metrics on the combined 365-pair pool.
