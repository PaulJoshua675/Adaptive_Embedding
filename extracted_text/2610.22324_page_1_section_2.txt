The first step of any research cycle is generating ideas worth testing, before an experiment can tell you which one was right. Current work with Large Language Models (LLMs) on idea generation has focused on one of two things: producing a single idea and scoring it against an existing, held-out idea (He-Yueya et al., 2026), or building end-to-end systems that carry one idea through execution and judge whether that execution succeeded (Kirgis et al., 2026; Ning et al., 2026; Falck et al., 2026; Garikaparthi et al., 2026; Si et al., 2026). Either way, an idea is generated and judged on its own, never alongside other candidates it could be weighed against. Looking at how humans do research, a researcher facing a body of literature brainstorms several directions off the same shared context, and then selects among them using some signal (Platt, 1964; Klahr & Dunbar, 1988), rather than just committing to one idea. We draw a connection between ideation and creative problem solving. The brainstorm-then-select process is how creative think- ing/problem solving can also be formalized, divergent thinking explores a space broadly for many candidates, and convergent thinking narrows it through selection (Boden, 2004; Guilford, 1967). The way current LLMs are used for ideation can look different. Because each idea is generated and judged on its own, multiple candidates come from repeated independent sampling, with no shared context to explore deliberately and no pool to converge over. Any diversity among them is incidental rather than designed (Puri et al., 2026; Bahlous-Boldi et al., 2026). Work on autonomous research agents has also flagged diversity as a shortcoming (Liu et al., 2026; Garikaparthi et al., 2026).
