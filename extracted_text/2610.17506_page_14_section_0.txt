For our purposes, increasing ensemble size yielded better performance for every computa- tional budget and ESN size tested, even when accounting for additional interaction costs–for computational costs that total twice or more than their minimum cost per ensemble size (10 units per ESN), the performance advantage for each increase in ensemble size holds true even if costs are doubled to account for interactions. Interaction costs are far less in our model because they only involve pooling and broadcasting read-out values, which scale linearly with ensemble size. Natural interactions may cost and scale differently and it is likely to depend on the kinds of agents and interactions. Finally, Figure 5b quantifies the efficiency gains for each ensemble size by plotting the ratio between collective and individual ESN performance at matched budgets. The figure shows that, while both the mean and range of efficiency gains increased with ESN size, they both leveled off at a mean gain of 1.86 over individual ESNs for the maximum ensemble size of 16, about double the performance gain for dyads with the same computational cost. In summary, group performance scaled up with ensemble size more than ESN size; the best average performance we observed was for 16 ESNs with reservoirs of only 20 units each. While this scaling effect may be attributed to ESNs and how read-outs were pooled, our model nonetheless demonstrates how ensemble dynamics can scale up with ensemble size to support collective performance. 4.3 Summary Our model allows systematic investigation into the collective benefit of interaction in larger groups by controlling underlying factors such as the computational budget of individuals. Our findings with groups are consistent with the benefits of pooled-feedback observed in dyadic networks in the first experiment. We also show that the relative gains from interaction depend on the group size and capacity of the networks. We observe that smaller, less capable networks in larger groups generally benefit more from pooling information compared to smaller groups of larger networks.
