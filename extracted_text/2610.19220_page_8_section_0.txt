The fixed point curve is a traveling wave The curve (§3) accounts for a phenomenon the CTM authors report: low-frequency traveling waves across a UMAP of activation profiles, arising with no driving signal. They are the moving fixed point made visible. Ordering the D neurons by the phase of the fixed-point trajectory z⋆(At) or by their own phase recovers the same traveling wave (Fig. 5, left vs middle). This ordering recovers the authors’ activation-phase ordering (peak-tick Spearman 0.82, circular phase correlation 0.88; medians over 128 images) — as expected, since the state tracks z⋆within about one step, so activation phase is essentially fixed-point phase. The neurons’ peak activation times are spread across essentially the whole window — standard deviation 15.0 of 50 ticks for both the fixed points and the activations, against ≈14.4 for a uniform spread — so neurons traverse the shared motion in staggered phase rather than together, which is what makes the front travel rather than stand. Because ρ(J) < 1 the fixed point moves smoothly and the resulting pattern is a transient propagating pulse rather than a sustained standing wave, matching the low-frequency, driverless character of the reported phenomenon. Figure 5: The traveling wave is the moving fixed point. One held-out image; neurons ordered by the phase of z⋆(At). Left: activations zt over ticks — the reported wave. Right: the fixed points under the same ordering, indistinguishable from the left. That the two panels match follows largely from the one-step lag of §3. The wave is not, however, coupled to the attention schedule: across 128 images, the cross-validated canonical correlation between the fixed point’s leading temporal modes and the schedule’s (0.52) does not exceed a permutation null (95th percentile 0.54; p = 0.17). The fixed-point trajectory is also higher-dimensional than a single front: its top-two temporal modes hold only 0.38 of the variance, on average over these images. 8
