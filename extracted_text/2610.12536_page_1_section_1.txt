This work was motivated by the broader study of recursive self-improvement and LLM-guided program search: how these algorithms are structured, which tasks they address, how well their solutions generalize, and which mechanisms support improvement. The study examines one part of that motivation: whether a program-search algorithm inspired by prior work can also show improvement through repeated eval- uation and revision. In program search, one part of the improvement loop proposes a solution, often source code or an executable policy. Another part, the evaluator, provides a score or feedback on how that solution performs on the task. Optimization by PROmpting (OPRO) illustrates this feedback pattern: a language model receives a task de- scription and previously evaluated solutions, then uses that feedback to propose further solutions [1]. FunSearch makes this feedback loop concrete for ex- ecutable programs. It uses an island-based database of solutions, maintaining multiple populations to support diversity. Its applications include cap-set constructions and online bin-packing heuristics [2]. AlphaEvolve extends evolutionary code modification to scientific and computational problems, using evalu- ator feedback to guide revisions [3]. In these systems, evaluation gives program search an external measure of whether a proposed change is useful. These algorithms differ in what they retain and how they generate candidates. ShinkaEvolve combines par- ent sampling, code-novelty rejection, and bandit-based selection among language models [4]. Evolution of Heuristics evolves descriptions of heuristic ideas together with their executable implementations, giving the language model both conceptual and code- level information about the solutions it explores [5]. EliteTable draws on these approaches through a fixed- capacity table of high-scoring programs. New candidates compete with retained solutions after each generation, while three operators generate fresh proposals, edits, and remixes. The design aims to provide a simple feedback loop for repeated program improvement. Keeping incumbents eligible guarantees that the best recorded search score cannot decrease, but does not guar- antee that newly generated solutions improve. A search may retain elites based on similar heuristics and become confined to a local optimum [2]. Selected solutions may also fail to generalize to new episodes, so held-out evalu- ation is necessary [10]. The study asks two questions. First, do in-sample winner scores and candidate-score distributions improve across generations? Second, do selected-winner gains transfer to held-out episodes? I examine these questions across six Gymnasium control tasks [7]. Records were kept for every candidate across the sixty searches. Between generations one and ten, pooled candidate-score medians increased in all six tasks and median selected-winner search scores increased in five.
