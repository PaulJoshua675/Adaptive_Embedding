The importance of this model family is reflected both externally—through the MLPerf recommendation benchmark evolution and the adoption of DLRM [20], DLRM-v2 [16], and DLRM-v3 [14,21]—and internally through the scale of Meta’s production deployment and infrastructure investment dedicated to improving DLRM efficiency. Sparse evolution. Embedding tables grow rapidly in both vocabulary size and dimensionality, forcing distributed sharding and collective-heavy embedding exchange. As training clusters expanded, two practical directions emerged. First, multi-stage pipelining became essential [22]: embedding lookup, all-to-all exchange, and optimizer steps must be organized to reduce exposed latency and avoid synchronization bubbles. Second, sparse parallelism itself evolved beyond single dimension. Recent work on two-dimensional sparse parallelism [23] in- troduces a hierarchical strategy that adds data-parallel structure on top of model-parallel embedding sharding, reducing all- to-all scope, lowering embedding activation memory growth, and mitigating imbalance/straggler behavior. Together, these trends reflect a broader shift: the sparse subsystem is no longer “just embeddings,” but a distributed system whose parallelism strategy, pipeline structure, and optimizer semantics jointly determine scalability. Dense evolution. Classical DLRMs pair large embedding tables with relatively shallow dense components (e.g., a few fully connected layers for feature interaction). Recent evolution of DLRMs (Fig. 1) shows that scaling the dense subsystem can yield predictable quality gains analogous to LLM scaling laws [24, 25]. Wukong [26] demonstrates this by stacking factorization-machine layers with a synergistic upscaling strategy, achieving smooth quality improvements over orders of magnitude in dense-side FLOPs. A complementary trend is the adoption of Transformer-like modules to model long user-interaction histories. Meta’s HSTU [21] exemplifies this sequence-centric direction, replacing shallow pooling with attention over hundreds of past events. Together, these advances open a second axis for improving recommendation quality: in addition to scaling embedding tables, practitioners can now scale dense computation (FLOPs) to capture richer feature interactions and temporal patterns.
