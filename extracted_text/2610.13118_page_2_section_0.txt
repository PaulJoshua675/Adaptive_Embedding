In it, the controllers of the robots are selected based on their performance in a task, and behavior emerges from the interaction between the agent, its body, and the environment [1]. This approach has shown that simulated and physical robots can develop useful signals for foraging [2] and for group navigation [3]. This work has transformed robots into a laboratory for studying biological questions about the evolution of social behavior [4]. A central question in this line of inquiry is under what conditions communication emerges. The genetic relationship between individuals and the level at which selection operates influence the reliability of signals [2], [5]. History also plays a role. Populations that start from the same conditions can arrive at different signaling strategies due to historical contingency [6]. A more basic condition has received less attention. A signal is useful when it conveys information that the receiver cannot obtain independently. Otherwise, the signal becomes redundant, reducing the selective pressure for its maintenance. This condition is easy to overlook when designing an experiment. When the controller receives inputs such as the direction or distance to the target, in addition to its sensors, evolution is facilitated, but communication can become unnecessary. In that case, communication signals may appear only if bonuses are added to the fitness function or rules are used to force their emission, and it becomes difficult to distinguish a signal that evolved from one that was imposed. Added to this is the gap between simulation and reality [7]. A poorly validated robot model can produce behaviors that depend on simulator artifacts rather than the task itself. In previous work, we transferred a communication protocol co-evolved in 2D directly to a 3D physical simulation without retraining, and the transfer was partial and asymmetric [8]. Here we take the opposite route. We evolve directly in a validated 3D model and ask under which conditions communication emerges at all. A second question refers to the controller architecture. Vertebrates solve the problem of choosing what to do using the basal ganglia, a set of nuclei that arbitrates between competing motor programs [9]. Computational models of these circuits have been incorporated into robots and have produced coherent behavioral sequences in foraging tasks [10], [11]. Recently, EASA, an implementation of this model that controls a Khepera robot under realistic contact physics, was presented [12]. Such an architecture may provide a natural scaffold for evolution.
