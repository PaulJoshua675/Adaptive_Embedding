That work proposed boundary dependence, a table of how much the model’s own loss rises when a patch start is removed, as a label-free fix. Here we take a different, cheaper route, motivated by compression. An LZ77 compressor [Ziv and Lempel, 1977] encodes text as copies of earlier substrings plus new literals: copies are cheap, and new literals are where information enters. We ask whether patch boundaries belong where the compressor would stop copying. Novelty patching starts a patch where the upcoming bytes are unlikely to repeat the last 128 bytes, predicted from the previous two bytes by a lookup table fitted on raw text in seconds. It needs no labels, no reference model and no losses, and at inference it costs a table lookup. Our contributions: 1. A strong, scale-dependent effect in math. In BLT-style models trained from scratch at 1M, 12M and 50M parameters on the same corpus and patch rate (3 seeds each), novelty patching has no effect at 1M, a clear effect at 12M and a large one at 50M: computed results 57.5% vs 20.7% for entropy, final answers 89.5% vs 59.9%. A table fitted on the raw pretraining corpus keeps the gain (§4). 2. Scope outside math. Novelty lowers bits per byte on prose, ties entropy on source code (also on exact token accuracy) and on synthetic execution traces (§5). 3. Why it works. Computed results have ordinary surprisal, so surprisal-seeking boundaries miss them: boundary enrichment, the metric SOMBRERO optimizes, is highest for entropy, the rule that is worst on computed results. Novelty succeeds where new content begins at segment starts near the computation; in code and traces it does not. A learned chunker (H-Net) recovers such structure on its own (§6). 4.
