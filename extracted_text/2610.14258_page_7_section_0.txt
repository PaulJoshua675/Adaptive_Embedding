Do Artificial Neural Networks Need Dendritic Nanotubes? 7 4 Experimental Design 4.1 Benchmarks and models Permuted MNIST (P-MNIST). Twenty tasks built from MNIST [24], the first with the original pixel order and each of the others with a fixed random permutation of the pixels [13]. All tasks share one 10-way output layer, so the protocol is domain-incremental [41]. The network is a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 256 ReLU units. Studies 1 and 2 train on all 60,000 training images and report test accuracy over a grid of 𝜆. Studies 3 and 4 hold out 10% of the training images for validation and select 𝜆on validation accuracy only. Split CIFAR-100 (Study 3). Ten tasks of ten CIFAR-100 classes each in a fixed class order [21], with a separate 10-way head per task, a task-incremental protocol. The heads are not regularized. CIFAR-100 superclass (Study 4). Five tasks. Task 𝑘contains the 𝑘-th fine class of each of the 20 superclasses, and the label is the superclass, predicted by one shared 20-way head. Unlike the multi-head split, this domain-incremental protocol leaves substantial room for forgetting. All parameters are regularized. Both CIFAR benchmarks use a convolutional network with four 3 × 3 convolutional layers of 32, 32, 64, and 64 channels with padding 1 and ReLU activations, 2 × 2 max pooling after the second and fourth, and a fully connected layer of 256 ReLU units, without batch normalization. Inputs are standardized per channel with training-set statistics, and we use no data augmentation. The CIFAR networks train for five epochs per task with batches of 64, and the P-MNIST networks for three epochs per task with batches of 128. Unless stated otherwise we use Adam with learning rate 10−3 [19], reset at the start of each task, and add the gradient of the penalty to the task gradient. With SGD (learning rate 𝜂= 0.01, momentum 𝜇= 0.9), an explicit penalty gradient becomes unstable once 2𝜂𝜆Ω𝑖exceeds 2(1 + 𝜇), which large importance values can trigger. We therefore apply the penalty as an exact proximal step after each optimizer step, outside the momentum buffer, 𝜃𝑖←(𝜃𝑖+ 𝑐𝑖𝜃(𝑡−1) 𝑖 )/(1 + 𝑐𝑖) with 𝑐𝑖= 2𝜂𝜆Ω𝑖[31]. 4.2 The four studies Study 1, mechanisms inspired by dendritic nanotubes (10 seeds). Study 1 compares six arms on P-MNIST. Three have no consolidation, namely a plain MLP, a wider MLP with two hidden layers of 350 units that matches the parameter count of the lateral arm (401,110 against 400,394 parameters), and an MLP with a dense lateral weight matrix 𝑀in each hidden layer. The lateral matrix is applied once after the feedforward activation as ℎ←ReLU(ℎ+ ℎ𝑀⊤), with an L1 penalty of 10−5 on 𝑀and magnitude pruning of entries below 2 × 10−3 every 200 steps. The other three arms use unit-level consolidation, either alone (tag only) or with transient links formed at random or by co-activity, with 𝑘= 8 new links per step. We used 𝜆∈{0.003, 0.01, 0.03} with lifetime 𝐿= 200, and 𝐿∈{25, 50, 500, 1000, 2000} at 𝜆= 0.01. Study 2, links versus shrinkage (10 seeds). The arms are tag only, graph-free shrinkage of the task importance of each hidden layer with 𝛽∈{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1} (Eq. 3, one group per layer, applied before normalization), and transient links under the five rules of Section 3.4 with 𝐿∈{25, 200}. All rules form the same number of links per step, so link counts are matched. We used 𝜆∈{0.001, 0.003, 0.01}. We logged the coefficient of variation (CV) of the consolidated importance of each hidden layer at the end of training, averaged over the two hidden layers, together with the mean fraction of units with at least one live link (coverage), the fraction of units linked at least once per task (touched), and the Gini coefficient of link formation across units.
