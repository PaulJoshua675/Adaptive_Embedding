In OLMo3-7B (Figure 1), after layer 16 of 32, the logit-lens direction for a token deviates by 75 degrees from the model’s own lexical readout and the Jacobian-lens direction by 77 degrees, and four layers before the output both still deviate by at least 40 degrees. Our starting point is that these directions, the lexical readouts, form a reference frame: we call their family at a state its lexical reference frame, and the frame that the complete model induces its native frame. The native frame returns the model’s output at every layer, so a useful frame withholds a stated part of this computation: the frame of order n, written LEXFRAME[n], transports a state through the model’s own layers with n rounds of attention between positions and replaces the remaining atten- tion by a typical attention output, estimated once per layer from unlabelled text. LEXFRAME[0] reads what a state holds by itself, LEXFRAME[1] what it draws from its prefix, and LEXFRAME[∞] is the na- tive frame. The idea is simple: read a state the way the model reads it, through the layers that remain and with the context it sees. A lens ignores the context and is therefore bounded; a frame uses it, as far as its order allows. As nothing is fitted to the model’s output, lexical reference frames are comparable across layers, checkpoints and models. We evaluate lexical reference frames at every layer of SmolLM3-3B (Hugging Face, 2025) and OLMo3-7B (Team OLMo, 2025) and at 16 of their training checkpoints, against the logit lens, the Jacobian lens, a supervised probe and dis- tances on the point cloud. From the middle layers on, LEXFRAME[1] recovers the native frame’s lexi- cal readouts beyond the bound of every lens and matches the model’s output in content and confi- dence, which lenses do only near the output; one round of attention to the prefix closes most of the gap that LEXFRAME[0] leaves. Its readings settle on the model’s prediction early enough for an early exit to skip more than two fifths of the layers, and they stay faithful along training while the logit lens degrades. Distances on the point cloud barely pre- dict the effect of replacing a state, as lexical read- outs lie almost entirely outside the directions in which the states vary. In the coordinates of the frame, the lexical basis writes a prediction into the stream more selectively than the readout, and dif- ferences of coefficients attribute it to layers and sublayers more faithfully than attribution patching. We therefore expect lexical reference frames to be a strong alternative to lenses in these applications. In summary, this paper makes three contributions. • We show that the layers that remain give every hidden state its own frame in the vocabulary, and we prove that no lens with one map per layer can follow this frame across the different contexts in which a state occurs. • We define frames that read a hidden state in the model’s native semantics through its own layers with a chosen amount of context, and we compute them in closed form from unla- belled text, without any training. • We show on two model families and their train- ing checkpoints that these frames follow the model from the middle layers on, where lenses fail, and support early exit, steering and attri- bution at every layer of the model. 2
