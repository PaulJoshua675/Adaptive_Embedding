Several key observations follow. 1) The agent-inference phase consistently converges in fewer total generations than the corresponding agent-training phase across all benchmark functions, confirming that eliminating the warm-up period directly translates into earlier effective search onset. This validates the core premise of the two-phase design, the agent-training phase functions as a one-time investment in policy learning, after which the trained agent can be deployed with immediate, unsupervised effectiveness, demonstrating strong generalization and intelligence from a single 40-episode training realization to diverse problem instances with randomized initializations. 2) Although this generation reduction is consistent across all functions, its magnitude is in most cases smaller than the 5,000 generations corresponding to the full warm-up period, with reductions of approxi- mately 3,000 generations observed for Rastrigin and approximately 900 generations for Rosenbrock. This indicates that the inference phase does not simply recover the warm-up overhead and nothing more, since the absence of online critic feedback introduces a degree of action noise relative to the fully supervised training setting, meaning the trained policy, while generally effective, does not always produce identically optimal generation-wise decisions across independent runs. The moderate standard deviations in total generation and function evaluation counts, notably lower than their training-phase counterparts, confirm that this variability remains well controlled and that the inference phase is stable across all benchmark functions. 3) The agent-inference phase is consistently the fastest per generation among all evaluated configurations, including the agent-training phase and the three adaptive EA baselines, though the source of this advantage differs across comparisons. Relative to the training phase, the gain is structural and substantial. Removing the critic forward pass, temporal-difference target computation, and actor-critic weight updates leaves only a single actor forward pass and standard population evaluation, eliminating the entire RL update overhead that the training phase must otherwise carry at every generation. Relative to the adaptive EA baselines, this efficiency advantage is more modest, mainly stemming from the actor’s forward pass being lighter than the adaptive bookkeeping, such as success-history memory and archive maintenance, that JADE, LSHADE, and LSHADE-SPACMA carry out every generation. To summarize, these results demonstrate that DRL-DCO achieves a meaningful and practically useful separation between its learning and deployment costs. The trained policy generalizes reliably across independently initialized runs on all five benchmark functions, producing convergence performance that is both faster per generation than the training phase and competitive with, or superior to, the best non-DRL baselines in total generation cost, all without requiring online adaptation or feedback during deployment. This confirms that the learned orchestration paradigm offers genuine operational advantages beyond training, and that the intelligence acquired during agent-training translates into a self-contained, supervision-free optimization capability.
