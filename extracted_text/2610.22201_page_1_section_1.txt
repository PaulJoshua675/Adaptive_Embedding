Modern AI systems increasingly face tasks that require composing multiple specialized capabilities— dynamically routing queries across varying LLM sizes to optimize inference costs, gathering and synthesizing evidence from disparate sources, or dispatching subtasks to external tools. The domi- nant paradigm centralizes this composition in a single controller (an “orchestrator” LLM or a fixed computation graph), which must explicitly plan the sequence of operations and assign credit to each component (Hong et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2023). An alternative, inspired by economic theory, is to let a society of specialized agents coordinate through a market mechanism. To solve temporal credit assignment across multi-step trajectories without hierarchical oversight, we draw on the foundational Bucket Brigade principle (Bartholdi III and Eisenstein, 1996; Holland, 1985): each agent purchases the current task state from the preceding agent in an auction, transforms the state, and resells the updated state to the next downstream agent. Terminal task rewards earned at completion naturally propagate backward through the chain of resale prices, rewarding upstream contributors proportionally to their marginal value added. To ground this decentralized economy in a reinforcement learning framework, we build on the work of Chang et al. (2020). They proved that a Vickrey (second-price) auction perfectly aligns each agent’s locally optimal bidding strategy with the globally optimal MDP policy. However, translating this theoretical breakthrough to real-world AI societies reveals a critical gap: their optimal equilibrium relies on the restrictive assumption of perfect competition via artificially cloned agents.
