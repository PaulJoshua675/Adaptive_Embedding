A detailed understanding of human geometry and motion is important across many fields, including robotics, extended reality, entertainment, healthcare, sports analytics, and sign language understand- ing. Yet people remain challenging for general dynamic scene reconstruction systems because their motion combines body articulation with the independent deformation of clothing and hair. Following these surfaces through a video requires identifying the same points as they move, become occluded and reappear. Reconstructing each frame independently does not establish this correspondence. We study dense 4D human correspondence, the task of matching points across the body, clothing and hair in different images and recovering their three-dimensional trajectories. Human correspondence methods commonly map image pixels to a shared body template (Güler et al., 2018). Such coordinates describe the underlying body, but do not directly represent the geometry and independent motion of loose garments and hair. In parallel, many 4D dynamic scene reconstruction systems jointly estimate geometry, motion and cameras across diverse scenes (Sucar et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). We introduce Dense4D for dense 4D human correspondence without a body template. The model establishes correspondence on the person without jointly reconstructing the surrounding scene or predicting camera poses. Dense4D follows a top-down approach: we isolate each person in the scene and jointly process their crops across time (Fig. 2). Using the focal length and person mask, a known virtual-camera warp reorients each crop towards the person, and the crop zoom is supplied to the network. A Human Point Map (HPM) assigns each person pixel the 3D position of its observed surface point at a selected reference time, expressed in the reference crop’s camera coordinates. These maps support amodal correspondence to the reference surface, including regions hidden in the reference image but observed in other frames. Their union samples this reference-time geometry (Fig. 1, right). Where methods such as V-DPM predict point motion in a shared camera frame (Sucar et al., 2026), Dense4D identifies corresponding pixels by matching HPM coordinates across person-centred crops. The learned mapping relates observations of the person with known crop transforms and zoom, without also predicting the person’s displacement through the scene. Each matched pixel is lifted with the depth predicted for its image to recover the 3D track position. External scene geometry and camera poses are used separately to place the reconstructed person and tracks in world coordinates. We use the image encoder and aggregator architecture of Wang et al. (2026). An additional crop token conditions the aggregator, which alternates attention within and across frames. Two dense heads predict HPMs and per-image depth in a single forward pass. Training uses human geometry and correspondence supervision from synthetic motion and multi-view renderings of real scans. Across articulated motion and clothing deformation, Dense4D brings observations from different frames into coherent reference-time human geometry, with visibly aligned body parts and fine surface details (Sec. 4.4). On real footage, Dense4D recovers more accurate human trajectories and better matches the scanned reference geometry than the methods we compare against (Secs. 4.2 and 4.3). The shared reference representation also supports tracking across long intervals, as demonstrated on an 86 s video (Fig. 12). Our contributions are: • We introduce Dense4D, to our knowledge the first feed-forward model dedicated to template-free dense 4D human correspondence from a set of monocular RGB images. Its predictions assemble observations of the body, clothing and hair into a coherent reference-time geometry, including regions hidden in the reference image. • We decouple correspondence estimation from camera-pose and scene-motion recovery. Dense4D supports direct matching across long temporal intervals and integrates with scene reconstruction through shared image pixels. • We introduce an amodal 3D localisation protocol on 4D-DRESS, measuring how closely observa- tions across time align with the full reference-time scan.
