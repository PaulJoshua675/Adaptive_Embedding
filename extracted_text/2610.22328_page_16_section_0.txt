We crop the box of each anchor from the source and from the final image, grey out in both crops the parts that fall inside the box of any edited object, and compute the DINOv2 [35] distance (one minus the cosine similarity of the CLS features) between the two crops. Because the boxes are coarse, an anchor can lie largely inside the box of an edited object (e.g., a shirt worn by a person who is edited), leaving too little of it to compare. We therefore use an anchor only if at least 30% of its box remains after the masking, and take a chain’s an- chor distance as the mean over its usable anchors; 148 of the 252 chains have at least one, and the anchor distance is averaged over these chains. We also compute LPIPS [60] between the source and the final image over the background, i.e., the whole image outside the boxes of all edited objects (a median of 57% of the image). Added objects are placed in this region, so the background distance should be read together with the instruction-following score. Editing. A model can preserve content trivially by not editing, so we check that the requested edits are carried out and survive the chain. The judge applies the instruction- following prompt: it sees the source, the final image and the chain’s ten instructions as one numbered list, and scores from 1 to 5 whether the requested changes are present and correct (5: all present and accurate; 3: partially; 1: not at all), leaving unrequested changes to the consistency score. As a negative control, showing the source image instead of the final image yields a score of 1 for all 20 chains. C. Long Sequences with Repeated Content Re- placement To distinguish sequence length from long-term preserva- tion demands, we consider a 40-edit sequence produced by the unmodified distilled FLUX.2 Klein 9B model (Fig. 7). Starting from a dog in a living room, we repeat four ed- its ten times: replace the dog with a cat, move the scene to a beach, replace the cat with a dog, and move the scene back to a living room. The subject and background are each replaced every two turns, so neither must retain its origi- nal details throughout the sequence. The outputs remain visually coherent through turn 40, without an obvious pro- gressive loss of visual quality. However, the animal’s ap- pearance and the room layout vary across cycles; returning to the same semantic categories does not imply recovery of the original image. This qualitative example shows that a long editing sequence need not exhibit progressive degra- dation when content is repeatedly replaced, and motivates evaluating stability in settings that require content to persist across many edits. D. Results on LME-Bench Table 3 reports results on LME-Bench [61] (Sec. 5.3) in MT-OPSD’s format. We score it with MT-OPSD’s evalu- ation code, using Gemma-4-31B in place of GPT-4o as for our other judged metrics, and show every image to the judge at 512 pixels. A turn succeeds if the judge rates both its in- struction following and its consistency above 6 out of 10; the success rate (SR) at turn k is the share of the 100 ses- sions whose turns 1 to k all succeed. The collapse rate (CR) at turn k is the share of sessions in which, by turn k, two consecutive turns have been judged destroyed (severe con- tent loss or surface corruption relative to the source). MT- OPSD and its base model are sampled as in Sec. 5.1, with- out upsampling; we run our model and its base model with their own 4-step sampler, also generating at 512 pixels and passing each turn’s output image to the next turn. Absolute numbers depend on the judge: MT-OPSD reports an SR at turn 10 of 0.38 for its FLUX model under GPT-4o, against 0.10 under our judge, so Table 3 is not comparable with the numbers reported in [61]. Our model completes all ten turns in 53 of the 100 ses- sions, against 10 for MT-OPSD, and collapses in 2, against 16. Most of this gap comes from the backbone and its sampler, as our base model already completes 49 sessions. Compared with it, our model succeeds significantly more often at turns 5 and 8, but not at turn 10 (0.53 vs. 0.49), and the base model rarely collapses on this benchmark (0.04 at turn 10), leaving little room for improvement.
