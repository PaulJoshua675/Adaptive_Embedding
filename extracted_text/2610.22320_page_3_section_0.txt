Not Just a Subject 3 (1) Cross-Contextual Bridging: We hypothesize that semantic misalignment arises when long and complex prompts are encoded holistically, causing scene- varying attributes to be diluted [14,16]. Rather than encoding all information in a single prompt, we decouple the narrative into an identity prompt describing the subject and a scene prompt describing the scenario. For instance, “a woman with blond hair reading a book.” is split into an identity prompt (“a woman with blond hair.”) and a scene prompt (“a woman reading a book.”). However, this separation introduces a new challenge: since the scene prompt contains no appearance information, the subject generated within the scene has no way to reflect the actual target identity. To bridge this semantic gap without revert- ing to monolithic encoding, we preserve the scene sub-prompt as a semantic anchor and softly inject appearance information at the embedding level. Mo- tivated by the strong generative influence and summarization ability of ⟨EOS⟩ embeddings [20, 37], we interpret it not merely as a terminal token, but as a residual-based semantic control point for scene-aware storytelling (Sec. 3.1). Un- like prior EOS-based methods that substitute the ⟨EOS⟩embedding between prompts differing by a single word (e.g., an object class) [9, 20], we capture a phrase-level appearance difference and infuse it into the scene’s ⟨EOS⟩, reflecting rich multi-attribute cues without explicit word-level repetition (Sec. 3.2). This provides the scene image with the specified appearance cues at the semantic level, enabling more effective feature-level identity transfer. (2) Adaptive Identity Preservation: To mitigate subject inflexibility induced by spatially blind feature transfer, we introduce a spatially adaptive identity injection mechanism that better aligns identity features with the scene- specific layout (Sec. 3.3). We observe that the scale mismatch between identity and scene subjects prevents various identity tokens from corresponding to the scene tokens. As a result, only a few identity tokens are repeatedly transferred to the scene and cause structural artifacts. Motivated by this, we apply Iden- tity Anchoring, which adaptively resizes identity features to match the intended framing and scale of the scene. This enforces structural compatibility between identity and scene representations, reducing scale-induced distortions (Fig. 10b). We then refine the integration through Identity-Aware Self-Attention, which en- ables scene features to selectively attend to subject features, building upon [30]. Unlike existing spatially blind transfer strategies [17,30,40], our approach sup- ports scene-conditioned variation in subject structure (e.g., viewpoint changes) while preserving core identity characteristics. To evaluate scene-specific struc- tural variance, we introduce Consistory+_+, extending Consistory+ [17] with diverse framing prompts [15] to simulate the realistic framing shifts of real- world storytelling. We validate our method on Consistory+ and Consistory+_+, demonstrating consistently improved performance under framing variations. Our contributions are as follows: – We show that the ⟨EOS⟩embedding can carry phrase-level, multi-attribute information, enabling appearance cues to be infused during generation. – We propose EOS-Story, a training-free framework with two modules: Cross- Contextual Bridging (text-space semantic recomposition) and Adaptive Identity Preservation (vision-space scale-aware transfer).
