Nearest neighbor data structures. Spatial indexing methods for nearest neighbor search can be organized along two axes: exact versus approximate, and coordinate-based versus general metric space. Among exact methods for coordinate spaces, KD-trees [3] achieve O(log n) query time in low dimensions but degrade to O(n) as dimensionality grows, and their axis-aligned splits tie them to a coordinate representation; ball trees [4] improve on KD-trees through hierarchical ball decompo- sition, extending efficient search to moderate dimensions; because they partition by distance to cen- ters rather than along axes, they apply to any proper metric. For general metric spaces, vantage-point trees [5] use binary partitions around single pivot points but offer only O(n) worst-case query time. Navigating nets [6] introduced the theoretical framework of the doubling dimension c (also called the “expansion constant”), which cover trees [7] made practical with an O(c6 log n) query algorithm, later improved by Izbicki and Shelton [8] with simplified and nearest ancestor variants(though a history of nuanced theoretical runtime errors and fixes [9–12] is beyond this study), tree merging for parallel construction, and cache-optimized packing. On the approximate side, locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) [13] provides sublinear search but requires metric-specific hash families, while hier- archical navigable small world graphs (HNSW) [14] achieve state-of-the-art recall–speed tradeoffs for approximate search but offer no exactness guarantees. Cover trees are a practical structure com- bining exact search, arbitrary metric spaces, and query complexity parametrized by intrinsic rather than ambient dimensionality. They are not alone in that combination—navigating nets, cited above, share it—but they are the variant with the broadest empirical track record, which is why we take them as our case study. 2
