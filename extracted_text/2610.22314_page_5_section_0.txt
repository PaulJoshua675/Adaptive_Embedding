(3) MHQR ranges from 0 to 10, with lower scores indicating lower average weighted severity. 3.4 Validation For evaluation, we prompt an LLM with MHQR and compute the model score according to Section 3.3. For this, clinicians and LLM judges must apply MHQR consistently. We present an analysis on the corre- lations between human judgments as well as on ranking consistency. Since we want to compute rankings at an acceptable cost we also perform a cost analysis. Our analysis is based on 67 conversation excerpts (19 complete conversations, and 48 excerpts of five turns within a conversation) resulting in a total of 411 counsellor turns. These conversations are ob- tained from three patient simulators (MindSim, MindEval (Pombal et al., 2025) and Patient-Ψ (Wang et al., 2024)) and three counsellor LMs (GPT-5.6 Terra (OpenAI, 2026a), GLM-5.2 (GLM-5 Team, 2026) and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026c)) together with real sessions from an online mental health app. Three clinical psychologists (see Ethics Statement) annotated each excerpt turn by turn, without knowing which system had produced it. For each counsellor message they gave a severity level and the corresponding issues. As judges, we experiment with GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI, 2026b), GPT-6 Astra (Ope- nAI, 2026c), Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, 2026b), Hy4-preview (Tencent Hy Team, 2026) and DeepSeek- V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026). Inter-Annotator Agreement. Ten of the above mentioned excerpts, corresponding to 66 counsellor turns, were annotated by all three annotators. On these turns the mean pairwise quadratically weighted κ of the turn-level severities is 0.44 (Table 2). Although exact agreement is only achieved in about half of the turns when pooling across the three annotator pairs, the disagreements are almost exclusively between adjacent severities, with 97.5% differing by at most one level, and only 2.5% are between ‘No concern’ and Major. Notably, agreement on individual clinical issues is lower (Appendix B). However, MHQR scores depend on the actual turn-level severities only. Furthermore, the remaining disagreement on turn-level severities averages out at a ranking level. Kendall’s W mea- sures rank concordance rather than chance-corrected agreement. Ranking Consistency. For every ex- cerpt and annotator, we compute the conversation score once for the anno- tator and once for five different judges. We then aggregate these scores for each of the counsellor LMs following Sec- tion 3.3. We compare the resulting rankings with Kendall’s τb. On their own excerpts the annotators rank the counsellor LMs identically, but they dif- fer in strictness. A2, for example, scores GPT-5.6-Terra at 1.47, whereas A1 and A3 score it at 0.82 and 0.66. We there- fore use the pooled annotations as the reference, which averages out individual calibrations and also roughly doubles the number of excerpts per counsellor LM. All five judges reproduce the pooled ranking of the three counsellors (τb = 1; Ta- ble 3). With only three counsellors, this covers three pairwise model comparisons. Against individual annotators, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra reproduce all three rankings, whereas the remaining judges swap two counsellor LMs for only one annotator. Hy4 and DeepSeek V4.1-Flash also achieve signifi- cantly lower pooled issue recalls and F1-scores, see Appendix B. While the severity F1 score is an impor- tant metric for model ranking in MHQR, under-reporting clinical issues rules them out as judges as they would seriously overestimate model quality when compared to annotators. To analyse ranking stability, we bootstrap 2,000 excerpts resamples for each counsellor LM and report the fraction in which the judge rankings equal the annotator’s. In addition, we bootstrap from the annotator pool and report the fraction in which the ranking is consistent with the one on all excerpts. GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra reproduce the ranking in 87% and 89% of the samples, close to the stability of the annotators themselves (90%). 5
