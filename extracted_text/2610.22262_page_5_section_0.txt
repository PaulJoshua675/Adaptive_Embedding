The analysis therefore does not assess vector-field topol- ogy, such as flow critical points or separatrices, which is a distinct direction. 4.2 Why differentiable proxies Directly optimizing persistence-diagram agreement requires con- structing the prediction’s diagram and matching it to the target dur- ing training. Such a procedure can be made piecewise differen- tiable by holding the current pairing and matching fixed and dif- ferentiating the matched cost with respect to the predicted values at critical vertices, as in prior work [5, 19]. In our setting, persis- tence diagrams are extracted by an external TTK computation that is not part of the PhIRE training graph, so this approach would re- quire extracting and matching a diagram for every prediction at ev- ery training step. We instead use auxiliary losses that are ordinary differentiable functions of the predicted speed field; topology en- ters training only through ground-truth locations that are computed once, before training. This keeps fine-tuning inside the existing training loop, but it also means that none of our losses optimizes dB, W2,q, or MT distance directly. Agreement under those distances is an empirical outcome that we measure. We also piloted a more direct formulation: a differentiable cubical-complex persistence-diagram Wasserstein loss applied in- side a small residual refiner. It made persistence supervision ac- tive during training but did not improve the final TTK-based PD or MT distances on the evaluation topology crop. Because that pilot differed from the final evaluation in crop size, model capacity, im- plementation framework, and diagram construction, we interpret it cautiously: it suggests that persistence-oriented losses need to be aligned with the evaluated scalar field and diagram convention, not that differentiable topology is ineffective. Fixed-pair supervision (Sec. 4.3) satisfies this alignment by construction, since its pairs come from the same TTK convention and the same fixed 160×160 topology crop used for evaluation. 4.3 Topology-inspired loss components We developed five auxiliary losses in roughly increasing order of topological specificity. The first three supervise the scalar field as a whole; the last two supervise values at locations selected from the ground-truth topology. Scalar speed. Lspeed = MSE(sSR,sGT) supervises the physical magnitude used by the topology evaluation, which Luv constrains only indirectly through the normalized components. Gradient magnitude. Lgrad = MSE(|∇sSR|,|∇sGT|) (6) penalizes errors in local ridges, fronts, and sharp transitions, coun- teracting the smoothing tendency of standard fine-tuning. Soft high-speed level sets. At thresholds T = {5,10,15} m/s, we define a differentiable occupancy mask Mτ(s) = σ(k(s−τ)) with k = 10, and compare predicted and ground-truth masks: Llevelset = 1 |T | ∑ τ∈T MSE(Mτ(sSR),Mτ(sGT)). (7) This term encourages agreement in the location and extent of high- speed regions across physically meaningful thresholds. High-speed local maxima. Our first attempt to target persistence-relevant critical values directly supervises prominent maxima without computing any persistence.
