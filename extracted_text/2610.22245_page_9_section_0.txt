Weak–strong disagreement. For each weak response yw,i, we define bd(p, yw,i) := 1 Ks Ks X k=1 A(yw,i, ys,k), i = 1, . . . , Kw. (9) This is the fraction of strong-model responses that disagree with yw,i, and hence provides an empirical estimate of the weak–strong disagreement probability Pr(Ys̸ = yw,i | P = p). Strong-model self-disagreement. We similarly define bg(p) := 1 Ks(Ks −1) X k̸=k′ A(ys,k, ys,k′). (10) This is the fraction of pairs of strong responses that disagree, and hence provides an empirical estimate of the strong-model self-disagreement probability Pr(Ys̸ = Y ′ s | P = p). The disagreement indicator A depends on the task and only needs to determine whether two responses are equivalent, rather than whether either response is correct. For example, it can be implemented using exact matching, task-specific parsing or execution, or an LLM judge. Importantly, A is used only offline to construct the training targets and is not required at test time. We train two predictors, bfd(p, yw) and bfg(p), to predict the targets in (9) and (10), respectively. The first predicts the probability that the strong model disagrees with a given weak response, while the second predicts the strong model’s self-disagreement from the prompt alone. In Appendix A.5, we provide implementation details for the predictors used in our experiments. At test time, we combine the two predictors according to the corrected-disagreement score: bSCD(hσ) = min i≤σ(p) bfd(p, yw,i) −bfg(p). (11) Thus, bSCD can be computed using only the prompt and the sampled weak-model responses.
