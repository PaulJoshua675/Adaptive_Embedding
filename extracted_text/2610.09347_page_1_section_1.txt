Genetic programming was conceived as a way of evolving solutions: the program is the answer, and fitness is the error of its own output. A substantial line of work instead makes the program an input to a separate learner, so that fitness measures the learner’s output rather than the program’s. Which learner to attach matters and depends on the problem, with no account of what decides it. The account has to concern the program: what makes a target hard for genetic programming is how much nonlinearity the program must build by composing primitives. Any nonlinearity the learner supplies, the program is spared from building. The quantity to measure is therefore how much of a target’s nonlinearity a learner can take over, and on continuous benchmarks it can only be estimated. Here we show that how much the program must still build is decided by which learner is attached, and is measurable on the programs themselves. Moving to Boolean domains, where a target’s nonlinearity is exactly its Fourier degree, we tune that degree from one to six with everything else fixed. Conditioned on success, a linear learner forces the evolved program to the target’s degree exactly, at one through six without a single exception over thirty runs per setting, while tree ensembles succeed with far simpler programs and four times as often. This gives the field two things. A learner can be chosen from a target’s structure instead of its reputation for difficulty. Our control target has lower degree than the parity problems yet gains nothing from a nonlinear learner, while targets reducible to a simple statistic gain a great deal. And the degree of the evolved program is a diagnostic free to compute during any run, showing whether the learner is contributing nothing or is carrying the model. The latter comes with a warning: evolution internalises what the learner supplies in only four of sixty-three conditions, and grows more dependent on it in forty-four. The more capable the learner, the less of the model is left legible in the program. 1
