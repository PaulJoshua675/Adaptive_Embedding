Between its input and output tokens, a language model computes nothing but states of its residual stream, which every layer reads and writes (Elhage et al., 2021). Its vocabulary is in this sense the na- tive semantics of the model, the only units of mean- ing that it is designed to receive and to express. The model defines the lexical meaning of a state at the two ends of the stream: the embedding assigns each token a state, and the unembedding maps a final state to logits over tokens. Intermediate states lack 1https://github.com/jianguda/lexframe such a definition, although interpretability research and its applications read them constantly: early exit and self-speculative decoding stop the computation at a layer whose reading already names the output (Schuster et al., 2022; Elhoushi et al., 2024), hal- lucination detectors judge an answer by its hidden states (Orgad et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), and knowledge editors locate and rewrite facts at in- termediate layers (Meng et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2025). Each depends on how faithfully a tool such as a lens reads these states, and model compression, which distils a smaller or pruned student by match- ing the intermediate states of its teacher, relies on what these states mean (Romero et al., 2015; Das- gupta and Cohn, 2025; Muralidharan et al., 2024). This meaning is set by the remaining layers, which read each state in the context of its prefix. Two families of methods address these states and fail in opposite ways. The first reads a state with a lens: the logit lens applies the output frame to every layer unchanged (nostalgebraist, 2020), the tuned lens learns one affine map per layer fitted to the model’s output (Belrose et al., 2023), and the Jacobian lens averages the Jacobian of the re- maining layers over positions and prompts (Gurnee et al., 2026). Each fixes one linear map per layer, so every state of a layer is read identically, regardless of its prefix. The second family keeps every state and does without a reading. It treats the states of a layer as a point cloud and reports its rank, intrinsic dimension, anisotropy or similarity to other layers (Kornblith et al., 2019; Ansuini et al., 2019; Gar- rido et al., 2022; Skean et al., 2025). Such statistics describe how the states are distributed rather than how the model uses them: two states close in the cloud may lead to different predictions, and two distant ones to the same.
