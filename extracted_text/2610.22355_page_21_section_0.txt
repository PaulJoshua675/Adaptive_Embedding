We explicitly instruct the verifier through the system prompt to generate sub-questions within three ranges: at most 10, 11–20, and 21–30. These results suggest that an appropriate decomposition granularity improves scoring accuracy, while simply increasing the number of sub-questions does not guarantee better performance. Excessive decomposition can introduce redundant questions or requirements unsupported by the original prompt. For example, given “A red apple is placed on a table,” the verifier may repeatedly ask whether an apple exists or introduce questions such as “Is the apple a fruit?” and “Is the apple round?” that do not correspond to explicitly requested visual constraints. Such questions can distort the aggregated score by repeatedly weighting the same requirement or introducing irrelevant criteria. We also observe that generating more sub-questions can lead to violations of the required output format, reducing the reliability of the final score. Together, these observations highlight the importance of balancing fine-grained coverage with faithful and non-redundant decomposition. C Limitations and Future Works Our world-modeling evaluation is restricted to game-based environments with explicit rules, where the ground-truth image is uniquely determined by the initial task state and rule instructions. We adopt these controlled tasks primarily to evaluate the effectiveness of our pairwise verification paradigm. Although our results demonstrate the advantages of pairwise verification in these settings, these tasks capture only part of the complexity and diversity of real-world environments. In future work, we plan to extend our investigation to open-ended settings with non-unique ground truths, construct larger and more realistic world-modeling datasets, and scale both the verifier and the generator to stronger backbones. Our long-term goal is to develop multimodal generative models that can reason about, simulate, and interact with complex real-world scenes.
