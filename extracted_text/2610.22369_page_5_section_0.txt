We ask whether there is more to this signal, not whether the cached residual can still be used, but for how long. Instead of reading Ai against τ as a hard threshold, we read how far below it the accumulator sits, which says how much of the reuse budget is left, and we let that quantity set the length of the next step. We define the headroom Hi = 1 −Ai τ , (8) which lies in [0, 1] at a cached node. Reusing the anchor’s residual costs a relative reuse error Ei = ∥ri −ra∥/∥ra∥, between the residual the stack would produce at node i and the one Eq. (5) substitutes for it. We measure it on uncached FLUX trajectories, where ri is available at every node (Fig. 2a): the error rises as the accumulator fills, and a node just past a refresh differs from one about to trigger the next by 2.2× in median error, although the threshold treats the two alike (Sec. C.1). The measurement says which nodes can carry the residual further, not how much further, so any rule that takes longer steps where the error is lower is consistent with it. We choose the simplest one. Freezing the same enlarged steps at even, back-loaded or random positions instead does worse at the slower operating point (Tab. C.4.3). A.3). 4.2 SAMPLING BETWEEN NODES A jump ends at a time the grid does not contain, and the node the sampler lands on is one no schedule placed. Like every cached node it reuses ra, and the further a node sits from the anchor the worse that reuse gets. A jump puts it further than any scheduled step does. Instead of holding ra, we extend it along the line through the two most recent fresh residuals, the residual motion of the cache: bri = ra + β λi (ra −ra−1), λi = ti −ta ta −ta−1 , (10) a straight line through the last two anchors, followed a fraction β of the way. The ratio λi is built from time gaps, so it is defined wherever the sampler is, on the grid or off it. Predictive caches that count steps instead have a residual one node later, two nodes later, and nothing in between. We apply Eq. (10) at every cached node, whether or not the node was reached by a jump. It costs one more stored tensor, ra−1, and no evaluation. The nodes ahead of the landing also need attention. The jump has passed several of them, and the first it has not passed may be a fraction of a scheduled step away, so following the old grid would spend a full node on a fraction of a step. The time the step ended at becomes ti+1, and the nodes after it are replaced. Writing mi for the number of nodes of the old grid still ahead of ti+1, we spread that many nodes evenly over [ti+1, 1], counting ti+1 as the first of them, ti+1+k = ti+1 + (1 −ti+1) k mi −1, k = 1, . . . , mi −1.
