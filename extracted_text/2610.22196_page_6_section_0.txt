For each window, eight rollouts begin from independent copies of the same parent bank. They write all chunks, are examined with reward questions, and receive per-chunk scores. The highest-scoring final bank is promoted to the next window in the same sequence. This is on-policy at two levels: tokens are sampled from the current writer, and future prompts are generated from banks produced by that writer. 5 Reward Construction: From Failure Surfaces to Attributed Feedback 5.1 Why benchmark questions are not enough Using only final questions from a long-memory benchmark creates a low-density learning problem. A long interaction sequence may contain hundreds of useful facts but only a few questions. An unasked write receives no evidence, and a failed answer does not identify whether the cause was omission, bad wording, stale-state confusion, retrieval failure, or reader failure. Binary exact match further collapses semantically partial answers and can eliminate variance within a small GRPO group (He et al., 2026). We construct reward items from the failure surface of memory writing, not merely from a list of benchmark answers. 5.2 Chronological segmentation Interaction sequences retain session boundaries and chronological order. Each session is segmented independently into chunks of roughly ten turns, so a chunk never crosses a session boundary. The resulting chunks are flattened into one chronological stream; up to five consecutive chunks form one RL window, which may span sessions. Windows from the same sequence are consumed in order, and the selected policy-written bank from one window initializes the next. This preserves long-range state transitions while keeping each optimization step bounded. 5.3 Two annotation passes Local write quality and long-range memory utility require different context, so the two reward types are annotated in separate passes (Table 3). Why the PRM pass sees the two preceding chunks. A per-chunk probe must test information that chunk t itself introduced. Annotating chunk t in isolation fails in two ways. Coreference: mentions such as “that project” or “the same reviewer” cannot be resolved, so the annotator cannot write self-contained questions about them. Novelty: the annotator cannot tell whether a statement is new in chunk t or repeats something already established, so probes would credit chunk t for facts it did not introduce. The extracted facts of chunks t−1 and t−2 supply the referents and the recently established state. They are given as facts rather than raw text and serve as context only: no probe is generated from them, and they earn no credit for chunk t. 6
