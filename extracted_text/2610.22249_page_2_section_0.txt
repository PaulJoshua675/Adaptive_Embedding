When model produc- tion is studied, the usual measure is frequency, with words and phrases that are over-represented relative to human text treated as targets for suppres- sion (Juzek and Ward, 2025; Paech et al., 2025). Frequency answers the last of Hymes’s questions, how often an expression is in fact produced. For expressions with a legitimate function, it conflates appropriate and inappropriate uses, and suppres- sion removes both. Whether models command ap- propriateness itself remains to be examined. We examine this question through corrective negation, which denies one alternative and asserts another in its place. Its English form, it’s not X, it’s Y, is regularly cited as a hallmark of chatbot writing (Oremus, 2026), and Chinese online commentary singles out the Mandarin bushi X, ershi Y (‘not X, but Y’; 不是X，而是Y) in the same way, chiefly because the rejected X often appears to be a po- sition that no one has taken.1 Corrective negation is also the ordinary way to correct a misconcep- tion. Each use of the construction thus raises the two questions behind Grice’s maxims (Figure 1): whether the context made X a live alternative (rel- evance), and whether rejecting X contributes infor- mation not already established (quantity). We ask whether models, like competent speakers, know when the construction is called for. Linguistic theory makes these questions precise as conditions on licensing. As a construction, bushi X, ershi Y conventionally pairs its form with a corrective function (Goldberg, 2006; Shao and Wang, 2010; Zuo, 2025). The correction presup- poses alternatives that contrast along a shared di- mension (Rooth, 1992), and X need not have been asserted by anyone as long as it is accessible in the common ground (Toosarvandani, 2013; Stalnaker, 2002). What is rejected may be a proposition or the manner in which it is expressed, as in metalinguis- tic negation (Horn, 1985), and whether the correc- tion is informative depends on the question under discussion (Roberts, 2012). These accounts sepa- rate three cases that are formally identical: a cor- rection that resolves a live alternative, one that is well formed but unneeded, and one that is defective because it rejects something true or misattributes a view. 1For example, https://blog.darkthread.net/ blog/ai-slop-not-but-pattern/. We introduce a diagnostic framework built around an ordered decision tree (Figure 2) that sorts each use of the construction into these three cases, first screening out defective uses and then testing the relevance and quantity conditions; a use that passes both is substantive, and one that fails either is optional. We apply the tree to an- swers from five commercial models on Chinese prompts and compare their usage with a human reference. We then investigate the sources of this preference, scoring each answer against minimally edited counterparts with reward models and an LLM judge, and tracing the construction’s fre- quency across training stages of open-weight mod- els and in public preference data. For a substantial share of uses, models do not. They use the construction 2.5 to 9.2 times as densely as the human reference, and the short- fall lies in appropriateness more than in correct- ness, with only 1.30% of annotated uses defective and 52.8% classified as optional. Several reward models favor the construction even when variants match in general quality, and an LLM judge fa- vors it even where the context does not license it. Along the training pipelines of open-weight mod- els, both supervised fine-tuning and preference op- timization tend to raise its frequency. Our contributions are as follows: • We formalize stylistic tics in LLM-generated text as a failure of appropriateness in the sense of communicative competence. • We distinguish uses that are correct from uses that are needed, and operationalize this dis- tinction as a decision tree derived from lin- guistic theory. • We provide a method for diagnosing how re- ward models and training stages shape the preference for such expressions. 2
