We report it together with the proportion of adjacent pairs that cost more than one bit. Every other encoding considered has a severity of exactly 1, and none of its adjacent pairs costs more than one bit: no pair of values adjacent under its metric, including the wraparound pair of the ring codes, differs in more than a single bit, by construction. Binary has a severity of 8, with 49.8% of adjacent pairs costing more than one bit. The cliff is therefore not an isolated defect at the power-of-two boundary but the tail of a geometric distribution, in which half of all adjacent pairs cost one bit, a quarter cost two, an eighth three, and so on. Global monotonicity Global monotonicity is measured by Spearman’s rank correlation 𝜌𝑠between semantic distance and Hamming distance, taken over all (256 2 ) pairs of values: each pair con- tributes one observation, its semantic distance 𝑑𝑆(𝑖, 𝑗) and the distance 𝐻(𝑐𝑖, 𝑐𝑗) between its code words. Spearman’s coefficient is the Pearson correlation of the ranks of the two quantities (Spearman, 1904); it equals 1 when Hamming distance increases monotonically with semantic distance, 0 when the two are unrelated, and it does not assume the relation is linear, which matters because only Thermometer and Johnson make it so. Hamming distances are heavily tied, since many pairs of values sit at the same distance, so the tie- corrected form is used throughout. Each encoding is scored against its own metric (Section 2.2.2), 𝑑cyc for the ring codes and 𝑑lin for the rest; scored against the other, Thermometer and Johnson would both fall from 1.000 to 0.747. Global monotonicity separates the encodings that cliff severity does not. Thermometer and Johnson reach 𝜌𝑠= 1.000 against 𝑑lin and 𝑑cyc respectively; the four hybrids evaluated here retain between 0.980 and 0.998; Binary scores 0.323 and Gray only 0.374. Gray’s reflected con- struction also makes it cyclic, but scored against 𝑑cyc it does barely better, at 0.392. Gray therefore removes the cliff entirely while carrying little information about semantic separation, the distinction anticipated in Section 2.4. Neighbourhood quality Neighbourhood quality asks what a code’s local region contains. Around each anchor we take the Hamming ball of radius 𝛼𝑊, with a floor of one bit, meaning every code word within that many bits of the anchor, and count how many disjoint runs of consecutive values it holds; the result is averaged over all anchors. The radius is chosen to match the network’s behaviour: by the tolerance law of Section 4.4, a ball of radius 𝛼𝑊is what the network accepts at a gain of 1∕(1 −2𝛼) times its minimum. The text quotes 𝛼= 10%, which corresponds to a gain of 1.25, at the low end of the range swept on hardware, where the network has only just begun to tolerate mismatch.
