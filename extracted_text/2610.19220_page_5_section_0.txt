What training adds. Attraction itself comes with the architecture: an untrained CTM also has ρ(J) < 1 at every fixed point (median 0.05), as does a random core—the trained backbone with the recurrence at initialization, so the recurrence sees the same image features (median 0.24). Against this matched-input control, training lowers the spectral radius (0.24 →0.15) and raises the non-normality σmax(J)/ρ(J) from about 7 to about 23, while the untrained map is a plain Euclidean contraction (σmax(J) < 1 everywhere). Training also concentrates each neuron model’s sensitivity on the newest entry of its pre-activation window, where zt enters (2.6× its uniform share, against uniform at initialization), so the contraction is not an artifact of zt entering only one of M window slots. One tick behind. Because the memory changes with every tick, the target moves, and the trajectory is simply the curve these fixed points trace, followed one update behind (Fig. 2b). The state is not on the current fixed point but on the previous one: the mean distance ∥zt −z⋆ t−k∥is minimized at k = 1 and is then seven times smaller than the distance to the current fixed point (5.9 versus 41.4, over 128 images; Fig. 2c). The offset points backwards along the fixed point’s own motion (median cos(zt −z⋆ t , z⋆ t−1 −z⋆ t ) = 0.995) and measures a full step of it (median ratio 1.01), so the state trails the moving attractor by one update rather than sitting on it (Fig. 3 shows the trailing state). This lag is largely structural: zt is one contraction step from z⋆ t−1, and the shrink factor across one step (median 0.11 over 1536 image–tick pairs) is what ρ(J) at the same fixed points predicts (median 0.14); what is not structural is how far the fixed point moves each tick, which keeps the state a full step behind (App. A). 5
