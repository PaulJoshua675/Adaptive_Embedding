To address this gap, we define scale sensitiv- ity as the degree to which LLM-generated scalar outputs vary in response to changes in rating scale design. We systematically examine three design elements of the rating scale: format (continuous vs. ordinal), range (e.g., 3-, 5-, 11-, and 21-point scales), and sign (signed scales centered at 0 vs. unsigned scales containing only non-negative val- ues). We assess scale sensitivity across tasks with different conceptual foundations (political leaning, toxicity, and sentiment), prompt designs (zero-shot vs. rubric-based), and models. We further distinguish between two forms of scale sensitivity: numerical sensitivity and se- mantic sensitivity. Numerical sensitivity cap- tures whether a model assigns different normal- ized scores to the same item when the rating scale changes, whereas semantic sensitivity captures whether those changes switch the substantive inter- pretation of the output. Using the experimental design shown in Figure 1, we find systematic evidence of both numerical and semantic scale sensitivity. Specifically, changing one rating scale design element shifts normalized scores by about 0.10 and changes the category of roughly one in five texts. The toxicity scoring task shows the greatest sensitivity, particularly to scale sign. Compared with zero-shot prompting, rubric- based prompting does not reduce sensitivity. Our paper makes three key contributions. • We conceptualize and empirically characterize scale sensitivity as an underexamined source of variation in LLM-based measurement. • We demonstrate that scale sensitivity has di- rect implications for the comparability, repli- cability, and validity of LLM-scored measure- ments. • We provide practical guidance for more reli- able LLM-based text scoring. We recommend robustness checks across alternative rating scales, choosing scales that match the struc- ture of the construct, and assessing agreement with suitable human labels, when available, as evidence of validity. 2
