C.4 Memory stress test In this section, we share additional implementation details for the memory stress test evaluation and the results presented in Table 3. We use the StreamArena (Zhang et al., 2026d) Historical Retrospection (HR) split as the benchmark, which has 877 questions across L1–L4 over 237 videos. L1 to L4 indicate the evidence-to-query gap of less than 5 min, between 5 and 15 min, between 15 and 30 min, and longer than 30 min, respectively. We compare MaLoW (Qwen3.5-9B) against strong streaming video understanding models: MOSS-VL- Realtime-11B (Wang et al., 2026), which retains the full historical context; VideoChat3-4B (Li et al., 2026) and AURA-8B (Lu et al., 2026), which operates with a local 16 / 30 -second observation window, respectively; VST-7B (Guan et al., 2026), which compresses the historical observation as text-based memory. For AURA and VST, we follow the official implementations in StreamArena to evaluate the model performance. For MOSS-VL-Realtime, VideoChat3, and MaLoW, they share the same question prompt derived from the prompt used by AURA. MaLoW (Qwen3.5-9B) samples video frames at 1fps with a maximum per-frame pixel budget of 262,144. The final two-second window is regarded as the current observation and will be sent to the VLMs, while the rest of the history frames will be sent to the MaLoW and converted into LoRA. The VideoChat3 uses 32 unique frames at 2 FPS from the latest 16 seconds, with an aspect-ratio-preserving maximum of 200,704 pixels per frame (448^ 2). MOSS-VL samples frames at 1 FPS and with maximum per-frame pixel cap as 262,144 pixels. VST uses up to 384 causal-prefix frames at 1 FPS with aspect-ratio- preserving resolution determined dynamically by its 8,192-visual-token-per-segment budget. AURA takes most recent 30 seconds video at 1 FPS as input, with per-frame max pixel 602,112 pixel. C.5 Streaming Inference Speed Profiling We provide additional details on the inference-speed profiling reported in Figure 4. We measure the streaming inference latency of MaLoW and four baselines on 100 deterministically sampled OVO-Bench (Niu et al., 2025) questions spanning a wide range of history lengths. Video durations range from 8 to 1,117 seconds, with a median of 153.5 seconds, a mean of 193.3 seconds, and a 90th percentile of 312 seconds. This set includes both short clips and videos exceeding 10 minutes, enabling evaluation of long-context streaming efficiency. At each query timestamp t, we measure synchronized GPU computation required to process the current observation and answer the query under each method’s native streaming protocol. Video decoding, resizing, and other input preprocessing are excluded. For MaLoW, each streaming step includes encoding the current observation, updating the hidden state and generating the LoRA adapter for the next time step, applying the adapter generated for the current time step, prefilling the model with the question and the two most recent frames from [t - 2, t], and decoding the first output token. For SimpleStream (Qwen3.5-9B), the model receives only the question and two frames sampled from the most recent two-second window, [t - 2, t], without earlier visual history. The measured latency includes prefilling with these inputs and decoding the first output token. For Qwen3.5-9B with full context, the measured latency includes prefilling the question together with the complete video history from the start of the stream to t, sampled at 1 FPS, followed by decoding the first output token. For VideoChat3, we follow its official streaming implementation. At each query, the model re-prefills the question together with a sliding window of the most recent 32 frames, corresponding to approximately 16 seconds at 2 FPS, before generating the streaming response.
