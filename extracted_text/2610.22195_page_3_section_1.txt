2.1. Multi-Agent Orchestration Multi-agent orchestration methods vary in how they dis- tribute responsibilities between global task planning and local execution, and most designs favor one side of this tradeoff. Early frameworks such as AutoGen (Wu et al., 2024) and MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024) organize col- laboration through conversation patterns and role-based procedures that provide clear global structure but follow fixed interaction protocols. AFlow (Zhang et al., 2025b) and MaAS (Zhang et al., 2025a) reduce manual design effort by automating workflow and architecture construc- tion, while Conductor (Nielsen et al., 2026) and MAS- Orchestra (Ke et al., 2026) learn to generate orchestration decisions through reinforcement learning. Although these methods can discover effective coordination patterns, the resulting structures are determined before or independently of task execution. Runtime delegation shifts toward local adaptability by al- lowing an orchestrator to assign work as execution proceeds, with Magentic-One (Fourney et al., 2024) and AOrches- tra (Ruan et al., 2026) managing task progress through agent feedback. Kimi K2.5 (Kimi Team, 2026a) and Apodex 1.1 (Apodex Team, 2026) further support parallel subagents dynamically dispatched by a main agent, and such dispatch decisions can also be learned through policies for agent selection and tool invocation (Dang et al., 2025; Su et al., 2026). However, these approaches couple task decomposi- tion, subtask dispatch, and result synthesis within a single orchestrator, without explicitly separating global planning from local execution management. Workforce, introduced with OWL (Hu et al., 2025a), addresses this by separating a domain-agnostic planner from a central coordinator that manages worker assignments and task dependencies. HTN- Orchestrate shares this separation but assigns a dedicated Orchestrator to each executable leaf subtask, while the Plan- ner maintains and incrementally expands the task network, allowing local coordination within each subtask to operate independently of global planning over the task hierarchy. 2.2. Task Decomposition The tension between local execution and global planning is particularly evident in task decomposition, where the chal- lenge is determining not only how to break a task apart but also when to revise that breakdown in response to execution findings. Static approaches commit to a full decomposition before observing results. Least-to-most prompting (Zhou et al., 2023) generates subproblems before solving them sequentially, and Agent-Oriented Planning (Li et al., 2025) accounts for agent capabilities when decomposing and allo- cating tasks. D-CORE (Xu et al., 2026) uses reinforcement learning to incentivize decomposition reasoning in large reasoning models, and Select-Then-Decompose (Liu et al., 2025) chooses a decomposition strategy according to the task. These methods improve the quality of decomposition but still finalize it before execution begins. When intermediate findings affect subsequent work, decom- position must also adapt during execution. Plan-and-Act (Er- dogan et al., 2025) trains models on planning trajectories and revises the plan after each executor step, ADaPT (Prasad et al., 2024) recursively decomposes tasks that resist direct execution, ReCAP (Zhang et al., 2025d) revises the remain- ing plan using execution results, and ReAcTree (Choi et al., 2026) organizes execution through hierarchical agent trees. TodoEvolve (Liu et al., 2026) extends adaptation to the planning architecture itself by learning to generate and re- vise task-specific planning systems. These methods allow plans to evolve but provide limited support for explicitly modeling precedence dependencies among subtasks. HTN- Orchestrate uses prerequisite results to inform the further decomposition of dependent subtasks, deferring decisions about their internal structure until the relevant evidence be- comes available. 2.3. Hierarchical Task Networks and Dependency-Aware Planning Explicit dependencies connect global planning with local execution by determining which results must be available before subsequent work can proceed. Hierarchical task net- works represent complex activities through task decompo- sition and constraints on the resulting subtasks (Erol et al., 1994), and recent work such as ChatHTN (Muñoz-Avila et al., 2025) combines this tradition with language mod- els by interleaving LLM-based decomposition with sym- bolic HTN planning. At a different level of granularity, LLMCompiler (Kim et al., 2024) constructs dependencies among function calls to schedule parallel execution, whereas FlowSearch (Hu et al., 2025b) models research as an evolv- ing graph of interdependent knowledge units and dynami- cally revises its structure in response to intermediate find- ings. HTN-Orchestrate distinguishes the parent-child rela- tion that defines a decomposition from precedence depen- dencies among sibling subtasks. The former determines how results are synthesized upward after all children complete, while the latter determines which prerequisite results are available for further decomposition and execution.
