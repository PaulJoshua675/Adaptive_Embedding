We provide an extended review of related work in Appendix F, but summarise the most relevant works here. Hahn et al. consider verify- ing minimax objectives using the logic patl, although they consider a model checking problem rather than a synthesis problem as they allow relabelling, and only allow an objective considered between a single coalition and its complement. A closely related model-based tools is PRISM-Games [33], which supports verification and strat- egy synthesis for rpatl-style probabilistic and reward objectives in stochastic games, including multi-objective and equilibrium-based extensions [31, 32, 34]. These methods assume explicit transition models. Varricchione et al. construct a reward machine from an atl specification, but their approach assumes a fully cooperative grand coalition, which in turn allows delegating to reward ma- chines generated for individual agents. Hu et al. consider reward machines for stochastic games, but are limited to exact Nash equi- libria, non-quantitative specifications, and two-player general-sum games. Jothimurugan et al. provide a bespoke enumerate-and-verify procedure for a non-probabilistic task specification language that is designed exclusively for equilibria with high social welfare. Ham- mond et al. studied temporal-logic reward optimisation of a sum of probabilities, but restricted their analysis to cooperative MARL. Our use of equilibrium constraints is inspired by classical rational synthesis [14, 20, 52]. Subramanian et al. study reward-machine based learning in general-sum stochastic games, proposing CROM and QROM, which combine counterfactual experience updates over reward-machine states with opponent modelling and provide tab- ular convergence and finite-time guarantees for Nash Q-values under additional assumptions. However, our paper deals with prob- abilistic thresholds rather than discounted rewards. We provide sampling-based certification guarantees and bounds on discounting effects, as well as equilibrium stability and convergence analysis. 6 Conclusions and Future Work Our approach targets multiple strategic agents, each equipped with its own probabilistic temporal-logic objective in pltl𝑓over finite traces. Each agent seeks a strategy satisfying its own probabilistic threshold within a profile that is stable under a chosen equilibrium notion (solution concept), interpreted over the utilities induced by the probabilistic requirements. Compared to existing approaches, our work focuses on model- free strategy synthesis in a learning setting, without requiring known transition dynamics. This allows considering objectives beyond those that a strategic coalition can unilaterally enforce, as required by logics such as atl and patl. We analyse how dis- counting affects the learning of probabilistic objectives, establish conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, and provide algorithm-agnostic finite-sample certificates of objec- tive satisfaction. Future work could target nested probabilistic properties and the undiscounted infinite-horizon case for 𝜔-regular properties, which would require learning maximal end components [11, 24], although this is non-trivial as end components for multi-agent sys- tems depend on the dynamics of other agents. Another extension of interest could be evaluating different solution concepts, such as a jointly satisficing objective [43]. It may also be worth investigating confidence-aware equilibrium selection with Nash-Q; however this introduces statistical noise into the selector and non-local depen- dence across states.
