Averaging over stages gives each original QA equal total weight, so a three-stage point counts no more than a one-stage choice. Efficient batching. Joint supervision removes the need for a separate language-backbone training pass at each refinement stage, but questions still require different amounts of computation. In particular, point ex- amples add two image crops, while choice and numerical examples have different candidate lengths and stage counts. Padding dissimilar sequences wastes work, and assigning unequal work to GPUs delays synchroniza- tion. We sort the expanded sequences within each global optimizer batch by length to form similar-length microbatches. A longest-work-first assignment (Graham, 1969) then distributes the microbatches across GPUs, using a cost that accounts for padded sequence length and visual-token work. This combines length grouping with cross-GPU load balancing while preserving the questions in each optimizer batch and their weights in Equation (7). Every QA remains an independent causal row: packing different questions together would additionally require isolation of Qwen’s Gated DeltaNet states (Yang et al., 2025c). The assignment rule and distributed normalization are specified in Section C.3. Given the scale of our expanded training corpus, we adopt full-parameter fine-tuning of the language back- bone, although parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) is also applicable (Zhou et al., 2025d, 2026c, 2025e,f, 2026d). The final configuration uses eight GPUs, a per-device microbatch size of eight, and eight gradient accumulation steps, yielding a global batch size of 512. We use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with learning rates of 5 × 10−6 for the backbone and 2 × 10−4 for the readout. Language computation uses BF16, while trainable weights, gradients, optimizer states, and candidate scoring remain in FP32. The vision en- coder and merger are frozen.
