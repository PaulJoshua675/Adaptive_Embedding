We test the same steering model without additional training on three more challenging benchmarks that require deeper multi-hop search, namely Frames (Krishna et al., 2025), GAIA (Mialon et al., 2024), and xBench-DeepSearch (Chen et al., 2025a) (Table 2). Steering improves both Avg@4 and Pass@4 on Frames and GAIA, and raises Pass@4 on xBench-DeepSearch. These results show that the learned steering capability generalizes beyond the training distribution. The steering agent mainly learns to recognize and correct goal drift, while task-specific ability stays within the frozen executor, which mitigates overfitting to the training tasks. To test whether the steering intelligence learned with one execution model can transfer across different model families, we use the same steering model to supervise three unseen executors, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Jiang et al., 2023) and Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024). As shown in Table 3, zero-shot steering behaves consistently across the four model families and brings small gains. The steering agent trained with the Qwen executor shows strong transferability, improving the three unseen executors by 15.78, 12.01, and 5.92 points while effectively reducing the executor turns. These results show that the learned steering intelligence transfers to executors unseen during training, allowing the executor to be freely switched without retraining. We further examine the controllability of the intervention frequency by varying the threshold of Equation 4 at deploy- ment without retraining. We visualize the total gain and the gain per intervention in Figure 4. In the left panel, RL steering consistently improves task performance across all intervention budgets, surpassing zero-shot steering, Filtered BC, and the planning and verification baselines. At the highest intervention frequency, RL steering raises the total gain to 17.11 points. These results show that the fast path design turns intervention frequency into a deployment-time con- trol, allowing the same model to trade steering cost for task performance. In the right panel, RL steering achieves its largest gain per intervention at low intervention frequency, while zero-shot steering and filtered BC remain low across frequencies, lacking the ability to judge where intervention matters most. This demonstrates that the fast path learns to prioritize steering at the states that influence the final outcome most, yielding the largest gain under a fixed intervention budget. 4.4 Steering Multi-Agent Systems (RQ3) We further conduct experiments on the multi-agent setting. Following TacoMAS (Xu et al., 2026), we build a five- role pipeline for search tasks, where agents with specialized roles collaborate in a fixed order, details of which are provided in Appendix A.4. The observation space of the steering agent is the task goal and inter-agent handoffs. The action space includes appending a guidance note or rejecting the message for retry. The steering agent, the training procedure, and the benchmarks stay the same as in the single-agent experiments.
