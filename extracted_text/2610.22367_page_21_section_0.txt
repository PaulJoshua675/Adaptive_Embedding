BM therefore replaces the template when the current candidate has predicted more syndromes than the stored template had when it was saved, and retains it when the current candidate has predicted fewer. When the two counts are equal, either choice gives a correct decoder [46, p. 125]. Textbook BM retains in that case. The circuit instead chooses to replace, which reduces the history it must store (see “Reconstructing BM history” below). The circuit makes this decision with a signed counter d. The counter is nonnegative whenever a replacement is required and negative whenever the template must be retained; when the counts are equal it may have either sign. (33) After the final round, σ is the error locator. Adaptive coefficient ranges. A naïve implementation would apply every BM update across all 36 possible coefficient positions in every round. Even in the worst case, this is unnecessary: after r syndromes have been processed, only coefficients through degree min(r, 35) can be active, so the update range need only grow by one position per round. The dashed line in Figure S1(b) shows this worst-case bound. On the DQI input distribution, however, the candidate polynomials typically grow more slowly still. A degree-L locator σ has L unknown coefficients after fixing σ0 = 1, while its evaluator ω, whose degree is less than L, has at most L unknown coefficients. Since each syndrome supplies one coefficient constraint, r syndromes typically determine a locator of degree L ≈r/2, consistent with the samples in Figure S1(b). The discovered circuit exploits this typical behavior, at the price of failing on rare inputs. Before compiling the circuit, we classically simulate BM on the DQI input distribution to choose a cutoff k for each round. The circuit then updates only the prefix σ0, . . . , σk, treating higher coefficients as zero. The prefixes expand as the rounds progress, which produces the rising per-round cost in Figure S1(a). Shared BM updates. Compiled directly, the two correction cases of Equation (33) would produce two separately controlled arithmetic paths. Only one path acts on any basis state, but both would appear in the fixed circuit and contribute to its Toffoli count. The main text shows how the circuit avoids this (Equation (5)). Under a flag that marks the replace case, it exchanges σ with the shifted template eτ. One shared update then subtracts a multiple of the template register from the locator register. The multiplier is α in the retain case and α−1 in the replace case. The replace result differs from the standard update only by the nonzero factor −α−1 and therefore has the same roots. The circuit also stores no discrepancy δ. The rescaled update maintains the invariant σ0δ = 1. (34) The invariant holds initially. The shifted template has zero constant coefficient, so the retain case changes neither σ0 nor δ. In the replace case, the new constant coefficient comes entirely from the correction term: σ0 ←α−1σ0 = (δ/∆)σ0 = ∆−1, while δ ←∆. The circuit therefore reads δ = σ−1 0 from the locator. In each round it forms the product ∆σ0, which equals α, and inverts it. A controlled selection between the product and its inverse supplies the multiplier for the shared update. Reconstructing BM history. Reversing a round requires the discrepancy that the round used, which the round computes into a temporary register. Keeping a twelve-bit history word for every round would raise the qubit count to 2,677. A replace round leaves σ0 = ∆−1 by Equation (34), so the locator determines the round’s discrepancy. The circuit therefore erases the temporary discrepancy register by MBU, and the reverse pass recomputes the erased value from the locator. A round with ∆= 0 changes nothing and has nothing to recover. A retain round with ∆̸ = 0 is the exception. No exchange occurs and σ0 is unchanged, so the constant coefficient that reveals the discrepancy on a replace round carries no trace of it here. The current ∆remains only in the temporary discrepancy register, and the circuit stores it on the history stack before erasing that register. A stack that never overflows needs 35 entries, or 665 qubits. Retain rounds are rare on the DQI input distribution. Starting from d = 0, a run of nonzero discrepancies repeatedly selects replace and leaves d = 0. A zero discrepancy raises d; a later replacement can then make it negative, producing retain rounds while d returns to zero. Such accidental zero discrepancies are uncommon over F212 before the locator reaches its final degree. Once it has, subsequent discrepancies stay zero and do not start new runs of retain rounds.
