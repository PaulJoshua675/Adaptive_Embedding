Agents that still give the true answer in private al- most never endorse the false claim (2.3% of their turns), but agents that give the false answer mostly do not endorse it either: 58% of their turns hedge on or ignore it. This matches minority influence in human groups, which shifts private judgment more than public behavior (Moscovici, 1980; Wood et al., 1994), and it is why the transcript understates the damage. We contribute a measurement protocol that pairs each agent’s public stance with its private answer in every round (§3), and use it to study six models on three factual-QA datasets (18 model–dataset cells). We find: 1. Hidden infection. Agents privately hold the false answer far more often than they publicly endorse it (51.6% vs. 19.7% of exposed turns under three attackers), and the false answers persist when agents are asked for their own view rather than to report what was discussed (§4.3). 2. Easy damage. Benign groups are stable (0.6% final infection without attacker), but a single attacker leaves 44.8% of benign agents on the false answer and three leave 60.5%; ten agents are not significantly more robust than one. 3. Weak correction. Agents that privately retain the true answer publicly reject the false claim in only 61.9% of their turns; in the remaining turns, they stay silent, hedge, or go along with it. 4. Knowledge does not predict robustness. Two models that know the same 85% of facts abandon 25% and 80% of them. A few attackers thus change what most agents an- swer in private while changing much less of what they say in public, and the transcript understates how far the false answer has spread. 2
