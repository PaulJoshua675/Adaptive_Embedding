Large language models (LLMs) can now process hundreds of thousands or even millions of tokens enabling applications involving books, scientific papers, financial reports, and long conversation his- tories (Team et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). However, the ability to accommodate long inputs does not guarantee effective reasoning over them: models struggle to use information located in the middle of the context (Liu et al., 2024b), and their effective context length often falls well short of the claimed window (Hsieh et al., 2024). Existing evaluations typi- cally treat context length as the main indicator of difficulty, assessing models on the same tasks at different input lengths (Hsieh et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a). While this approach reveals how performance changes with length, it leaves open which properties beyond token count make long-context reasoning difficult. Long-context benchmarks have increasingly moved from testing whether models can retrieve infor- mation to testing whether they can reason over it, covering multi-document, domain-specific, and aggregation-heavy tasks (Wang et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a). Yet these benchmarks mainly reveal where models fail, rather than what makes individual instances difficult. Goldman et al. (2024) take an important step by conceptualising two dimensions of long- context difficulty: scope (“How much necessary information is there to find?”) and dispersion (“How hard is it to find them in the context?”). These concepts help explain why questions of sim- ilar length may impose different demands on a model, but they are defined qualitatively rather than as measurable quantities. ETHIC (Lee et al., 2025) quantifies information coverage, but relies on an evaluator model’s judgements of which context chunks are potentially necessary, and captures only one aspect of difficulty in a limited range of task settings. Without quantitative definitions, it is difficult to establish how these properties vary across tasks, how they relate to model performance, and whether they account for differences in difficulty beyond context length. In this work, we propose quantitative definitions of evidence scope and evidence dispersion, based on the amount and positions of the supporting evidence within the context. We further extend this characterisation with reasoning depth, defined as the longest dependency path in an evidence-to- answer reasoning graph: parallel evidence lookups do not increase depth, whereas operations that depend on earlier results do. Together, these three properties characterise how much supporting information is involved, where it occurs, and how it must be combined to derive an answer. To study how these properties contribute to model failures, we construct LURCH (Long-context Un- derstanding and Reasoning from Contextual Hints), a multi-domain question-answering benchmark built from diverse, naturally occurring long-context tasks. Each question is provided with its an- swer, source-provided or automatically annotated supporting evidence, and an answer-conditioned reasoning graph, enabling all three properties to be quantified. Using LURCH, we conduct controlled experiments on multiple LLMs to address three research questions: RQ1: Difficulty at fixed length. Among instances of the same context length, how strongly are ev- idence scope, evidence dispersion, and reasoning depth associated with model performance? Finding: Across questions, greater scope, dispersion and reasoning depth are each associated with lower scores after accounting for context length, source dataset and model. Notably, doubling reasoning depth is associated with a score drop comparable to that of doubling context length. RQ2: Length-dependent difficulty. How does the effect of context length on performance vary with evidence scope, evidence dispersion, and reasoning depth? Finding: As context length increases, performance degrades sharply on instances whose evidence is widely dispersed, whereas it remains relatively stable on instances with localised evidence. This pattern holds across all evaluated models. RQ3: Compounding difficulty. Are model failures associated with particular combinations of scope, dispersion, and depth, even when no individual property is extreme? Finding: Difficulty compounds across properties: questions with moderately high values on all three properties are harder than those with an extreme value on just one. This pattern is broadly consistent with an additive combination of the three properties. 2
