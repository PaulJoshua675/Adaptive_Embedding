In cQA environments this exchange is largely goal-oriented and problem-driven [2, 10]: participants are motivated to contrib­ ute operational narratives and practical knowledge by reputation, reciprocity, and self- efficacy [6, 15]. Studies of Yahoo! Chiebukuro specifically document it as a venue where users request and disseminate everyday, often highly personal, domain-specific concerns and knowledge [30]. What this literature has not examined is whether the specific lexical content associated with an answer’s “best” status, or with a question’s sustained atten­ tion, can be characterized systematically across a large corpus, at a level of detail finer than topic. A substantial body of work examines answer selection and quality directly, using lexical, semantic, user, and interaction features to predict which answers are cho­ sen or rated helpful [3, 14]. More recent studies emphasize the relationship between an answer and its question: perceived helpfulness rises when an answer’s language attri­ butes, including concreteness, are congruent with the question’s [26], and thematic consistency between answer and question is positively related to helpfulness while senti­ ment consistency is not [16]. On the question side, how a question is crafted — including strategies affecting its concreteness — is correlated with both audience engagement and answer quality [8]. It is important to note that an asker-selected “best answer” reflects the asker’s subjective judgment rather than objectively verified correctness [12, 29]. Accordingly, this study treats best-answer selection as an indicator of perceived value within the platform, rather than as a measure of answer correctness. Relative to this predictive and experimental literature, the present study takes a descriptive approach. Rather than seeking to maximize predictive performance, we examine a domain-specific corpus to identify which individual nouns and semantic clusters are associated with each outcome. 2.3 Social media in logistics and supply chain discourse Social media research has extensively covered consumer behavior, political discourse, and entertainment, but its application to professional discourse in logistics and supply chain management remains comparatively underdeveloped [11, 13]. Logistics knowledge communication has traditionally been studied through formal organizational channels and B2B networks [19, 27]; recent supply-chain disruptions have driven practitioners toward informal digital spaces for real-time insight and peer validation [28], and recent reviews explicitly call for more social-media-based analysis in supply-chain and logistics contexts [25, 32]. A related recent strand applies NLP and large-language-model meth­ ods to logistics-relevant social media text from a different platform, YouTube: one study extracts logistics challenges from video subtitles and comments and characterizes them into recurring concern areas — time management, driver welfare, technological invest­ ment, policy transparency [22] — and a companion study uses the same YouTube corpus to generate policy proposals via graph-based retrieval-augmented generation [21]. Both target aggregate discourse characterization or policy generation rather than the fine- grained, word- and cluster-level question this paper addresses on a different platform: which specific content is associated with a post’s structural fate.
