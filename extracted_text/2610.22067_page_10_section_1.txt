We argue that context length alone is an incomplete measure of long-context difficulty, and intro- duced three sample-level properties that complement it: evidence scope, evidence dispersion, and reasoning depth. To quantify them, we built LURCH, a benchmark of 6,550 naturally occurring instances from 17 sources, with verified evidence and reasoning graphs produced by our annota- tion pipeline. Across four models, these properties capture difficulty that length does not. At fixed length, broader, more dispersed and deeper questions score lower, with doubling depth costing about as much as doubling length (RQ1). Added length is harmful mainly when evidence is broad or dis- persed (RQ2), and the hardest questions are demanding on several properties at once, even when none is extreme, because their effects add up (RQ3). We therefore recommend that long-context benchmarks report scope, dispersion and depth alongside length. Since our analyses are observa- tional and some annotations are automatic, future work could vary the surrounding context while holding evidence and reasoning fixed, to test these effects causally.
