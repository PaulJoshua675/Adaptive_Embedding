Large Language Models for Translation. Early studies showed that general-purpose LLMs can deliver competitive translation performance, al- though results vary across languages, domains, and prompting strategies (Jiao et al., 2023; Hendy et al., 2023). Translation-specific continued pre- training and supervised fine-tuning further improve smaller open models; ALMA, for example, com- bines monolingual adaptation with high-quality parallel data (Xu et al., 2024). These efforts im- prove the underlying translator, but largely leave inference-time information acquisition outside the modeling problem. A parallel line of work intro- duces explicit deliberation into LLM-based MT. MAPS elicits multiple translation-relevant analy- ses before selecting among candidate outputs (He et al., 2024); TEaR adopts a translate–estimate– refine procedure (Feng et al., 2025); and DRT dis- tills extended reasoning traces for figurative text (Wang et al., 2025a). These studies suggest that additional computation can help difficult inputs, but the form and amount are typically fixed in ad- vance. SRAT instead learns when extra reasoning and retrieval really help for translation. Retrieval-Augmented Machine Translation. Re- trieval has long been used in MT through trans- lation memories and nearest-neighbor examples, while recent LLM-based systems draw on broader forms of external evidence. IdiomKB retrieves ex- planatory knowledge for idiomatic translation (Li et al., 2024), RAGtrans incorporates multilingual unstructured documents (Wang et al., 2025b), and cross-cultural MT has explored multilingual knowl- edge graphs (Conia et al., 2024). These results demonstrate the value of external knowledge when the source alone is insufficient. Yet most systems either retrieve by default or rely on predefined rout- ing, while retrieval is often optimized separately from the final translation objective. Our focus is in- stead on whether retrieved evidence improves trans- lation quality for a given input and model state. Adaptive Retrieval and Reinforcement Learning. Adaptive retrieval has been studied mainly in lan- guage modeling and question answering. FLARE triggers retrieval when generation becomes uncer- tain (Jiang et al., 2023); Self-RAG learns retrieval and reflection actions from supervised preference data (Asai et al., 2024); and Search-R1 uses rein- forcement learning to acquire multi-turn search- and-reason behavior from answer-level rewards (Jin et al., 2025b). These works show that retrieval can be integrated into a learned generation policy. Translation, however, differs in that a plausible out- put can often be produced without retrieval, while useful evidence may guide the rendering without explicitly containing the target translation. This makes selective retrieval and alignment with trans- lation quality especially important. 3 Method Our method addresses two decisions induced by the partial-evidence regime: when external evidence should be acquired and what evidence should be re- turned when retrieval is invoked. We first formulate selective retrieval as part of the translation policy, and then introduce two strategies for aligning the retriever with downstream translation quality. 3.1 Selective Retrieval as a Translation Policy Let x denote a source sentence, P(ℓs, ℓt) the trans- lation instruction prompt given source and target languages ℓs and ℓt, and y⋆a reference avail- able only during training. An external corpus D = {dj}N j=1 contains potentially useful evidence such as idiom explanations, terminology and entity, background documents, or online information. We model the LLM translator as a policy πθ with initial state s0 = [P(ℓs, ℓt), x]. Starting from s0, the policy generates a trajectory τ = (a1, . . . , aT ) that may interleave reasoning, retrieval, and the final translation ˆy. Making information acquisition part of the trajectory allows retrieval to be decided dynamically rather than applied as a fixed prepro- cessing step. Specifically, the trajectory contains three types of action during the workflow: • Reasoning (<think>). The model may analyze ambiguity, figurative meaning, cultural context, entities, or terminology, or assess whether its current knowledge is sufficient for translation. • Retrieval (<tool_call>). When the current evidence is insufficient, the model generates a natural-language query qt and invokes the re- triever rϕ over D: Dt ∼rϕ(· | qt, D).
