Honesty interventions For inference-time interventions, one could simply insert an honesty in- struction to the prompt; but it relies on the model to police itself, and the improvement does not generalize (Ren et al., 2025). Activation steering (Turner et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2025) intervenes on the residual stream, and two recent methods target honesty directly. G´oral et al. (2025) distribute a fixed steering budget across depth with a Gaussian schedule, while Herbster et al. (2026) uses a logistic-regression decision boundary to determine which tokens to steer and projection geometry to determine by how much. For training-time interventions, Wang et al. (2025) find SFT on harmful system prompts along with honest responses to be the strongest mitigation. Beyond SFT, Jagadeesh et al. (2026) train truth- fulness as one of several beneficial traits with RL and report that the behaviour persists under ad- versarial prompting and harmful fine-tuning, and Joglekar et al. (2025) train a separately-rewarded confession issued after each answer. Adversarial training A parallel line of work studies aligned LLMs through the lens of adver- sarial robustness. Automatically optimized suffix attacks such as GCG (Zou et al., 2023), together with jailbreak methods such as PAIR (Chao et al., 2025) and AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2024), show that safety-aligned models remain vulnerable to targeted prompt perturbations that induce harmful behaviour. To defend against these attacks, Mazeika et al. (2024) demonstrates that adversarial train- ing can improve robust refusal; however, discrete attack generation is computationally expensive. CAT addresses this bottleneck by replacing discrete token search with embedding-space pertur- bations, showing that robustness to continuous attacks transfers to discrete jailbreaks while being substantially more efficient to train (Xhonneux et al., 2024). Similarly, Latent adversarial train- ing perturbs hidden activations instead of input embeddings and improves robustness to harmful behaviours (Casper et al., 2025). These methods have been evaluated primarily on suppression of harmful outputs and robustness towards jailbreaks, rather than on honesty. Our work targets this gap by adapting CAT from safety alignment to honesty alignment. 3 METHODS Designing adversarial targets Let x be a prompt containing some pressure to lie and a proposition the model is being pushed to misstate. Following (Xhonneux et al., 2024), we associate with x two continuations: y+, an answer consistent with with the knowledge available to the model, whether learnt during training or supplied in context; and y−, an answer that complies with the pressure and asserts the contrary. A model is dishonest on x when it has a high probability to output y−. Training models for honesty is different from safeguarding models against generating harmful out- puts in jailbreaking. In safety alignment, a single generic refusal “Sorry, I can’t do that” serves as the toward-target y+ for every harmful prompt (Mazeika et al., 2024; Xhonneux et al., 2024), and the away-target y−typically begins with a compliance prefix such as “Sure”. However, for honesty, both the truth and lie depend on the context, so both targets must be constructed case-by-case. Continuous adversarial training Adversarial training optimizes a min-max objective, where an inner maximization finds a worst-case perturbation and an outer minimization makes the model robust to it (Madry et al., 2019). In the LLM setting the threat is targeted, such that the attacker aims to elicit one specific, harmful continuation y−, of which the inner loop maximizes the likelihood. Searching over discrete token sequences for the worst prompt is far too expensive to do inside a training loop (Mazeika et al., 2024). Following Xhonneux et al.
