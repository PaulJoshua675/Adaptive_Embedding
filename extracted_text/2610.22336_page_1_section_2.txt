Adapting treatment to the evolving condition of a patient has received considerable attention in medicine, with success reported in a broad range of applications [4, 7, 25, 26, 39, 50]. In oncology, adaptive therapy pursues this idea to delay the emergence of drug resistance [18]. Its rationale is that, under continuous treatment, drug-sensitive cells are eliminated and drug-resistant cells are left to grow without competition. By pausing treatment once the tumor has responded and resuming it once the tumor has regrown, clinicians can retain sensitive cells that continue to suppress the resistant ones [18, 45]. This approach was tested in a pilot clinical trial of the drug abiraterone for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer [52, 53]. In this clinical trial, clinicians paused abiraterone once prostate-specific antigen (PSA), a blood marker that tracks tumor burden, fell below half of its pretreatment value, and resumed it once PSA returned to the pretreatment value, a protocol we refer to as AT50. Compared with a contemporaneous cohort who received continuous treatment with abiraterone as the standard of care, the median time to progression more than doubled under AT50, while patients received no abiraterone during 46% of their time in the clinical trial [52]. Despite its remarkable success, AT50 is by design a simple heuristic in the sense that it applies the same pair of thresholds to every patient and responds only to the most recent PSA value. In addition, its benefit has so far been observed in only 17 patients, and, based on the models fitted to the same data, other schedules are expected to further improve outcomes [52]. Existing efforts toward more refined schedules include optimal control of competition models [9], deep reinforcement learning (RL) for controlling the emergence of drug resistance [12], and more recently RL policies trained within models calibrated to prostate cancer patients [16,33,34]. Nevertheless, these policies are evaluated within the same models used for their training, and their performance beyond these models remains largely unexamined. Ideally, one would like to learn, in a data-driven manner, a dynamic treatment policy that is more sophisticated than AT50, attains improved performance, and generalizes to the broad population of patients beyond those enrolled in the clinical trial.
