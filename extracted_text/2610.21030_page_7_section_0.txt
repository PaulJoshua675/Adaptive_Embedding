We evaluate on the official development set, TREC-DL’19, and TREC- DL’20, all of which use held-out queries disjoint from the training split. These splits provide an in-domain check outside the reasoning-intensive BRIGHT setting. AURA-4B is comparable to RaDeR-7B across all three splits despite using a smaller backbone. This result suggests that utility- aware reasoning supervision does not substantially degrade conventional in-domain retrieval relative to a strong reasoning-trained retriever; broader general-purpose evaluation is left to future work. 4.8 Ablation Study Table 4 reports ablation results on BRIGHT. We examine five design dimensions of AURA, each targeting a specific question about the framework. Q1: Is the specificity score robust to negative aggregation? The choice of negative aggregation in S(zt) has minimal impact: mean top-3, max, and mean all negatives yield similar performance (34.7, 34.6, 34.4). This indicates that AURA is robust to this design choice. Mean over all negatives may dilute the signal from hard negatives, while using only the hardest single negative may be susceptible to outlier noise; the default mean top-3 offers a reasonable balance. Q2: Does utility-aware supervision improve over dense intermediate supervision? This is the central ablation: we ask whether the retrieval specificity signal provides meaningful supervision beyond simply applying contrastive training at more positions. We compare four different supervi- sion strategies: (1) final state only, which applies InfoNCE only at zT as in prior work; (2) random utility labels, which trains the utility estimator with randomly assigned labels; (3) monotonic utility la- bels, which assumes reasoning quality increases monotonically with depth; and (4) uniform-weight intermediate states, which applies InfoNCE at all intermediate states with equal weight, removing utility-based differentiation. Results show a clear ordering: final-state-only (31.2) < random (31.9) < monotonic (33.2) < uni- form (33.6) < AURA (34.7). Two findings are notable. First, uniform-weight supervision already improves substantially over final-state-only (+2.4), confirming that denser contrastive training is benefi- cial. However, AURA outperforms uniform weight- ing by a further 1.1 points, demonstrating that utility-aware differentiation among intermediate states provides supervision that cannot be achieved by simply increasing the number of contrastive training positions. Second, monotonic labels under- perform uniform weighting (−0.4), indicating that the assumption of monotonically increasing reason- ing quality is actively harmful—consistent with the over-reasoning phenomenon motivating this work. Random labels degrade close to final-state-only, confirming that the pairwise ranking loss requires accurate ordering signal to function effectively. Q3: Are trajectory filtering and gradient isola- tion necessary? Retaining trajectories in which no intermediate state improves over the initial query changes nDCG@10 only marginally, from 34.7 to 34.6. Filtering therefore provides a small benefit by removing trajectories without a positive within-trajectory ranking signal, but does not ex- plain AURA’s overall improvement. In contrast, re- moving gradient isolation causes a substantial drop to 29.8. Retrieval gradients propagated through earlier prefixes therefore interfere with stable joint generation–retrieval optimization, supporting the gradient-isolation design. Q4: How well does the utility estimator ap- proximate oracle selection? At inference time, ground-truth retrieval specificity is unavailable, which motivates utility estimation. The utility esti- mator Vϕ achieves 34.7, 0.3 points below the oracle upper bound (35.0), where the oracle selects the reasoning state with the highest true S(zt) using ground-truth relevance labels. Random state selec- tion drops to 32.3. This narrow aggregate perfor- mance gap indicates that Vϕ provides an effective approximation of retrieval utility without access to relevance labels.
