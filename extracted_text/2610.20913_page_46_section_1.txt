TransX is organized around two coupled questions: where should additional model capacity be allocated to improve recommendation quality, and must increasing that capacity proportionally increase online serving cost? The model’s compute–state decomposition separates sequence encoding, behavior memory, and candidate-conditioned fusion into distinct scaling axes, while the infrastructure preserves the same boundaries in training and serving. Behavior computation is shared across overlapping prefixes, recomputed as behavioral state changes, and materialized behind a bounded behavior-memory interface, while request-time computation is concentrated on candidate-conditioned prediction. Within the evaluated regimes, marginal returns differ substantially across these scaling axes. Encoder scaling improves prediction at fixed behavior-memory resolution but saturates beyond moderate depth at fixed fusion capacity; increasing supervision shifts the allocation frontier toward candidate-conditioned fusion; and the memory resolution needed to preserve quality remains roughly stable as overall model capacity grows. Together with disaggregated serving, these results suggest that efficient recommendation scaling is a joint problem of compute allocation and amortized/disaggregated execution: additional capacity should be allocated according to its marginal return rather than increased proportionally across all computational roles, while reusable computation should remain off the request-critical path. The same behavior-memory interface also supports capability specialization. Starting from a core response-prediction backbone, we post-train capability-specific memory readers and output heads for retrieval and preference ranking, with generative recommendation explored offline. Production deployment of the core, retrieval, and ranking variants further validates the framework across recommendation quality, training efficiency, and serving economics. Limitations and future directions. The observed scaling patterns are empirical and conditional on the studied data, supervision regimes, architecture family, and deployment envelope; they do not constitute a universal scaling law or uniquely identify the mechanisms behind the patterns. Materialized behavioral encoder continuation and memory state maintenance also introduce freshness, storage, compatibility, and recovery costs. Finally, for task specialization via post-training, a shared behavior-memory interface and frozen backbone need not be optimal for every task. Future work should therefore study: (1) adaptive allocation across encoder capacity, behavior-memory resolution, and candidate-conditioned fusion; and (2) when the behavioral memory interface and model backbone should remain shared, be partially adapted, or become task-specific. More broadly, the central scaling question for industrial recommendation is not only how much computation to add, but where the capacity should be allocated, when each computation should be executed, and over what scope the resulting states can be reused. 46
