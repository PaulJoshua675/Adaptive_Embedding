Despite that, we find that all models lean towards female- associated, older-associated, more popular, and more stereotypically white names. We in- clude the length of the names, in tokens, as a possible confounder, but find it isn’t relevant in this analysis. While prompts do modulate the strength of these biases, the overall pattern suggests that name-linked stereotypes remain a strong signal shaping an LLM’s choice. This suggests the sentence acts as an amplifier of existing biases rather than determining them. Mixed-effect modeling shows that the rel- ative strength of attributes matches the ob- served change in the attributes in more likely- to-be-chosen names. This quantifies the bias, showing how large the effect can be for some of the attributes. Finally, we analyze the correlation between different biases within the LLMs, and find large levels of variation. This suggests that coupling between biases is not universal. While our trials are contrived examples, it is easy to see how name bias could negatively affect society and businesses. Several recent works have suggested using LLMs to review r´esum´es for applicants for jobs (Gan et al., 2024). Asking that system to choose between two applicants, or score two applicants in ref- erence to each other, is a direct analog of our tests. In this example, choosing a worse appli- cant because of these biases systematically has obvious detrimental effects to the business, so- ciety, and the applicant. However, this form of name bias is already seen when humans review a r´esum´e, making it hard to know if LLMs will help remove or add to the level of bias of the human reviewers (Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004). 6.1 Contributions This work provides a model-comparative, measurement-driven characterization of name- triggered bias in local LLMs. We (i) demon- strate how name-driven bias is consistently de- tectable across models, (ii) show that differ- ent models have biases that trend towards the same associated attributes, (iii) quantify how bias for different attributes contribute to an LLM’s preference for a name while controlling for prompt effects, (iv) and we analyze bias coupling to identify when they appear aligned vs separable within a given model. 6.2 Limitations Our evaluation is intentionally contrived: a forced binary choice in short templates does not reflect open-ended generation tasks or real decision workflows.
