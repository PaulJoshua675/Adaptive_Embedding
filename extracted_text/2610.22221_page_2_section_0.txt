Audio captures what appearance does not. An object’s acoustic response to impact is governed by physical laws: stiffness, density, and geometry set the resonant frequency, and internal friction, radiation, and mounting set the damping. We confirm the gap directly. Frozen audio embeddings predict the quality factor Q at ρ = 0.73, while a single CLIP-encoded video frame taken at the moment of impact supports only ρ = 0.28. Here Q is a dimensionless measure of how many oscillations a struck object completes before its ring dies away, so a highly resonant glass has a large Q and a heavily damped cloth a small one. We write ρ for the Spearman rank correlation between predicted and true values: ρ = 1 means the model orders impacts by Q perfectly, and ρ = 0 means its ordering carries no information about the truth. We use ρ throughout because we care about whether a model can rank objects by how much they ring, not about the absolute scale of its predictions. Our question is the narrower one of how far a frozen visual encoder can be pushed toward the audio number. Our answer is that it can be pushed a useful fraction of the way. We distill from a frozen audio teacher into a frozen CLIP encoder, training a projection head as the only new component, and measure the outcome under a protocol in which knowing the material category earns nothing: correlations are computed within a material that was held out from the projection head and from the probe alike. Distillation improves prediction there for most of the 17 materials. Part of this improvement needs no audio at all, since giving the encoder four frames spanning the impact, so that it can see the object deform and recoil, supplies about half. Most of the audio signal nonetheless stays out of reach, which we take to mark the interesting part of the problem rather than a settled question. 1.1 Contributions • We propose probing frozen encoders for a continuous physical property, the quality fac- tor Q, rather than for the categorical material labels used in prior evaluations. Our mea- surements show that audio embeddings predict Q at ρ = 0.73 whereas a single CLIP frame reaches 0.28, that this audio advantage persists for materials withheld from training (ρ ≈0.60), and that every visual encoder we test is near chance at ranking objects within wood, ceramic, and plastic and reaches at most 0.36 within metal. • We show that part of the visual shortfall is temporal rather than material. Four frames spanning the impact raise vision-only prediction on unseen materials by 0.051 without any acoustic supervision, which suggests that some of what a single frame misses is visible dynamics rather than hidden material structure. • We propose distilling a frozen audio teacher into a frozen visual encoder through a trained projection head, and our experiments demonstrate that this moves a useful fraction of the signal across. The distilled CLIP encoder reaches ρ = 0.44 on Q and, with a four-frame input, improves prediction from 0.234 to 0.338 on materials withheld from the head as well as the probe (+0.104, 15 of 17 materials, p = 0.002; +0.053 over the matched four-frame baseline, 14 of 17 materials). • We report controls that establish where this gain comes from. A capacity ablation, a shuffled-teacher control, and a material-label reference together attribute most of the im- provement to acoustic correspondence rather than to added parameters or category recog- nition.
