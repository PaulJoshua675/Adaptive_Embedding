For a given conversation, the variants share the same information need and pro- file and differ only in simulated behaviour, for example in language proficiency or patience, so that a system is tested for robustness to how a user interacts rather than to what they want. The concrete behaviour of each variant is not disclosed by the organizers. Both the simulation and the grading are driven by rubrics: concise subtopic questions manually derived from each conversation, 92 in total across the 15 conversations, following the rubric-based evaluation methodology of Farzi and Dietz [16]. At each turn the simulator produces an utterance for the current rubric; our system retrieves passages and generates a response; an LLM judge then scores the response against that rubric on a 0–5 scale and advances to the next rubric once the score passes a threshold, otherwise letting the simulator give feedback and rephrase [1]. Three families of metrics are reported, all judged by an LLM; a manual evaluation is in progress at the time of writing. • Retrieval. The passages cited by our responses are pooled and judged against rubric-based graded qrels (0–4); we report turn-averaged NDCG@5. • Per-turn. Each response is graded against the current rubric on four criteria: the rubric score, which measures whether it answers the rubric question on a 0–5 scale, and engagement, relevance & usefulness, and overall subtopic quality on a 1–5 scale. • Dialogue-level. The whole dialogue is rated on a 1–5 scale for five criteria: personalization, abbreviated Pers.; trustwor- thiness, Trust; information flow & coherence, Flow; mixed- initiative strategies, Mix-Init; and overall user satisfaction, Sat. 7.2 Run Comparison Tables 6 and 7 report the four submitted runs on the retrieval-plus- per-turn metrics and on the dialogue-level metrics, respectively. Fusing PCDR with its own non-personalized backbone conv-Qwen3, another dense retriever, barely moves the score; fusing with the learned-sparse SPLADE-v3 helps more, and fusing with lexical BM25 helps most. The ordering follows the complementarity of the evidence rather than the strength of the partner, since the added retrievers are indi- vidually weaker than PCDR. This is the chorus effect we observed in our 2024 system [20]: rankings built from different matching signals cover different relevant passages, so a more heterogeneous fusion recovers more of them. An end-to-end, rewrite-free configuration. The PCDR + CDR run is fully rewrite-free: both legs are dense and encode the conver- sation and profile directly, so the entire pipeline consumes only the full conversation and the user profile, with no LLM query rewriting anywhere. It is the most genuinely end-to-end of the four runs and removes the latency and cost of an LLM rewriting step, and it remains competitive on all three families of metrics. This shows that the reformulate-then-retrieve stage can be dropped entirely without collapsing quality. Response quality is decoupled from retrieval. The response- quality scores do not track the retrieval scores. PCDR alone has the lowest retrieval score yet obtains the highest response quality at both the per-turn and the dialogue level, while the fused runs with stronger retrieval score lower on both. Since every run generates its response from the same top passages with the same model, a better passage ranking does not translate into a better judged response here. We do not have an explanation for this inversion and leave it to future work. Mixed initiative. Mixed-initiative strategies are our weakest di- alogue dimension. This is expected, as our pipeline answers each question directly and we did not build an explicit mixed-initiative mechanism such as asking clarifying questions or proactively sug- gesting follow-ups [6]. The low scores reflect the absence of that mechanism rather than a retrieval deficiency. What we cannot yet conclude. Three factors limit the analysis. First, other teams’ submissions were not made available to us, so we can read only within-system contrasts and cannot place our results against the field. Second, a per-user-type analysis is not meaningful here, because the variants cover different and unequal subsets of the conversations, so averages per variant are confounded with topic difficulty; only six conversations are run with all four variants, which is too few for a controlled comparison. Third, the organizers do not disclose which user characteristics the four variants are meant to distinguish, which further prevents us from interpreting robustness across variants as intended.
