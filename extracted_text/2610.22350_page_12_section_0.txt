LHAW [8] is the most systematic treatment of missing information and models the cost of asking; our L0 to L1 step reproduces its finding that agents rarely recover full-specification performance by asking, and our L3 adds a situation it does not cover: a request that is misdirected. Vending-Bench [30] showed that an executable business simulation can replace human judges; we attach one to an engineering deliverable. Two methodological warnings also shaped the design. Results obtained against LLM-played users depend on which model plays the user [14], which is why our stakeholders are scripted, and single-run success overstates reliability [36, 37], which is why every task is run three times. The admission anchors follow the practice of auditing an agentic benchmark with do-nothing and shortcut solutions before trusting its scores [38]. Beyond single benchmarks, long- horizon evaluations track the length of software tasks agents can complete [39], and Collaborative Gym [40] studies agents working alongside people; FDE-Gym instead sets people and agents the same tasks separately, so that the two can be compared. 7 Limitations A simulated customer. Stakeholders are scripted so that results are reproducible. Real people are less predictable: they change their minds, answer a different question from the one asked, and may respond differently on the basis of previous interactions. The contact costs are set by the experts who wrote each scenario and are a model of the cost of stakeholder time, not a measurement of it. Sample size across levels. Levels L0 to L2 are reported on the 20 original tasks only, and for three of these the L2 result is taken from the identical L3 task instruction. The drop from L0 to L1 is large relative to this sample; the differences among L1, L2, and L3 are not large, and we claim only that no large further drop is visible. No human baseline yet. This version reports models only. The scale is anchored by the do-nothing baseline and the reference solution, which shows that each task is solvable with full knowledge of the underlying facts; how far human experts can proceed when given only the sponsor’s request has not yet been measured. A single agent framework. All models run in the same minimal agent framework. The results describe these models in that framework, not the products built around them, and a stronger framework may change both the scores and the ranking. Isolation and cost. Runs were executed on several hosts with process-level isolation of varying strength; each run’s record is audited after grading, but one container per run would provide a stronger guarantee. Model usage cost is as metered on the route through which each model was accessed and is not a comparison of list prices. Coverage and contamination. Twenty scenarios across 18 sectors are a sample of delivery work, weighted to- ward agents and workflows in regulated operations. The scenarios are adapted from real deliveries, and although companies are fictional and variants change the facts, we cannot rule out that related material is in the training data.
