Preprint. Under Review. map onto a flaw of the cited work? The flaw counts whether the cited work declares it itself (‘‘future work needed...’’) OR the new paper empirically shows the cited method/benchmark is saturated, insufficient, or wrong. This can be in the introduction, results sections or the discussion. • foundation -- the cited work provides the conceptual frame, formalism, or method the new paper starts from. Test: would the method section need to be rewritten if this cited work didn’t exist? • extends -- the new paper is a direct continuation, adaptation, or generalization of the cited work -- positioned as ‘‘the next step after X.’’ Test: does the paper frame itself as building forward from this specific work? A ref is NOT a sparker (→goes in other ids) when it is: • a dataset / tool / model / implementation the paper merely consumes (PyTorch, scikit-learn, an embedding model, a judge LLM, etc.), • generic context-setting / related-work-list padding with no stance. SELF-CONSISTENCY CHECK. Before finalizing a sparker, re-read your own rationale. If it concedes any of: • ‘‘X provides theoretical grounding but the method does not build on X’’ • ‘‘name-checked in related work; not load-bearing’’ then the ref belongs in other ids, not sparkers. The rubric tests are strict -- a ref must PASS one of them, not merely gesture at it. For each SPARKER ref, also set INFLUENCE (orthogonal to the labels): • central -- load-bearing for the paper’s argument; removing it would damage the contribution, framing, or method. • incidental -- acknowledged but peripheral; paper would survive without it. Provide one short rationale per sparker ref and a list of context quotes drawn verbatim from the mention snippets or body (one per supporting passage; usually 1--3). WORKED EXAMPLES (from past papers; abridged): Ex. 1 -- exposes gap, central (empirical): Cited work: ‘‘RAT-100, a remote-associates benchmark for creativity (1968).’’ Mentions in new paper: ‘‘Frontier models score >95% on RAT-100; the test no longer discriminates.’’ [Experiments] ‘‘Our Table 2 shows RAT-100 saturates while our new benchmark does not.’’ Verdict: SPARKER, exposes gap, central. The new paper’s experiments establish the gap even though the cited paper does not declare it. Ex. 2 -- motivates, central (load-bearing motivation): Cited work: ‘‘Boden 1991, on combinatorial creativity.’’ Mentions: ‘‘We take motivation from Boden’s account of combinatorial creativity.’’ (cited once, in the introduction’s motivation paragraph.) Verdict: SPARKER, motivates, central. The paper’s stated reason-for-being traces explicitly to this work -- the test ‘‘would the reader still understand WHY?’’ fails without it. Ex. 3 -- NOT a sparker (single-sentence formalism mention): Cited work: ‘‘Kulesza & Taskar, Determinantal Point Processes.’’ Mentions: ‘‘Diverse selection has been studied via DPPs (Kulesza & Taskar) and submodular methods (Lin & Bilmes).’’ (one sentence, related work only, not used in the method.) Verdict: other ids. Fails the foundation test -- the method section would not need rewriting if these refs didn’t exist. Ex. 4 -- NOT a sparker (thin motivation): Cited work: ‘‘Chakrabarty et al., on AI creative writing evaluation.’’ Mentions: ‘‘Recent work has questioned whether AI can be creative (Chakrabarty et al.).’’ (half-sentence in related work, no follow-up.) Verdict: other ids. Fails the motivates test -- the new paper’s reason-for-being does not depend on this specific cite; removing it would not change the reader’s understanding of why the paper exists.
