The immense potential of AI applications has catalyzed an unprecedented expansion in the accelerator landscape. While NVIDIA remains the incumbent platform, the AMD Instinct™[1, 2] GPU has emerged as a viable alternative. Meanwhile, hyperscalers have also developed custom sili- con, including Meta MTIA [3–6], Google TPU [7], Amazon Trainium [8], Microsoft Maia [9], Huawei Ascend [10], Alibaba Hanguang [11], and Baidu Kunlun [12]. Capitalizing on this hardware proliferation, Meta deploys a heterogeneous fleet comprising NVIDIA GPUs, AMD GPUs, and proprietary MTIA accelerators at scale. This strategic diversification delivers several benefits: (1) enabling cost optimization by preserving the flexibility to adopt the most economical solutions as the market evolves; (2) strengthening supply-chain resilience; and (3) matching model characteristics to the most suitable hardware. However, the extensive history of NVIDIA-specific opti- mizations hinders performance portability to other hardware. For example, fundamental differences in memory hierarchies and execution units (e.g., 32-thread warps vs. 64-thread wave- fronts) thwart direct software migration between NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. These challenges are most acute in deep learning recommendation models (DLRMs) [13, 14]. Unlike the relatively uniform large language models (LLMs), DLRMs are characterized by diverse model architectures, fragmented compute kernel sets, irregular matrix dimensions, and complex memory access patterns. While industry adoption of AMD GPUs has largely focused on inference—evidenced by Meta’s deployment of hundreds of thousands of AMD GPUs—distributed training presents a far greater technical challenge. This paper delivers the first hyperscaler report of enabling AMD GPUs for production- scale DLRM training. We demonstrate that through systematic optimization, AMD MI350X achieves performance comparable to the incumbent NVIDIA B200 GPU across a diverse suite of production DLRMs. We distill our production experience into three reusable principles for new hardware integration: Hardware-model co-design. Different accelerator microarchi- tectures offer distinct advantages and trade-offs. Rather than directly porting models from NVIDIA GPUs, hardware-model co-design—revising the model structure and software recipe—is essential to leverage an accelerator’s specific strengths. Specif- ically, we exploit MI350X’s 1.6× HBM capacity relative to B200. To maximize training efficiency, we expand per-GPU batch sizes and reduce activation checkpointing while simulta- neously optimizing data or model parallelism and implementing efficient embedding re-sharding. Beyond training throughput, we utilize the additional HBM to enhance model quality by enabling longer input sequences, larger hidden dimensions, and expanded embedding tables. This holistic approach delivers a 20% training speedup and a >0.1% improvement in normalized entropy (NE) [15], demonstrating that architectural synergies can simultaneously drive improvements in both hardware efficiency and model quality.
