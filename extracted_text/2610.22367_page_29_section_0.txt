12 Branch decoders. A forward step computes b from the parity of g and uses it to control the update. After the update, g no longer holds that parity, so the step is left with b in a qubit of its own. Uncomputing b there would require the decoder on the forward walk as well. The forward step instead disposes of b in one of two ways. If the decision is ambiguous at depth j ≥jstore, the step pushes b onto the history stack. Otherwise, the step removes b by MBU: it measures b in the X basis. The measurement frees the qubit but leaves a phase that depends on b and on the measurement outcome. The reverse walk is the uncomputation of the forward walk. Once the circuit has used the inverse, it undoes the forward steps in the opposite order, which restores w and returns the walk registers to zero. The matching reverse step obtains b again, either by popping the stack or by applying the tests and decoders to the updated state. It applies the phase correction for the measurement outcome and uses b to choose which update to undo. The restored g then holds b as its parity, so a CNOT from the low bit of g clears it. Decoders therefore appear only in reverse steps. A walk that stores more decisions than the stack holds overflows it and fails. The decoders evaluate the second form of Equation (A11), b = bitj−1(p(f ′q)−1 mod 2j). Both f ′ and q are odd, so the depth-j decoder is a Boolean function of 2(j −1) bits. Write fi = f ′[i] and qi = q[i] = f ′ v[j + i] for 1 ≤i < j, where u[i] is bit i of u. For the prime p of Equation (51), the decoders at depths two through five are j = 2 : b = 1 ⊕f1 ⊕q1, (56a) j = 3 : b = 1 ⊕f2 ⊕q2, (56b) j = 4 : b = 1 ⊕f1 ⊕f2 ⊕f3 ⊕q1 ⊕q2 ⊕q3 ⊕(f1 ⊕f2)(q1 ⊕q2), (56c) j = 5 : b = f3 ⊕f4 ⊕q3 ⊕q4 ⊕Mf ⊕Mq ⊕f1q1 ⊕f2q3 ⊕f3q2 ⊕f3q3 ⊕Mf(q1 ⊕q2) ⊕(f1 ⊕f2)Mq, (56d) where Mf = f1f2 ⊕f1f3 ⊕f2f3 is the majority of f1, f2, f3 and Mq is the majority of q1, q2, q3. Written out as sums of products, these four rules have 3, 3, 11, and 26 terms. The decoders at depths six, seven, and eight have 79, 268, and 1,092 terms. The growth reflects Equation (A13): the deciding bit sits above the bit of every earlier decision in the excursion, and the multiplication by q−1 carries through all of them. The circuit implements equivalent forms of these rules. The determinant identity of Equation (A3) fixes g′ v mod 2j once f ′ is known, so a decoder may read a bit of g′ v in place of bits of f ′. The depth-two decoder is Equation (A14), which costs two Toffolis. At depths three and four, the rule is an XOR of bits of q, with no products, once f ′ mod 16 is fixed. From depth five on, the rule contains products of bits of q. These rules explain where the cutoff sits. Every reverse step must contain the decoder for every depth below jstore, whether or not that step needs it. Each decoder is conditioned on a flag marking the depth, so each term that reads a register bit costs at least one Toffoli. The benefit shrinks as the cost grows: among ambiguous decisions in sampled walks, 50.1% occur at depth two, 25.0% at depth three, 12.5% at depth four, and 12.4% at all greater depths. Beyond depth four, each further depth roughly triples the number of terms and removes half as much history as the depth before it. Early stopping. A walk terminates when g reaches zero: at that point f = ±1 and the inverse can be read from fv. Different inputs terminate at different steps, typically between steps 505 and 530 (Figure 3(b)), but the circuit runs a fixed schedule of 533 steps. The steps after termination must leave the state unchanged. Left alone, the recurrence would treat g = 0 as an even value, so it would keep doubling fv and incrementing z until both outgrow their registers. The circuit therefore freezes a terminated walk. Each forward step tests whether g = 0. On the first step where the test succeeds, the circuit sets a one-qubit frozen flag, and from then on the flag disables every update. The reverse walk must clear the flag at that same step. A frozen state looks identical at every later step, so the state alone cannot reveal when the walk froze. The circuit writes the step index into a small register when it sets the flag. Each reverse step compares the register with its own index, and a match clears both the flag and the register. This machinery costs Toffolis at every step: a test that every bit of g is zero, and one more control on every update. The test is most expensive in the early steps, where g is widest and termination is least likely. The first 498 steps omit the test, the flag, and the flag’s controls. A walk that terminates within those steps fails. The measured probability is 1.4 × 10−6 per inversion, and Table S4 uses 3 × 10−6 as an upper bound. A walk whose counter z outgrows its register also fails, with probability 9 × 10−8 per inversion; the two events give the early-stopping row of Table S4. Removing the power-of-two scale.
