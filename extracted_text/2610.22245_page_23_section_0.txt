Recall from Section 3 that, given hσ = (p, yw,1, . . . , yw,N), the optimal selection policy is ϕ∗(hσ) ∈arg max i≤N Pr(Y = yw,i | P = p). Thus, the selection policy should choose the weak response most likely to be correct. Learning this probability directly requires correctness labels. We compare several selection rules that do not require ground-truth supervision, together with a supervised correctness probe: • Majority vote. Group equivalent responses and select a response from the largest group. • Token entropy. Select the response with the lowest mean token entropy under the weak model. • Disagreement (ours). Select the response with the lowest predicted weak–strong disagree- ment, ϕ(hσ) ∈arg mini≤N bfd(h(p, yw,i)). This favors responses that the strong model is more likely to agree with. • Correctness probe. Select the response with the highest predicted probability of correctness. This probe uses ground-truth correctness labels during training and serves as a supervised comparison. Selection across routing scores. Figure 7 compares these selection policies on Math-500 with N = 8 weak responses per prompt. Each panel fixes the routing score and varies the selection policy and routing budget. Majority vote achieves the highest accuracy at every reported budget for all four routing scores. This is consistent with the effectiveness of grouping equivalent final answers in mathematical reasoning. Disagreement-based selection performs similarly to the supervised correctness probe, and both improve on random selection and token entropy. Selection across sample counts.
