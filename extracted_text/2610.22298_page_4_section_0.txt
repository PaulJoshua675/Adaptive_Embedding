Complementary clinical AI systems have addressed narrower but clinically important tasks, including diagnosis prediction from longitudinal health records and automated mapping of free-text diagnoses to ICD codes Umerenkov et al. High diagnostic accuracy, however, is not equivalent to comprehensive response quality. Hager et al. developed MIMIC- CDM using 2,400 real-world cases and evaluated not only diagnosis but also information gathering, interpretation of laboratory findings, adherence to diagnostic and treatment recommendations, robustness to the order and amount of available information, and instruction-following ability Hager et al. [2024]. Their results indicate that strong diagnostic accuracy does not guarantee adequate performance across the full clinical decision-making process. Similarly, a randomized trial of management reasoning found that GPT-4 assistance can improve physician performance on open-ended patient-care tasks, but that its effect needs to be evaluated separately from diagnostic accuracy and with consideration of interaction time Goh et al. [2025]. In 2026, Medieval introduced a broad multidisciplinary blinded expert evaluation framework for diagnosis and management across 685 real and simulated clinical questions, including explicit recording of fabricated medical facts Chen et al. [2026a]. This work is particularly relevant as a precedent for multidimensional evaluation of free-form clinical responses. Reliability also requires recognizing when the available evidence is insufficient: uncertainty-aware selective-prediction methods for medical text can improve diagnostic reliability by allowing a system to abstain from uncertain decisions Vazhentsev et al. [2026]. Taken together, these studies support separately assessing case-fact utilization, quality of explanation, differential diagnosis, diagnostic workup, treatment, risk, uncertainty, and safety rather than relying on a single aggregate measure of diagnostic accuracy. 2.3 Structured Clinical Reasoning, Verification, and Test-Time Inference Medical studies of structured prompting suggest that the format of reasoning can affect output quality even when the underlying model remains unchanged. Sonoda et al. compared direct and structured prompting on the same radiology cases and observed improved diagnostic performance with an explicit clinical-reasoning structure Sonoda et al. [2025]. Savage et al. showed that diagnostic reasoning prompts can elicit more interpretable clinical rationales, although the generated chain of thought should not be interpreted as a faithful record of the model’s latent internal reasoning process Savage et al. [2024]. Shao and Zhang employed a two-stage framework with predefined verification steps on MedQA and NEJM cases and found that an explicit verification stage could improve diagnostic performance and uncertainty handling Shao and Zhang [2025]. Strong inference-time baselines are essential for interpreting such gains. Self-consistency demonstrates that simply increasing the number of independent generations and subsequently aggregating them can itself improve reasoning performance Wang et al. [2023]. Self-Refine demonstrates the value of iterative self-critique and answer revision by a single model Madaan et al. [2023]. Therefore, when a structured or council-based condition uses more model calls, more tokens, or additional critique stages, superior performance cannot automatically be attributed to the structure or to inter-agent interaction. General-domain equal-compute studies of test-time scaling further highlight the importance of comparing debate, mixtures or ensembles, and self-consistency under comparable inference budgets Wunderlich et al. [2026]. Recent medical work has further treated the decision pipeline itself as an inference-time optimization target, using LLM-guided evolution to improve accuracy–cost trade-offs in interactive consultation and other clinical decision tasks Sviridov et al. [2026]. Although these findings are methodological rather than direct clinical evidence, they provide an important basis for designing appropriate baseline comparisons. 2.4 Medical Multi-Agent Systems and the Computational Confounding Problem Multi-agent architectures in medicine are developing rapidly. MedAgents organizes LLM roles as a set of discipline- specific experts that first provide independent assessments, then engage in multiple rounds of discussion, and finally converge on a shared decision Tang et al. [2024]. MDAgents adaptively selects a solo, group, or more complex collaboration mode based on estimated task complexity and achieves strong results across several medical benchmark settings Kim et al. [2024]. MAC employs multiple doctor agents and a supervisor to diagnose rare diseases and subsequently recommend additional investigations Chen et al. [2025]. More recent systems extend this paradigm through confrontation and debate or structured MDT-style orchestration Sun et al. [2026], Liu et al. [2026a]. Related deployed systems include CLARITY, which combines structured dialogue control with collaborative LLM agents for patient routing, clinical consultation, and triage Shaposhnikov et al. [2025]. These studies establish the feasibility of multi-agent approaches but do not demonstrate that interaction itself is responsible for performance gains.
