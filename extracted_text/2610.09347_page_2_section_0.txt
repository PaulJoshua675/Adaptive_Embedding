The difference from standard genetic programming is not incidental. Once fitness stops measuring the program’s own output, the program stops being a solution and becomes one component of an answer completed elsewhere, a representation. The literature does not put it this way, and so never asks the question that follows: what is the program now responsible for? The most recent instance of the arrangement is also its simplest, and makes the question unavoidable. One evolved program supplies a single feature; its values on the data form one semantic vector, and a univariate learner is fitted from that feature to the target. Fitness is the error of the composition, and the evolutionary loop is left entirely unchanged [17], an arrangement we refer to below as LaSER. Because only the learner varies, the effect of the learner can be read directly. That work reports a linear learner dominating one benchmark suite while nonlinear learners take over on a harder one, and reports that the evolved program sometimes attains the target unaided, which it reads as a Baldwin effect. Neither result is explained. The first says which learner won where without naming any property of the targets that would transfer to a new problem, and the second is measured only at the end of a run on the easiest problems. What the learner contributes is therefore still unknown. Benchmark comparison cannot settle it, because such a comparison characterises a system, not a problem. The account has to concern the program. What makes a target hard for genetic programming is, to a large extent, how much nonlinearity the program must build by composing primitives: a deeply nested product of the right variables is hard to assemble in a way that a rescaling of it is not. Whatever nonlinearity the attached learner can supply, the program does not have to build. The question of which learner to attach is therefore the question of how much of a target’s nonlinearity a learner can take over, and the quantity that answers it is the target’s order of nonlinearity in the sense of Vladislavleva et al. [18]. The difficulty is that on the continuous benchmarks where this work is done that quantity must be estimated, and the estimate moves with the sampling range, the error tolerance and the polynomial basis. A claim of the form “a nonlinear learner is needed when the order of nonlinearity is high” cannot be tested cleanly while the quantity inside it is partly an artefact of the experimenter’s choices. The question has stayed open because it is a measurement problem before it is a scientific one. We treat it as such. On {−1, +1}n every function has a unique multilinear expansion, and because x2 i = 1 the monomials of degree at most d span precisely the multilinear terms of degree at most d; order of nonlinearity therefore coincides with Fourier degree and is computable in closed form by a Walsh-Hadamard transform [19]. The parity family supplies a dial that moves that degree from one to six while the input space, the data, the primitive set and every search parameter stay fixed. The domain is an instrument chosen to make a measurement possible, not the subject of the claim. The measurement itself is the departure: instead of inferring the division of labour from performance, we compute the order of nonlinearity of the program each run actually produced, a quantity that to our knowledge has not been reported for any system of this kind. Here we show two things: which learner is attached decides how much the program must still build, and that amount is directly visible in the programs evolution produces. Conditioned on success, a linear learner pins the order of the evolved program to the target’s exactly, at one through six with no exception in any of the thirty runs per condition, while tree ensembles succeed with programs of order two or three; success rates separate accordingly, at six and seven runs of thirty against twenty-two, nineteen and twenty-six at the highest order. The advantage is real but bounded, and the bound is informative: a 6-multiplexer control, of lower order than the parity problems where the separation is largest, gains nothing at all from a nonlinear learner, so difficulty and the appropriate learner are orthogonal. Neither component approaches the result alone: the same learners reach 0.112 held-out accuracy on the raw inputs, and evolution without a learner collapses to a constant program. A second finding follows from reading the arrangement literally. The learner is refitted every generation and its fitted parameters are never inherited, which is the structure of lifetime learning in the Baldwinian sense [20, 21]. Following the evolved program across generations shows that evolution does not internalise what the learner supplies. Across sixty-three problem and learner combinations, genetic assimilation (the genotype coming to replicate, on its own, what learning used to supply) occurs in four, three of which are canonical genetic programming, while in forty-four the contribution of learning widens over evolutionary time; on parity of order four the innate accuracy of the best individual falls from 0.713 to 0.394 while its learned accuracy rises to 1.000. The more capable the learner, the less of the model lies in the program one can read, which places predictive accuracy in conflict with the self-sufficiency of symbolic models that motivates the use of genetic programming in the first place. The contributions are as follows. We pose the division of labour between evolved program and downstream learner as a question about a measurable property of the target, and make that property exact by choice of domain. We prove that an affine learner is order preserving: it supplies no order of nonlinearity, so the evolved program must carry all of it. This places linear scaling and least squares combination at the boundary of the design space, not at a weak point inside it.
