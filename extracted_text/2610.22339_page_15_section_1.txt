A.1 RETRIEVAL TASKS AND BENCHMARKS General retrieval benchmarks evaluate different aspects of the relationship between queries and sup- porting evidence. BEIR measures retrieval across heterogeneous domains (Thakur et al., 2021), while MTEB evaluates embeddings across retrieval and other representation tasks (Muennighoff et al., 2023). BRIGHT emphasizes queries that require reasoning beyond direct semantic match- ing (Su et al., 2025), motivating specialized retrievers such as ReasonIR, INF-X-Retriever, and DIVER (Shao et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025). In mathematics, NaturalProofs eval- uates reference retrieval for natural-language proofs (Welleck et al., 2021), and Matlas supports statement-level search over mathematical literature (Ju et al., 2026). Formal proof traces provide more explicit supervision for premise use. HolStep and HOList sup- ply proof-step data from HOL Light (Kaliszyk et al., 2017; Bansal et al., 2019), while LeanDojo extracts proof states, tactics, and premise annotations from Lean developments (Yang et al., 2023). LeanSearch v2 introduces two complementary retrieval benchmarks (Gao et al., 2026): MathlibQR evaluates queries paired with individual target declarations, whereas MathlibMPR annotates premise groups and alternative proof routes. Their metrics distinguish locating a relevant declaration from covering the supporting premises along a complete route. Our evaluation combines these retrieval measures with downstream proof completion to examine whether the retrieved declarations help construct checked proofs. A.2 FORMAL THEOREM-PROVING SYSTEMS Premise selection has long connected mathematical libraries to automated reasoning. Sledgehammer and CoqHammer select library facts for external provers and reconstruct successful proofs within a proof assistant (Paulson & Blanchette, 2012; Czajka & Kaliszyk, 2018). Thor integrates language models with hammer-based automation (Jiang et al., 2022), and Magnushammer learns premise se- lection through contrastive training and reranking (Mikuła et al., 2024). Neural provers also explore proof states through learned tactic selection, as in HOList and HyperTree Proof Search (Bansal et al., 2019; Lample et al., 2022). ReProver and REAL-Prover incorporate retrieved Lean premises into this stepwise proving process (Yang et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2025). Recent work improves the prover through mathematical pretraining (Azerbayev et al., 2024), scaf- folded data synthesis and self-correction (Lin et al., 2026), and reinforcement learning with formal verification (Xin et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2025; Hubert et al., 2026). Informal reasoning provides another source of guidance: Draft, Sketch, and Prove generates formal sketches from informal proofs (Jiang et al., 2023), while Lean-STaR, Hilbert, and LEAP connect informal reasoning to formal tactics or subproblems (Lin et al., 2025; Varambally et al., 2026; Kung et al., 2026). These approaches improve proof generation and decomposition. Our experiments hold the prover fixed to study how access to library declarations affects proof completion. Library structure and prior attempts can also guide formal reasoning. LEGO-Prover retrieves veri- fied lemmas from a growing library (Wang et al., 2024), while RAutoformalizer and Aria use depen- dency information and retrieval during statement autoformalization (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). For premise selection, Petrovˇciˇc et al. (2025) incorporate dependencies into graph-aware representations. LEANGRAPHSEARCH instead uses query-conditioned dependency traversal to ex- pand the candidates considered by a semantic reranker. During proof construction, COPRA records unsuccessful tactics and uses execution feedback to guide search (Thakur et al., 2024); Reflexion more generally stores reflections on failed attempts (Shinn et al., 2023). Our optional failure mem- ory associates unsuccessful repairs with recurring formal failure states, complementing the library evidence supplied by retrieval. A.3 THEOREM-PROVING BENCHMARKS Formal proving benchmarks cover competition problems, textbook mathematics, and existing li- brary developments. miniF2F, FIMO, and PutnamBench evaluate competition-oriented mathemat- ics (Zheng et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Tsoukalas et al., 2024), while ProverBench adds a further collection of formalized problems, including AIME questions (Ren et al., 2025).
