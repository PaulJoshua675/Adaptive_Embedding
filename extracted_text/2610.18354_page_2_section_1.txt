Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-agent settings as part of a larger compound system. However, a growing share of deployers treat the inter-agent dynamics, competitive and cooperative alike, as a black box. This makes it hard to build systems that remain capable, efficient, and reliable. Existing work addresses pieces of this problem in isolation. The strategic decision-making literature focuses on a fixed model’s play under a specified game setting [7, 28, 37]. In parallel, LLM routing addresses model selection under performance-cost trade-off [5, 26, 31], but with a static task distribution and no notion of a strategic structure or opponent. Neither accounts for the fact that contemporary LLM portfolios differ not only in scale and cost, but also in the type and quality of strategic behaviour they exhibit. Our work reframes model selection itself as a strategic decision. Concretely, we extend the game- theoretic evaluations and define strategic selection as the opponent-conditioned meta game layered on top of the base game. Here the base game is played between the fielded LLMs, while the meta game is played between two strategic selectors that field a model of their choice each round. In repeated settings, the dynamic and adaptive nature of this problem (as opposed to traditional routers operating on a fixed distribution of tasks) means our selection should be strategic. Beyond balancing the performance-cost trade-offs, such a selection layer can help steer the deployer’s general play, adding control over the reliability and safety of the interaction (Figure 1). We ask the question: Given LLM strategic capabilities vary across different games and models, can a selector exploit this by learning which model to field against an unknown and possibly adaptive opponent? Why This Matters. Traditional routers operate under the assumption of a fixed task distribution, learning a static mapping from queries to optimal models. In contrast, operating against an adaptive opponent transforms this into a non-stationary learning problem, where the optimal strategy can continuously shift over time. A further challenge arises from the partial observability of the meta game’s payoff matrix. In practice, a selector cannot observe the opponent’s net gain, since the opponent’s model choice is unknown, nor can it observe the counterfactual payoffs of fielding an alternative model in the current game state. We also acknowledge that LLMs are stochastic models. In strategic selection, we introduce an additional layer of environmental noise through the dynamic responses of the opponent. Therefore, benchmarking and result reporting call for a careful statistical treatment. Our contributions are threefold: • Strategic Model Selection. We formalise strategic selection as a repeated general-sum meta game between two players. Beyond capability and cost, this lets us treat the interaction itself as a first-class design consideration for model selection. • Quantifying Noise in Strategic Play. We characterise the signal and noise levels across games, and suggest adapting methods from traditional hypothesis testing to decide on the number of seeds per a game-portfolio pair. • Empirical Investigation. We first show that LLM strategic capabilities are scenario-dependent and exploitable by a selector.
