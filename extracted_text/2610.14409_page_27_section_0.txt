J EXPERIMENTAL IMPLEMENTATION This appendix gives the settings needed to reproduce the runs of Section 5, and the implementation choices that the PCN formulation requires but a feedforward one does not. Code is available at https://github.com/alexgower/EnergyMatchingEqProp. Shared recipe. Table 6 gives the settings shared by all arms, and the relaxation settings of the two PCN arms. The three arms of Section 5 share the architecture, optimiser, learning rate, schedule, effective batch size, EMA decay, step budget and data pipeline; the norm- and attention-free arm differs as App. I describes. What they do not share is the random realisation: there is no global seed in the trainer, so each arm has its own initialisation, its own noise samples for the optimal-transport coupling and the contrastive negatives, and its own dropout masks. With one run per arm we have no estimate of run-to-run variance, and the differences in Table 2 should be read with that in mind. The implicit-solve arm holds x fixed at xt and differentiates the flow loss on vPCN(xt) with respect to θ, using the implicit function theorem for the dependence of h∗on θ, so it uses no tether; the tethered objective LPCN approaches this fixed-x one as γ/λ →0 (App. B). Asynchronous relaxation. Nodes are updated in even/odd sweeps (Kerjan et al., 2026), the second half-step seeing the first’s updated values, so one sweep costs two energy evaluations. On a chain this is exactly Gauss–Seidel, since same-parity nodes share no energy term; on the DAG of App. D a skip may connect two same-parity nodes, so the decoupling is only approximate. We adopt it because Kerjan et al. (2026) find synchronous updates substantially worse at the same step size, and sometimes divergent; we did not repeat that comparison here. The fixed point is the same either way. Frozen dropout. A PCN training step evaluates the network many times: Tfree sweeps during the free phase and Tnudge in each nudged phase, each with Kh inner sweeps, where a feedforward step evaluates it once. Standard dropout would draw a fresh mask at every evaluation, so each sweep would relax a different energy and there would be no single equilibrium for the free phase to reach or for the finite difference to compare. We therefore use one mask per sample, held fixed across every sweep and phase of that sample’s training step. It is drawn as standard dropout draws it, so only the redrawing frequency differs, and for the feedforward arms — a single evaluation per step — the two coincide. FID protocol. All FIDs are computed from 50k samples against the full training set, with EMA weights, and Euler–Heun integration at dt = 0.01. The stopping time τs is chosen per model by sampling over a ladder of values and taking the minimum: phase-1 checkpoints are best at the noiseless endpoint τs = 1, and after the contrastive phase the optimum moves well past it (App. I). The τs = 3.25 chosen as optimal for the backpropagation arm is also near-optimal for both PCN arms, which a paired sweep of τs confirms. For the ImageNet-32 checkpoints we use the authors’ τs = 2.5. Measurement resolution and replication. To gauge the measurement’s resolution we evaluated the backpropagation arm’s phase-1 checkpoint at three sampling seeds: the values span 0.064. Changing how the 50k samples are divided across devices moves a fixed-seed evaluation by a similar amount, and unseeded draws of the same checkpoint span 0.15. We therefore treat differences be- low about 0.2 as unresolved. Our replication reaches 3.54 after phase 2 where the published model reports 3.34; the authors’ released checkpoint scores 3.44 under our protocol, so part of the gap is the sampler and part the replication.
