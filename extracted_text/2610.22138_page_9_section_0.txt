One practical way is to record each agent’s answer privately at every round, so that hidden answers are visible even when the transcript looks unchanged. Limitations We compare only groups of two and ten agents and do not vary network topology, or model mix- tures. The attacker acts only in context; parametric attacks such as knowledge editing are a different mechanism. All labels come from an LLM judge. On pri- vate answers, it agrees closely with an independent judge from another model family (§3.5). Public stance in free-form chat is harder to label than a one-sentence answer, and implicit endorsements are easy to miss, so public endorsement rates may be underestimated. Our private probe conditions on the agent’s memory; “private belief” thus means the answer an agent gives from its current context, and other probe wordings could yield somewhat different rates. Whether prompting agents to correct the claim early protects the group remains untested but could be evaluated at low cost. Ethical Considerations We study how a few agents that repeat a false claim can mislead the rest of a group. The attack needs no skill beyond taking part in a conversation. We introduce no new attack technique: the attacker prompt is a plain instruction (Appendix A). Data and participants. The study involves no human participants and collects no personal data; every message is generated by a model. Items come from public benchmarks. All models are used through their providers’ APIs under the providers’ terms, and no model is trained or fine-tuned. Use of AI assistants. Separately, we used AI assistants (Claude Code) to help write and debug code for running experiments and to improve the grammar and wording of author-written text.
