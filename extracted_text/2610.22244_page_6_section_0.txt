A mid-sized guard that over-blocks has not lost the ability to recognise an attack; it has become more suspicious of everything, and the extra blocks fall on benign steps because there are more of them. A small guard that under-blocks has lost the signal: with a request it parses less well, an unsafe action looks like every other action. Under the strict decision rule (block only if composite = 1), the placebo-adjusted flip rates under translation grow further for the guards that move (Nemotron 3.5 +5.7 pp, Nemotron 3 Nano 4B +5.1, Qwen 3.5 4B +2.9, TS-Guard +2.6) while every frontier guard stays within 0.2 pp under both rules (Appendix C). 4.3 The effect grows as the language gets rarer Table 6 (Appendix) pools each guard’s pairs by language tier (Joshi et al. classes 5, 4 and 2) before bootstrapping. For the six guards below 13B parameters, the mean placebo-adjusted flip rate rises monotonically with rarity: +0.8 pp on French, +1.3 German, +2.5 Turkish, +2.8 Swahili, +3.2 Amharic (the five general small models alone: +0.7, +1.1, +2.0, +2.5, +3.0). The four purpose-built guards follow the same order at a higher level (+2.5, +3.4, +4.3, +6.4, +6.2; §4.4), the open 12–30B guards at a lower one (+0.7, +1.1, +1.6 on the pre-registered languages; +1.0 and +2.1 on Swahili and Amharic for the two run there), and the frontier guards stay below +0.5 pp everywhere. Per guard, the low-resource tier is at least as bad as the high-resource tier for every guard that moves at all. It is the worst tier for Nemotron 3 Nano 4B (+0.6 →+4.1 →+7.2 pp from high through mid to low), Nemotron 3.5 30B (+1.2 →+2.5 →+3.2), Nemotron Nano 9B (+1.5 →+1.6 →+2.2) and Gemma 4 12B (+0.4 →+0.6 →+0.8). For TS-Guard, Qwen 3.5 9B and the self-hosted Gemma 4 26B, Turkish is marginally worse than the low tier; Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen 3.8 27B do not move in any tier. The error shifts are guard-specific rather than class-wide. Pooled over the five general small guards, ∆TPR is −0.7 [−1.3, −0.1] on high-, −1.2 [−2.0, −0.3] on mid- and −0.9 [−1.8, −0.0] pp on low-resource languages, and ∆FPR turns positive on the low tier (+0.9 [+0.3, +1.4]): the recall loss is carried by Nemotron Nano 9B (−3.8 →−4.3 →−5.5), while the two 4B guards drift towards blocking on Swahili and Amharic. For the over-blockers, Amharic is the worst single language in the study (Nemotron 3.5 +5.0 pp FPR, Gemma 4 26B +3.6, Gemma 4 12B +2.8, Qwen 3.5 9B +2.5), while Swahili is not: its flips are as frequent as Turkish’s, but the error shifts scatter in both directions (two significant FPR increases against three decreases, including TS-Guard at −2.2 pp), so Swahili scrambles verdicts without a consistent bias. Amharic is also the only non-Latin script in the set; separating script from resource level needs more languages than we ran. The pre-registered languages already showed the ordering: Turkish, the mid-resource language, was harder than German and French for every guard (seven guards with a significant FPR increase against five and zero; TS-Guard +4.8 pp placebo-adjusted, 9.3% raw). The low-resource pair, added afterwards, extends it: the two largest raw flip rates in the study are Nemotron 3 Nano 4B on Swahili (10.8%) and Amharic (11.1%), and the guard ranking is least stable under Amharic among the languages run on the same roster (τ = 0.93 against 0.99 for Swahili). 4.4 Purpose-built agent guards are the most language-dependent The four guards trained specifically to judge agent actions, run with their authors’ own prompts, are the most language-dependent guards in the study. “Language-dependent” means verdict instability, not harm: ShieldAgent’s error rates improve under translation, and the comparison is within guard. Their mean placebo-adjusted flip rate under translation is +3.4 pp (TS-Guard), +2.9 (Safiron), +4.3 (AgentDoG) and +7.7 (ShieldAgent), against +1.4, +1.3, +1.8 and +3.5 under English paraphrase, and each rises from the high- to the low-resource tier (TS-Guard +2.0 →+4.8 →+4.1, Safiron +1.9 →+3.2 →+3.7, AgentDoG +2.5 →+2.7 →+7.0, ShieldAgent +5.5 →+6.7 →+10.4). ShieldAgent changes 12.1% of its verdicts on Swahili and 11.9% on Amharic (its placebo floor is 1.6%). The direction differs by guard and does not follow the size rule of §4.2. ShieldAgent, which blocks 44% of benign steps in English, blocks fewer of them in every other language (−7.6 pp German, −5.4 French, −3.8 Turkish, −5.3 Swahili, −3.8 Amharic, all significant) while its recall holds. Safiron, the weakest of the four (recall 0.41), loses recall on Turkish (−3.4 pp), Swahili (−2.8) and Amharic (−3.1); AgentDoG flags every unsafe trajectory in every language and moves only on benign steps; TS-Guard over-blocks on German, Turkish and Amharic and under-blocks on Swahili. What the four share is that a translated request changes the operating point of a guard whose documented training data, prompts and benchmarks are English, by more than any general-purpose model of the same size.
