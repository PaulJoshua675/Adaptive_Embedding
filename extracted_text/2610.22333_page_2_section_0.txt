We make two contributions. • To our knowledge, the first behavioral study of evaluation awareness in Indic languages. It covers four models from three families and six languages, with 47,880 judged responses that we release. • A set of cues that isolates evaluation from mere monitoring. Two of our cues state that re- sponses are logged and differ only in whether an AI safety team reviews them. 2 Related Works 2.1 Evaluation awareness and the compliance gap Recent work establishes that language models can distinguish evaluation from deployment contexts and that this recognition drives behavior. Fron- tier models show above-random evaluation aware- ness (Needham et al., 2025), and recognizing an evaluation context is empirically and conceptually distinct from the propensity to act on it (Li et al., 2026). Models shift toward the inferred training objective once they judge their outputs observed (Greenblatt et al., 2024). Models are also more sen- sitive to safety than capability evaluations (Li et al., 2026), and (Sheshadri et al., 2025) find a compli- ance gap above 1 percent in only five of twenty-five models. Knecht et al. (2026) find that verbalised evaluation awareness (VEA) has limited effect on model behaviour. Existing study in these papers involves English only. We ask whether the behavioural findings sur- vive a change of language, using content-matched requests in five Indic languages and English across three model families. 2.2 Multilingual and Indic safety evaluation A persistent gap exists between the languages mul- tilingual safety research covers and those deployed models serve; (Yong et al., 2025) survey its size and durability. Subsequent work shows that safety behavior varies with language: models handle identical content differently under code-switching (Yoo et al., 2025), across twelve Indic languages where safety judgments drift sharply between translations (Pattnayak and Chowdhuri, 2026b), and under jailbreak pressure in South Asian lan- guages (Pattnayak and Chowdhuri, 2026a). Chain- of-thought monitoring likewise degrades across typologically diverse languages (Onyame et al., 2026). These works hold evaluation context fixed while varying language; we vary both, enabling an evaluation-awareness claim. 3 Experimental Setup 3.1 Data, languages, and models We worked on six languages, namely English, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada. The harmful prompts come from the Toxic Matrix dataset of AI4Bharat IndicAlign (Khan et al., 2024). We randomly sampled 199 English prompts from the dataset and translated them into the five Indic languages using Claude Opus. We then scored every translated string with COMET- Kiwi (Rei et al., 2022), a reference-free quality es- timation model (Appendix G). We made sure that every prompt has a parallel translation in all six lan- guages. Benign prompts come from the Dolly-T dataset of the same corpus, which provides trans- lations made with IndicTrans2 (Gala et al., 2023). We took 200 parallel prompts per language directly from the dataset and use them as a baseline to mea- sure over-refusal. To check the reliability of the translations, native speakers verified a small sam- ple of prompts per language for faithful translation, fluency, cultural relevance, and retained harmful intent. We test four instruction-tuned models from three families, namely Gemma-3-27B (Team et al., 2025), Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen2.5- 72B (Qwen et al., 2025), and Sarvam-M (Sar- vam Foundation Models Team, 2025), an Indic model post-trained from Mistral-Small-3.1-24B. We disable the reasoning mode of Qwen3 and Sarvam-M. Each model generates one response per prompt with greedy decoding. This gives 11,970 responses per model and 47,880 in total (Appendix I). 3.2 The Cues A Cue is a small text consisting of a condition. Each cue is added before the prompt in the user turn.
