Turn Generation When asked to roleplay as a patient, LMs generally drift towards a cooperative and insightful answer (Louie et al., 2024; Hoang et al., 2026). To mitigate this, we split response generation in two decisions. First, the patient LM commits to a move by calling a commit_move tool (Figure 6), which must be accepted before verbalisation. These moves operationalize dialogue acts within mental health contexts 3 and allow for better steering of behaviours by changing which moves are available. The call carries a short piece of reasoning, a primary move from a closed vocabulary of 39 moves (some of which are removed depending on the archetype), an optional secondary move, and a flag that ends the session after the turn. Table 23 presents the full taxonomy, grouped into four classes. Disclosing moves bring new material into the conversation (e.g., share, go deeper, voice an insight, change talk). Concealing moves hold it back (deflect, minimise, disengage). Expressive moves carry affect (e.g., sadness, anxiety, shame, hopelessness, relief, humour, self-criticism). Engaging moves keep the conversation going with- out revealing or hiding anything (e.g., answer a question, agree, disagree, ask for advice, seek validation, resist, set a boundary). The harness accepts the move and replies with a short writing instruction that is informed by the fixed writing style. Finally, the model writes the response that realises the move. Between the two steps, the model may also update the session agenda. In preliminary experiments, committing moves through a tool call made patients more steerable and varied than direct prompting, likely owing to the extensive tool-use training of current LMs (Baldo et al., 2026). Multi Session Each patient is simulated over an arc of sessions. A session is defined by a session scenario that narrates the context before the session begins, namely (1) what has happened since the last session; (2) the thoughts, behaviours, and signs it produced; (3) the goal the patient brings to the new session; (4) a short agenda of concrete things they mean to get to; and (5) an opening message. The first scenario is generated from the profile, and is focused on the experience since the precipitating event. The goal in the first scenario is related to the main goal of help-seeking. Subsequent session scenarios are generated by an LLM conditioned on the profile, the previous scenario, the full previous session, the time elapsed, and the agenda items the patient never got to in the previous session. The gap before each follow-up session (6 hours to 14 days) and a category for between-session events (a setback, a plateau, a breakthrough, a rupture, an external event or a new problem) are sampled in advance and held fixed across counsellors. The sampled category constrains scenario generation, while the concrete events are generated using the preceding conversation. Session style. Two style knobs also change across sessions and allow for further diversity. For each session, we sample the emotion closest to the surface from Ekman’s six basic emotions (Ekman, 1992). Concealment propensity (willingness to disclose) starts at a sampled level and may decrease on a fixed schedule shared across counsellors. Memory. Continuity across sessions is achieved from a single running memory (Figure 16). After each session, an LM rewrites the memory to cover the session that just ended. This memory is frame-agnostic and objective, and is shared between the counsellor LM and the simulated patient. Each entry is one third-person sentence recording a concrete event, preference, constraint, struggle or commitment. Prior entries are carried forward verbatim, with one exception: a commitment the patient has not yet reported back on is tagged as open, and is rewritten once they do. This memory is capped at 30 entries and when the cap is exceeded, the model is instructed to drop the least significant items. 4.3 Validation We validate the realism of our simulated patients through human judgments, surface statistics, and con- versation dynamics. We select a pool of 100 patients and generate the first session with up to 15 patient turns4 using Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026c) and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) as the patient model and four counsellor LMs: a proprietary model, GPT-5.6 Terra (OpenAI, 2026a), GLM- 5.2 (GLM-5 Team, 2026) and Claude Sonnet 4.6. We compare Mindsim against the patient simulators proposed in MindEval (Pombal et al., 2025) and Patient-Ψ (Wang et al., 2024), which receive the same patients through their own prompt templates. To compare simulated patients with real users, we include 1,584 sessions from 886 patients of an online mental health app. These sessions were anonymised and de-identified before analysis, are reported only in aggregate, and are not released.
