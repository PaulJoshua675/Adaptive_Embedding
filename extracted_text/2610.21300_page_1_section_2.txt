Demonstrating that a medical device complies with regulation is expert work that must leave a record. Experts interpret the applicable requirements, judge whether the documents and records—technical files, test reports and data held in electronic quality man- agement systems—meet them, and record each judg- ment with its evidence. The work recurs with every submission, design change and audit, on the manu- facturer’s side and on the side of notified bodies and regulators, and the records are kept for at least ten years [1]; a notified body must document its confor- mity assessment so that its conclusions “can represent objective evidence of such compliance to persons that are not themselves involved in the assessment” [1], and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) reviews the substance of a 510(k) (premarket notifica- tion) submission only after a completeness screening [2, 3]. Large language models (LLMs) could help with this work [4–6]. We take a limited view of their role. Deciding which regulations, standards and guidance apply, and stating what must be shown as assess- ment items, remains the experts’ work. For each item that experts define, an LLM can prepare a prelim- inary assessment—a label such as Satisfied or Not satisfied, a short reason and the passages it relies on, quoted verbatim—for a qualified expert to check against the source; the judgment, and the responsibil- ity for it, stay with the expert, whose decision, not the preliminary assessment, enters the quality record [7]. Regulators’ guidance recommends exactly this form of evidence, quoted sentences that can be checked [6], and warns against the documented tendency of even expert users to over-rely on an automated suggestion [8–10].
