On the other hand, models can directly lie to the user, for example, faking alignment (Greenblatt et al., 2024); claiming task completion that they didn’t achieve (Greenblatt, 2026; Smyth et al., 2026); telling users what they think the user wants to hear, even when it is not factually correct (Fanous et al., 2025); or simply making claims that contradicts some knowledge, whether it is a fact learnt during training or provided in context. In this paper, we particularly focus on this latter, which Ren et al. (2025) show does not go away even when the models get larger/more capable. Different techniques exist to control a model’s propensity to lie. Activation steering alters the model’s behaviour at inference time by intervening on its activations (Zou et al., 2025; Turner et al., 2024; Herbster et al., 2026). Nonetheless, benign steering can lead to increased vulnerability to jailbreaks (Xiong et al., 2026), decreased capability, and unintended changes in a model’s behaviour that’s not the targeted trait (Siu et al., 2026). Others consider honest prompting (Wang et al., 2025), emphasizing honesty in the system prompt or assigning models an honest persona; however, a sin- gle prompt that induces honesty across different conditions may be hard or impossible to design. Another popular approach is supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on harmful system prompts along with honest responses; yet, this requires large corpora of synthetic data (Wang et al., 2025), and may not generalize out of distribution. An open problem in the field is to be able to find a single intervention that works across different manifestations of dishonesty. This could be seen as a robustness problem, where a method is defined as robust when it mitigates dishonesty independently of how it arises. One potential approach to address this is Continous Adversarial Training (CAT) (Xhonneux et al., 2024), initially proposed to make a model’s refusal behaviour robust against a variety of jailbreaks. Here we modified CAT to make a model’s honest behaviour robust to three different forms of dishonesty: 1) instructed dishonesty, where a system prompt contains explicit pressure to lie; 2) dishonesty that emerges from incentives in a multi-agent game, where being dishonest increases expected reward; and 3) dishonesty that results from having trained a model to conceal the truth. We name this approach Continous Adversarial Training for Consistent Honesty (CATCH) and summarize it in Figure 1. Our contributions are as follows: • We introduce CATCH, a method that makes a model learn to exhibit honest behaviours across conditions, while highly preserving capabilities. • The learnt honest behaviour is robust and consistent: it reduces instructed dishonesty in MASK (Ren et al., 2025), counteracts dishonesty resulting from game incentives in Among Us (Golechha & Garriga-Alonso, 2025), and unveils dishonesty learned through training in AuditBench (Sheshadri et al., 2026). • Despite using only around 200 instances of training data taken from Herbster et al. (2026), our method outperforms all baseline methods and is on par with their SOTA honesty steer- ing on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen3.6-27B, particularly surpassing an implementa- tion of honesty-SFT trained on roughly 50 times as much honesty data (Wang et al., 2025) on MASK. • We study the effect of the utility dataset used during training, and find a novel approach to choosing a utility dataset that allows higher general capability preservation, outperforming the SOTA honesty steering. 2
