We compare RELAGRAM with DeepACO after training both for 40,000 optimizer updates. DeepACO is stronger in eight of nine size–decoder regimes: it achieves 3.94-point and 3.49-point lower gaps on CVRP-100 under greedy and 256-candidate decoding, and a 9.33-point lower greedy gap on CVRP-1000. RELAGRAM does not yet achieve solution quality competitive with DeepACO. Under 256 candidates, the two converge as instances grow: ants sample every step, so DeepACO’s candidate scaling weakens with size while RELAGRAM’s does not. In the matched ablation, the single-pass model achieves slightly lower greedy gaps than RELAGRAM at 10k and 20k updates (18.47/17.60% versus 18.89/17.86%; Appendix A). Neither difference is statistically significant: the comparison provides no evidence of an advantage from recursion. Recursion as refinement and search. RELAGRAM improves from its first stage to the trained fourth stage at every size (Table 2). Progress is non-monotonic: a later stage may worsen before producing a better route, so the deepest output is not always the strongest. Applying the loop further yields gains that are negligible at the trained size but grow with instance size: the best observed gap beyond depth four improves on the depth-four gap by 0.05, 0.74, and 1.45 points on CVRP-100, CVRP-500, and CVRP-1000 at depths six, eleven, and ten, respectively. 5 Discussion and Conclusion RELAGRAM shows that a small latent-recursive model successfully learns to route. It transfers without retraining to graphs up to ten times larger and can improve beyond its trained depth, suggesting promising inference-time depth scaling. The model does not reach state-of-the-art results, and the matched greedy comparison finds no significant advantage from recursion. We therefore see RELAGRAM as a proof of concept for applying latent recursion to graph combinatorial optimization. We hypothesize that an important limitation is the sparse RL signal, which needs further engineering for latent recursion. Importantly, y and z receive only indirect RL signal, making learning harder. Lastly, RELAGRAM’s recursive nature may be very useful for dynamic problems. The loop already runs zero-shot past the conditions it was trained under; if it handles an updated instance as well as extra depth, a single model with a decoder could solve such problems end-to-end, by recurrently processing the last latent state and the updated problem. 4
