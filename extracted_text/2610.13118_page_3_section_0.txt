However, it is not obvious that an innate repertoire is helpful when the task is social. A reflex designed for an isolated agent could interfere with behaviors that depend on the partner. In this work, we address both questions with a system built from scratch to avoid the aforementioned problems. Two e-puck robots [14], reconstructed from their official model in Webots [15] and reproduced in the PyBullet physics simulator [16], must find a food zone that they only detect by passing over it with their floor sensors. Neither robot receives information about the food's position. The only communication channel is a one-bit signal, the direction and intensity of which are perceived by the receiver. Each robot is controlled by a recurrent network with GRU units [17] and a residual connection, which acts alone or as an additional channel within the EASA basal ganglia architecture. Fitness is implicit. It only rewards the time the robots spend over the food, without rewarding transmission or approach [1]. Based on this, we manipulated two factors. The first is the need for information, which we varied by giving the robots a compass to guide them toward food and by changing the size of the arena. The second is the architecture, which ranged from the network alone to various versions of the basal ganglia, from manually set priorities to priorities that evolved along with the network. Each condition was replicated with independent runs. The signal function was evaluated with paired ablations, behavioral diagnostics, and robustness tests under conditions the robots did not encounter during evolution. The contributions are fourfold. The first is an open model of two e-pucks with one-bit communication, designed so that other groups can replicate and extend the experiments. The second shows that the signal disappears when the information it provides becomes available through another channel. The third shows that, when communication is useful but not essential, independent runs arrive at different solutions with the same performance, and that a more difficult task does not increase the frequency of communication, but does favor the referential signal over other strategies. The fourth shows that, in this social task, the innate reflexes of the basal ganglia architecture constrain the learned network, and that the evolution of its priorities tends to diminish their influence.
