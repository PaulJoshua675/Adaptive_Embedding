Decentralized LLM Workflows via Sequential Auctions In practice, this perfect-clone assumption breaks down due to two fundamental economic realities. First is a participation barrier: with exact clones, the expected highest and second-highest bids are identical. The winning agent pays exactly their true valuation, driving their net profit to strictly zero. Because participating in a market carries inherent risk, agents will simply sit out rather than opt in to risk their resources for a net-zero expected return. Second is the anti-cloning incentive: perfect competition will not naturally arise because no rational actor will clone their own agent (which cannibalizes their own profit margin), and participants will actively protect their models’ intellectual property from being copied by competitors. If perfect clones are theoretically unprofitable and strategically irrational, does the market collapse? We first demonstrate that the opposite extreme—absence of competition (a monopsony at any step in the task chain)—does indeed cause the market to fail. When an agent is the sole eligible buyer for a given state, they act as a monopsonist, bidding the absolute minimum required to win. Without competitive pressure, the clearing price collapses, and downstream value is not efficiently extracted and passed upstream. This breaks the sequential credit assignment required to sustain a multi-step pipeline, as upstream agents are starved of the revenue needed to offset their own inference costs. To bridge this gap, we adapt the framework of Chang et al. (2020) to societies of Large Language Models (LLMs), formulating a decentralized, sequential forward auction mechanism tailored to the operational realities of modern LLM pipelines. In doing so, we shift the theoretical focus to the critical, realistic middle ground: imperfect competition. We model societies populated by LLM agents that occupy similar capability niches but are differentiated by heterogeneous inference costs, prompt engineering, and localized heuristics. Because off-the-shelf language models are notoriously miscalibrated judges of their own success probabilities and token costs (Fradkin and Krishnan, 2026), agents cannot rely on zero-shot prompting to evaluate states. Instead, each agent learns a lightweight neural valuation policy via reinforcement learning to navigate the tradeoff between winning the right to act and shading their bids to secure a positive profit margin. In this paper, we present a comprehensive empirical study of decentralized LLM routing under imperfect competition. Our core contributions include: 1. Decentralized LLM Markets and Imperfect Competition: We introduce an architectural and algorithmic framework for sequential auction MDPs in LLM societies, demonstrating experimentally that imperfect competition is sufficient to sustain positive profit margins and enable viable multi- agent pipelines under traditional auction mechanisms. 2. Monopsony Failure Validation: We reveal empirically through a targeted synthetic domain that absence of competition collapses the market, establishing imperfect competition as a viable operational regime under traditional auction mechanisms. 3. Algorithmic Bidding Strategies: We formulate and benchmark four bidding algorithms—Mirror Descent, Thompson Sampling, Bayes-Optimal, and PPO—demonstrating how individual agents can efficiently learn optimal pricing and bid-shading policies. 4. Extensive Benchmarking: We deploy our framework across diverse LLM domains (including a math reasoning task and newly-constructed synthetic news market).
