Recurring expressions in model-written text are often treated as signatures of machine au- thorship, yet most of them serve legitimate functions. Whether such an expression is a tic depends on whether the model knows when it is called for. This judgment of appropri- ateness, central to communicative competence, remains largely unexamined for language mod- els. We study it through the Mandarin correc- tive construction bushi X, ershi Y (‘not X, but Y’). Drawing on Gricean pragmatics and cor- rective negation, we introduce a decision tree that classifies uses as substantive, optional, or defective by testing whether the context makes X a live alternative and whether rejecting X is informative. Five commercial models use the construction 2.5 to 9.2 times as densely as a human reference. Of 307 annotated uses, only 1.30% are defective while 52.8% are optional, that is, correct but unneeded, so models fall short in appropriateness far more often than in correctness. Several reward models favor the construction even over quality-matched vari- ants, an LLM judge favors it even where the context does not license it, and both supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization tend to raise its frequency in open-weight models. We hope this work encourages the development of models attentive to the appropriateness of their expressions. 1
