It primarily adjusted synaptic weights, though later extensions (Schrauwen and Van Campenhout, 2004) added delay learning to the same gradient scheme. In parallel, the tempotron (G¨utig and Sompolinsky, 2006) offered a single-neuron supervised rule that shaped the sub- threshold voltage trace so the neuron would or would not cross threshold in response to a given spatiotemporal spike pattern: a landmark demonstration that precise timing information could be exploited by a gradient rule, even though it too operated on weights rather than delays. The Time-Delay Neural Networks (TDNN) (Waibel et al., 1989) is another historical example of the interest of integrating synaptic delays in neural networks. In modern deep learning, there exists some trials to adapt SNNs to learning in time dimension. A first line of work consists in finding a closed-form solution for the spiking time of post-synaptic neurons (Mostafa, 2017; Comsa et al., 2020; G¨oltz et al., 2021). The gradient of such models is then derived from the spiking time of the neurons, and thus the learning can happen in the temporal dimension. However, this method suffers from several limitations. First, finding an analytical solution for the spiking time of neurons is not an easy task, and (Comsa et al., 2020) and (G¨oltz et al., 2021) have to resort to approximations. In that regard, the DeNN completely bypasses the difficulty associated with finding an analytical solution to the spiking time, by direct computation. Second, the solution built by this method is strictly confined to specific models. The method used by (Mostafa, 2017; Comsa et al., 2020; G¨oltz et al., 2021) is the same (even though the techniques used may differ), but applied to different models. Thus, if we wished to change the model, it may prove difficult to use their method. Finally, they tested their models against static images datasets, such as MNIST (Le Cun et al., 1998) or CIFAR (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), which do not have any temporal dimension. The DeNN used here belongs to the TTFS framework built by (Mostafa, 2017; Comsa et al., 2020; G¨oltz et al., 2021), but offers a change in the method. Consequently, it could be tested against neuromorphic datasets, including a highly temporal one. The DeNN showed state-of-the-art results on the GSC datatset, proving that models built in the TTFS framework can represent temporal information, as long as they clearly manipulate the temporal dimension. Another line of work consists in finding exact gradient formulation for spiking models (Wunderlich and Pehle, 2021; Lee et al., 2023; Ferdowsi and Aral, 2025; G¨oltz et al., 2025). EventProp (Wunderlich and Pehle, 2021) was further extended to synaptic delay learning (M´esz´aros et al., 2025) and learning of membrane and synaptic timescales (Nowotny et al., 2025), and showed good results when tested against complex temporal datasets. While it constitutes an interesting path, it is important to note that gradient computation in DeNN is straightforward, and can be computed directly with Automatic Differentiation libraries, while exact gradients for LIF neurons have to be hard coded. Works in (Lee et al., 2023; Ferdowsi and Aral, 2025; G¨oltz et al., 2025) were not tested against temporal datasets. More recently, (Hammouamri et al., 2023) reparameterized delays as learnable positions within dilated convolution kernels (DCLS), allowing continuous, gradient-based optimization of delays with far fewer parameters than equivalent recurrent architectures, and reaching state-of-the-art results on datasets such as the Spiking Heidelberg Digits (SHD) and Spiking Speech Commands (SSC) (Cramer et al., 2022). 6
