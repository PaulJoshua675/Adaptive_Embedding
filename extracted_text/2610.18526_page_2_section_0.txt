Among languages competing in- side the mixed index, it asks whether they con- verge. They do not, and the divergence follows two regimes: some models crowd a query with its own language, while others pull the ranking toward En- glish. The pattern is directional: a language’s doc- uments stay reachable by other languages’ queries, while its own queries retrieve almost exclusively within that same language (Lim et al., 2025). Disagreement is also actionable. Knowing which documents other languages retrieve and which they miss gives not only the size of the gap but also a direction across it. We introduce disagreement-guided steering: contrast the doc- uments other languages agree on for a question against those a language retrieves idiosyncratically, and add the difference of their representations to that language’s queries at inference. This requires no neuron attribution, no training, and no relevance labels; its directions are estimated on a separate corpus, never on the evaluation data. We compare it against per-language mean removal (Libovický et al., 2020), language-subspace removal (Xie et al., 2022), and rotation toward a cross-lingual consen- sus (Liu et al., 2019). The corrections are separated by what they repair: mean removal gives the best ranking quality and rank agreement, while steer- ing gives the largest reduction in same-language crowding and remains competitive on recall, and holds up on non-parallel corpora, where documents are not translations of one another. Our contributions are: (1) Cross-lingual dis- agreement as a label-free diagnostic, read within languages, across the monolingual-to-mixed transi- tion, and among languages sharing an index. (2) An anatomy of cross-lingual retrieval failure: demo- tion rather than loss of correct answers, displace- ment by same-language non-relevant documents rather than hubs, two model-dependent crowding regimes, and a directional asymmetry between doc- uments and queries.
