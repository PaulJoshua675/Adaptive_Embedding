SIMPLEQA provides only coarse answer-type labels, so we first use rules to infer a fine-grained relation (e.g., year, count, city, person) from each question; we then sample a question from the same topic and relation. We ensure that the borrowed answer does not appear in the target question. The resulting false answers are therefore type-compatible (Appendix C). Screening. We keep only items whose manipu- lated answer is absent before any discussion, so that any later appearance can be attributed to the attack. A screening model (deepseek-v4-flash) answers each candidate’s probe question several times without context. We keep items that it often answers correctly and answers with the manipu- lated answer in at most 5% of samples, and we cap the number of items per relation (Appendix C). We also discard some POPQA items whose true answer appears in the question itself (e.g., “What is the capital of Artern?”), leaving 40, 53, and 50 items from 18, 14, and 15 relations. To check that private-answer labels do not hinge on the judge model, an independent judge from a model family used neither for the orig- inal judge nor for any agent (claude-sonnet-5.5) relabeled 200 private answers. Weighted to the analysis population, the two judges agree on 87.5% of answers (Cohen’s κ=0.82), and applying the rates at which the independent judge relabels each DeepSeek label to all private answers lowers final infection under three sybils from 60.5% to 57.8%. 4 Results 4.1 A single adversarial agent causes most of the damage Figure 2 reports final private infection (FPI). With- out an attacker, benign groups are stable: FPI av- erages 0.6% and never exceeds 3% in any of the 18 model×dataset cells, with 0.2% initial infec- tion. A single adversary raises average FPI to 44.8% (range 19–74%), and three attacker to av- erage 60.5% (range 27–81%). A single adversary thus produces 74% of the infection rate observed with three sybils. Because the manipulated answer is essentially absent at baseline (§3.4), the false an- swer is not what the models produce on their own; it becomes a common private response only after adversaries introduce it in the chat. The benign agents do not filter it out; as §4.5 shows, they help spread it. Does redundancy help? If peers cross-checked one another, adding benign agents should dilute a single attacker. We test this by pairing one at- tacker with one benign agent (N=2), keeping ev- erything else fixed (Appendix E). In the pair, the adversarial agent writes half of all messages; in the ten-agent group, a tenth.
