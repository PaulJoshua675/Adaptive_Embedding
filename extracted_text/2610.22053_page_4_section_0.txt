This ex- posed which attributes were favored by the model, on average. The results of this analysis are shown in Figures 1 and 2. • Relative Bias Between Sentences: Second, we measured the effects of differ- ent sentences on the relative levels of bias by asking how the distribution of names chosen for the sentence differed from the average. The results of this analysis are shown in Figure 3. • Mixed-effects Modeling: Third, we fit a mixed-effects model to the chance that the second name in the pair shown to the LLM was chosen. Since the LLM showed extreme bias for the first name, this was an effective way to measure how a chang- ing a demographic attribute affect that bias. We used the statsmodels pack- age for this analysis, shown in Table 2 (Seabold and Perktold, 2010). • Bias Correlation: Finally, we computed the correlations between biases within each model to assess whether models bi- ased in one attribute (e.g. gender) are bi- ased towards other attributes (e.g. race), which helps distinguish “one-factor” from multi-attribute biases. When ranking prompts by how their bias differs from the over- all, we find that the most biased prompts cluster around salient social and institu- tional contexts. These could perpetuate harmful stereotypes about the associated at- tributes.
