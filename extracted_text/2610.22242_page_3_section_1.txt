Deep Learning for Survival Analysis. The toolbox of survival analysis has been considerably extended in the last years to include deep architectures (Katzman et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2018; Kvamme et al., 2019) that can accommodate a wide variety of data and event types, including time series, competing events, images and text (Zhu et al., 2016; Thorsen-Meyer et al., 2022; Bleistein et al., 2024; Wiegrebe et al., 2024; Alberge et al., 2024; Jeanselme et al., 2024). Our work builds on this research by tailoring deep transformers and training procedures for tabular data, which is the most common form of survival data. Deep Learning for Tabular Data. Gradient-based decision tree models have long dominated benchmarks on tabular data (Gorishniy et al., 2021; Grinsztajn et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2024). Tabular foundation models have recently challenged their superiority and sparked a new line of research that focused on designing tailored architectures and training procedures (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026; Kim et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). We build on these ar- chitectural advances and adapt them to calibrated survival prediction. Once pre-trained, our method performs in-context inference without fine-tuning or weight updates. Tabular Foundation Models for Survival Analysis is a burgeoning field of research. Efforts follow three distinct strategies. (i) Reducing survival to a task that generic TFMs already solve. Kim et al. (2026) frame survival prediction as a series of binary classification problems over a time grid, so that any TFM that handles classification can be deployed for survival analysis. Vieyra (2026) instead plugs an off-the-shelf TFM regressor into a log-normal accelerated failure time model and impute censored times iteratively, while SurvFM (Lyu et al., 2026) converts censored follow-up into pseudo- observations of the restricted mean survival time (RMST), which are then regressed in-context by unmodified TFMs. These approaches inherit the strengths of generic TFMs, but their backbone has never seen censored data: they either depend on a time discretization, impose a parametric form on the event-time distribution, or only output a summary statistic (the RMST) rather than a full survival function. (ii) Adapting generic TFMs with a survival head. Pham et al. (2026b;a) train classical survival heads (Cox, DeepHit, multi-task logistic regression) on top of frozen or fine-tuned representations of TabPFN, TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025a), and TabICL, and systematically compare these adaptation interfaces on 74 datasets. They find that zero-shot reformulations work best on small datasets, whereas trained heads take over as the sample size grows. This line of work matches the common practice of fitting shallow survival models on top of deep foundation models, and gives up the pre-training + in-context learning recipe: the head must be fitted, and possibly tuned, on each new dataset. (iii) Pre-training on synthetic survival data. Closest to our work, Seletkov et al. (2026), B¨ohm et al. (2026) and Qi et al. (2026) pre-train a tabular foundation model on synthetic survival data, following previous work in the field of tabular foundation models (Hollmann et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2025; 2026). All three models predict on a discretized time grid (see Table 1), while SurvICL outputs a continuous quantile function of the event time. Finally, foundation models for time-to- event prediction have also been pre-trained on large real-world electronic health records (Steinberg et al., 2024); such models are tied to a specific data modality and coding system, whereas we target generic tabular data through a synthetic prior. 3 SURVIVAL ANALYSIS SETTINGS: CENSORED TARGETS, SMALL SAMPLES 3.1 CENSORED DATA AND SURVIVAL MODELS Survival Data.
