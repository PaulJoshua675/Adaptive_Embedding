C. Trajectory Collection Yield We compare trajectory collection from the full and escapability-filtered grasp datasets using 13,440 collection attempts per condition across all fifteen objects. Both con- ditions use the same frozen experts, set of eligible goals, sampling mixture, randomization, and quality filter (Ap- pendix VII-B). Each trajectory follows successive voxel-to- voxel goals until failure or a 120 s timeout. A trajectory is usable if, after removing the termination tail, it still passes the quality filter. Each condition splits its attempts between the two route samplers of Appendix VII-B. Combining both samplers, usable yield increases from approximately 29.0% to 32.5% (+3.5 percentage points), and usable frames per attempt from approximately 938 to 1,053 (+12%). The fraction of attempts failing within the first 10 s falls from approximately 26.7% to 22.9%. Accepted trajectories contain approximately 3,200 frames under both conditions, suggesting that filtering mainly increases the number of accepted trajectories. The improvement in collection yield is consistent but modest. We use the filtered grasp datasets for trajectory collection because they raise the usable data per collection attempt without notably reducing the coverage of the grasp datasets. APPENDIX VII WBD CONTROLLER DISTILLATION DETAILS Section III-C gives the WBD controller’s architecture and its guidance mechanism. Here we specify the network settings, training data, sampling recipe, and inference proce- dure. A. Architecture and Optimization We follow the diffusion-and-inverse-dynamics formulation and published state-encoder/U-Net configuration of [11], adapting the representation and conditioning to whole-body manipulation. Our U-Net uses one middle block, two linear layers per block, and a 256-dimensional timestep embedding. Each arm contributes four keypoints: one at the elbow and three on the hand, at the thumb, index finger, and middle finger. Forward kinematics expresses these points in the torso frame using the fixed closed-fist configuration. Each history entry contains 24 torso-frame arm-keypoint coordinates, 14 measured joint positions, 14 applied joint targets, and 14 joint tracking errors. Four entries at offsets (−15, −10, −5, 0), in 50 Hz control steps, and five static geometry features form the 269-dimensional diffusion input; no task-mode embedding is used. The model predicts 48 keypoint-displacement coordinates at future offsets (5, 10), with targets defined as future measured keypoint positions minus their current measured positions. The inverse model takes the current history entry, predicted motions, and geom- etry features (119 inputs), using width 1536 and four two- layer residual blocks to output 14 joint-target increments. We train both networks jointly for 130,000 AdamW up- dates with a global batch of 2,560 training samples and base learning rate 3×10−4, including a final 10,000-update cosine cooldown. The inverse model is supervised on an equal mixture of recorded expert motions and diffusion-generated motions, using the expert’s realized joint-target increment at the same state for both: the applied target after action scaling, tracking-error clamping, and joint limits, minus the current target. Diffusion training uses 100 timesteps with a cosine schedule (offset 0.008, β capped at 0.999). Training the simulation and real-world controllers uses approximately 22 H100 GPU-hours per model across eight H100 GPUs. B. Trajectory Collection Each frozen WBD-EX expert starts from its object’s escapability-filtered grasp dataset (Appendix VI) and follows successive position-and-grasp-mode goals without resetting between goals. Trajectories are recorded at 50 Hz until failure or a 120 s timeout. Random voxel-sampled routes sample eligible goal vox- els and compatible grasp modes, with initial left/right/both modes in a 1:1:2 ratio. Random region-graph routes sample initial and target grasps from voxels in connected body re- gions. The first transfer follows one of sixteen directed region pairs: front–shoulder, front–underarm, shoulder–underarm, and underarm–back, in both directions and on both sides; subsequent goals follow the voxel goal graph (Appendix II). Voxel-sampled routes with at least four completed goals also supply a longer-trajectory subset. Both samplers balance the 2–4, 4–6, and 6–8 cm goal-distance bands, while keeping the maximum distance used to build the goal graph at 8 cm. We keep the usable portions of failed trajectories, discard- ing the final 100 frames before failure termination. Clean timeouts are left untrimmed, and we require at least two completed goals and 50 frames. Stalled intervals are removed using 50-frame windows in which the goal is unchanged, all joint spans are within 0.02 rad, the object position bounding box is within 5 mm, and the maximum pairwise object rotation is within 0.1 rad. Each training sample is drawn from a contiguous 26-frame trajectory segment that supplies the required observation history and future supervision. This segment never crosses a removed interval or an episode boundary. C. Sampling and Augmentation Batches mix training samples from random voxel-sampled routes, region-graph routes, and the longer-trajectory subset. Each 320-sample per-GPU batch draws 125, 165, and 30 samples from these three sources and approximately equal numbers of samples from the 2–4, 4–6, and 6–8 cm goal- distance bands, with objects equally represented, while spa- tial and grasp-mode sampling promote coverage of body regions and single-arm grasp goals. Training samples are drawn from eligible timesteps throughout each trajectory; spatial sampling uses the object’s position at each sample’s current frame. We split trajectories and their derived samples by initial grasp using a fixed hash, assigning approximately 5% of grasp groups to validation.
