Backward Compatibility in Zero-Bit Neural Audio Watermarking evolve, with new embedder-detector pairs introduced over time to harden against new attacks, improve quality, or reduce cost, while previously distributed content stays in circulation and must remain verifiable. Existing methods provide no explicit mechanism for an updated detector to keep recognizing watermarks from earlier embedders while still rejecting unrelated content, leading to invalidated watermarks or elevated false positives after updates. Re-embedding old content is often infeasible, and maintaining many detector versions at inference is complex and itself raises the false-positive rate. We call the ordered sequence of legacy embedders 𝐸1, . . . , 𝐸𝑣−1, that a newly trained detector 𝐷𝑣 must continue to support, the legacy chain. A backward-compatible 𝐷𝑣must (i) detect watermarks from all embedders in its chain, (ii) reject unwatermarked audio, and (iii) reject watermarks from embedders outside the chain. Objective (iii) is not merely an accuracy concern; it is what makes a compromised version revocable. If an embedder 𝐸𝑖is leaked, an adversary can stamp arbitrary audio with its watermark. The response is to revoke 𝐸𝑖, dropping it from every future detector’s chain so that any detector issued after the leak rejects it outright. Without this, a single leak permanently invalidates the watermark, as every newer detector keeps accepting the forgeries. This motivates strict version separation rather than monotone forward acceptance. In this work, we conduct the first systematic study of backward compatibility in neural audio watermarking. The following contributions are made: • The backward-compatibility objective for zero-bit watermarking is formalized, and the legacy-chain abstraction is introduced together with its security implications. • A training framework is proposed that combines a performance-weighted sampling scheme across frozen legacy embedders with ADL applied to the new embedder. • The framework is evaluated across eight model versions that span architectural changes in both embedders (DenseNet, ViTMAE) and detectors (ResNet, AST), and the ADL is shown to substantially restore TNR against out-of-chain embedders with no measurable degradation in detection accuracy or audio quality. • The same mechanism is shown to enable revocation: a leaked embedder can be dropped from the legacy chain and reliably rejected by detectors trained without it. 2.
