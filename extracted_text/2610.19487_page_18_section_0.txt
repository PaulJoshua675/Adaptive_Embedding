For neuro-inspired design, the question is therefore ex- plicit: which contact groups, nonlinear features and state dependencies should each model unit retain? The same question applies when reducing circuit motifs for compo- sition. We have shown that coupled models of canonical circuit motifs (divisive competition and winner-take-all normalization) can generate joint computational oper- ations absent from the uncoupled motifs41. A reduc- tion adequate for an isolated motif must therefore be reassessed when that motif interacts with others, an- swering does the composition preserve the state dependen- cies through which the coupling acts? Our scale-indexed decomposition and computational results provide con- trolled comparisons that answer the question of what to retain, beyond merely matching cell counts or copying cell-level connectivity. E. How long is, then, the coast of a neuron? In analogy with Mandelbrot’s coastline paradox 1, the question is well posed only after specifying i) the ruler, ii) the target observable, iii) the class of perturbations, and iv) the domain of conditions. Our scale-dependent con- struction makes mechanistic non-identifiability explicit and shows why the amount of information lost at a given scale in a biological regime must be determined empiri- cally. One should hold the output fixed and then ask how much extra predictive information finer-grained variables contribute. That comparison is a direct test of observa- tional sufficiency (Eq. (43)). Doing so isolates the con- tribution of higher-order interactions and finer variables from the residual that any fitted model family leaves un- explained. One can then apply perturbations (such as branch-targeted inhibition, shifts in spatial input cluster- ing, or changes to the temporal input regime) to probe whether an observational description that was adequate continues to be so under new conditions. The continued success across such tests supports the validity of reduc- tion and corase-graining within the tested domain. Such empirical support, however, does not uniquely identify the microscopic implementation. A coarse variable can be simple to record, easy to fit, and straightforward to simulate. It may even ac- count for most of the in-distribution output entropy. Yet that convenience can be misleading. The same somatic or point description that suffices to forecast spikes in a fixed population regime may fail to capture phenomena that depend on subcellular detail. Branch voltages, for example, may be essential to explain coincidence detec- tion or distal–proximal coupling. Likewise, contact- or spine-resolved variables are often required for questions about plasticity. Conversely, resolving more microscopic detail can in- crease estimation error without improving the prediction of interest23. Interaction order, directness, sparsity, and capacity are joint properties of the neuron and the cho- sen scale of description. Integrating out hidden degrees of freedom renormalizes apparent interaction strengths and can change which orders of interaction appear dominant. The appropriate scale is therefore question-dependent. Neither tractability nor goodness of fit alone establishes that a coarse-graining preserves the quantities an infer- ence about computation requires. The point-neuron is then a ruler. It is a useful, predictive abstraction. How- ever, it becomes misleading when its readings are re- ported as properties of the coast.
