Unlike optical sensors, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellites image the land surface using microwave backscatter rather than reflected sunlight, enabling all-weather, day-and-night acqui- sition well suited to land-cover mapping, flood assessment, and environmental change monitor- ing, particularly in regions where persistent cloud cover limits optical observation (Moreira et al., 2013). Since its 2014 launch, ESA’s Sentinel-1 mission has revisited most of the globe every 6– 12 days, accumulating a large and continually growing archive of freely available Sentinel-1 Ground Range Detected (S1GRD) imagery (European Space Agency, 2012). This unlabeled archive is abundant, but SAR imagery is difficult for non-experts to interpret visually, making manual annotation of large SAR scenes highly labor intensive. Publicly available labeled SAR benchmarks have therefore stayed small relative to computer vision norms, which in turn limits how well supervised deep learning models can perform on SAR downstream tasks (Wang et al., 2022). Self-supervised learning (SSL) offers one route to transferable representations without rely- ing on manually labeled data, and masked image modeling (MIM) (He et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022) is a natural fit for S1GRD pretraining specifically: it requires no S1GRD-appropriate pho- tometric augmentation scheme of the kind contrastive methods depend on (Wang et al., 2022), and it naturally yields the dense, patch-level representations that earth observation’s dense pre- diction tasks need. Existing MIM implementations, however, are almost all built on Vision Transformer (ViT) (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021) encoders, which typically demand large pretraining corpora and heavy compute to perform well; Copernicus-FM (Wang et al., 2025b), for instance, needed 512 GPU hours on 4×A100 GPUs and multi-modal inputs spanning four Sentinel mis- sions (Sentinel-1, 2, 3, 5P) to reach competitive results. For many research groups and applied teams working with SAR data, matching the com- pute scale needed to pretrain these large foundation models is itself out of reach. We therefore restrict this study’s primary comparisons to end-to-end finetuned convolutional neural network (CNN) encoders rather than reporting frozen ViT foundation model backbones as directly fine- tuned baselines. This is not solely a matter of compute: Copernicus-Bench’s own published learning-rate search for these models (10−4, 10−3, 10−2) was validated only for frozen-backbone and decoder-head finetuning, not for full end-to-end finetuning, so no full-finetuning result for these architectures could be defended as using a properly validated learning rate. Reporting one without a fair hyperparameter search would conflate architectural differences with an unvali- dated finetuning setup rather than isolate them. A rigorous evaluation of full fine-tuning for these foundation models, encompassing an architecture-specific learning-rate search, appropriate reg- ularization schedules, and comparable per-model tuning budgets, would constitute a dedicated study in its own right and lies outside this paper’s scope. CNN architectures, in contrast, bring stronger local inductive biases, remain data efficient under modest compute budgets, and can be finetuned end-to-end even on the small downstream datasets typical of SAR applications. SparK (Tian et al., 2023) was developed and validated on natural-image benchmarks such as ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009), and porting its default UNet- 768 (Ronneberger et al., 2015) decoder directly to S1GRD data at the prescribed learning rate proves numerically unstable: FP16 mixed precision training produces NaN losses, forcing either a redesigned decoder or a different learning rate.
