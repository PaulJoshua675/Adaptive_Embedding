Recent advances in generative AI have substantially increased the realism and accessibility of synthetic audio content. State-of-the-art audio generation techniques are capable of producing high- quality speech and audio at scale [1]. As a result, concerns surrounding large-scale misinformation, fraud and reputational damage have intensified, alongside a broader erosion of trust in digital media. Audio watermarking, a technique that embeds imperceptible signals at generation time and later recovers them to verify provenance, has emerged as a practical mechanism for attribution and misuse prevention. It is also becoming a legal requirement: the European Union’s AI Act (Article 50) mandates that providers of generative AI mark and detect artificially generated content, and its accompanying Code of Practice further requires that a replacement detector be backward compatible, detecting previously marked content with no loss of capability [2]. A line of recent deep-learning approaches jointly trains embedders and detectors, which we survey in Section 2. These systems all share an implicit assumption: an embedder and its detector are trained as a single matched pair and deployed together.
