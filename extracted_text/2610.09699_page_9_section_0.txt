This approach requires evaluating candidate solutions sequentially, rather than in parallel, but can reduce the total number of function evaluations per generation by avoiding unnecessary evaluations once progress is detected. 5 Adaptation In this subsection, the components that perform adaptation are discussed in more detail. Specifically, step-size adaptation (SSA) and covariance matrix adaptation (CMA). The latter comprises two modules, active update and matrix adaptation, and is somewhat of a misnomer, since the latter includes modules that do not require an explicit covariance matrix. 5.1 Step-Size Adaptation Supplementary to the default Cumulative Step size Adaptation (CSA), which is shown in lines 13–14 of Algorithm 1, ModCMA includes eight alternative mechanisms for performing SSA. These methods can offer advantages over CSA, which struggles with large population sizes λ and ineffective variable coordinates (Beyer and Arnold, 2003). In the latter case, or when f(x) is noisy, CSA may lead to too-small step-sizes and premature convergence. The following alternatives are implemented:2 1. Two Point step size Adaption (TPA) (Hansen, 2008), which resembles self-adaptation on a population level, was implemented as an alternative to CSA. The method is less sensitive to algorithm parameters and the properties of f, and does not rely on a predefined optimality criterion, such as the 1/5-th success rule (Rechenberg, 1973). It has the disadvantage of requiring two additional function evaluations, and the step-size adaptation is based solely on these two evaluations, thereby disregarding information from the population. 2. Success Rule (SR) (Igel et al., 2006): SR step-size adaptation uses the success ratio, which is an exponential smoothed variable, computed via: s = (1 −cs)s +  cs1f(x(g+1) 1:λ )<f(x(g) 1:λ)  . Here, f(x(g) 1:λ) denotes the fitness of the best individual in generation g. In other words, when the best individual in generation g + 1 improves over the best individual in generation g, the value s increases; it decays with a rate of 1 −cs. Based on the classic 1/5-th success rule, σ is adapted to match a target success rate of 2/11 (Igel et al., 2006). 3. Median success rule (MSR) (Ait Elhara et al., 2013): Similar to SR-based step-size adaptation, the MSR mechanism uses a notion of success in order to adapt σ. Specifically, it checks whether the median solution candidate, based on f(x), is improving in successive generations. This is also exponentially smoothed, with a target success rate of 1/2. That is, when s is larger than 1/2, σ is increased, and decreased otherwise. 4. Population success rule (PSR) (Loshchilov, 2014): While both MSR and SR rely on a single value to estimate success over generations, PSR incorporates the function values of the entire population to adapt σ.
