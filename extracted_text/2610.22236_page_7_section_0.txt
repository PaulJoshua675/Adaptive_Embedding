The reason is a trade-off that each specialist carries and the fused model does not. Each specialist is far ahead on its own domain and far behind on the other; TF-OPD is close to the specialist of the domain on every benchmark (Figure 1b), and in the plane of mathematics against code it sits beside the corner set by the two specialists’ own-domain scores (Figure 1a). It equals the mathematics specialist on AIME24 and is slightly ahead of it on OlympiadBench, is nearly level with the specialist of the domain on MATH-500 and on both code benchmarks, and keeps most of what reinforcement learning added on every benchmark. It is the only model that is never far from the best score, and its lead in mean accuracy does not come from the benchmark count: weighting the two domains equally widens it (Figure 1a). None of this required training. The procedure is one generation pass and one teacher-forced pass per specialist, followed by a linear solve per layer. It keeps no optimizer state, has no learning rate to tune, and finishes in a few GPU-hours per specialist (Table 4 in Appendix B). Training-based on-policy distillation spends the same kind of forward computation at every update and adds a backward pass each time. For specialists that share a base model, the closed form reaches a consolidated model at roughly the cost of collecting such data once. Its advantage over weight arithmetic is on hard problems: task arithmetic costs even less, but falls short on AIME25, where the closed form with clean rollouts matches the specialist in the same format (Section 4.5). On AIME25 the fused model is within a point of the mathematics specialist once both are scored in float16 (Table 5); Table 2 scores the released checkpoints in their native format. The three competition benchmarks have thirty problems each, so differences of a few points are within what they resolve. 4.3 Case study The fused model is not only a compromise between its sources: on some problems it succeeds where every sample of that domain’s specialist fails.
