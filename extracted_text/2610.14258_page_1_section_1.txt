Neural networks trained on a sequence of tasks tend to overwrite what they learned before, a failure known as catastrophic forgetting [13, 29]. Regularization-based methods address it by anchoring each parameter near its value after earlier tasks, with a strength proportional to an estimate of that parameter’s importance. Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) uses the diagonal of the Fisher information [20], synaptic intelligence (SI) accumulates each parameter’s contribution to the decrease of the loss along the training path [43], and memory aware synapses (MAS) measure how strongly the network output depends on each parameter [3]. In all three, the importance vector is the method’s entire account of what should be preserved. These vectors are estimates, and noisy ones. EWC is usually computed from a modest number of single-example gradients, SI from correlated steps of one optimization trajectory, and MAS from output gradients on a sample of inputs. How the Fisher information is computed changes the performance of EWC substantially [40], and the empirical Fisher differs systematically from the true one [22, 28]. Yet the methods treat the estimates as exact. Statistics has a standard response to estimating many quantities at once. When three or more normal means are estimated together, shrinking the individual estimates by a suitable amount toward a common value lowers the expected total squared error [16, 39], and empirical Bayes methods choose the amount of shrinkage from the data [11].
