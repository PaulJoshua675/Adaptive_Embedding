2.2 Action selection and basal ganglia in robots A robot with multiple behaviors needs a mechanism to decide which one to execute at any given time. Layered architectures solved this problem with fixed priorities, in which higher layers suppress lower ones [21]. Redgrave et al. [9] proposed that the basal ganglia fulfill this function in vertebrates, acting as a central selector between systems competing for motor control. Gurney et al. [22] formalized this idea in a model where each action has a salience and selection occurs through disinhibition, with a selection pathway, a control pathway, and dopaminergic modulation. This model was incorporated into a Khepera robot, where it produced coherent behavioral sequences under a wide range of sensory and motivational conditions [10]. Subsequent work with the same architecture studied the effect of reducing or increasing simulated dopamine on the robot's behavior [11]. EASA is an open reimplementation of this model that operates under realistic contact physics [12]. These studies share a fundamental idea: that robot and vertebrate control architectures organize behavior in layers with a common selection mechanism [13]. In all these cases, the basal ganglia select among hand-designed behaviors. Less attention has been given to what happens when one of the channels is a network whose behavior is acquired through evolution, and even less when the task requires coordination with another agent. This is the second question we address. 2.3 Simulation and design of the fitness function Evolutionary robotics relies on simulation because evaluating thousands of controllers on physical robots is impractical. The risk is that evolution will exploit simulator flaws that do not exist in the real world. Jakobi et al. [7] showed that carefully validated simulations with adequate noise treatment reduce this gap. Simulators such as Webots offer detailed models of commercial robots [15], including the e-puck, a robot widely used in collaborative and educational robotics [14]. In this work, we reconstructed the e-puck from its Webots model within PyBullet [16] and verified each component before using it. The design of the fitness function is the other critical decision.
