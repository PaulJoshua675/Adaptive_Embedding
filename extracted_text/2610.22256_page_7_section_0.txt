Table 3 reports the ReLU2 results in both BF16 and FP8; Appendix E.3 gives the corresponding ReLU results. At ρ = 0.9, BitSparse is the most memory-efficient lossless method, dropping VRAM usage by up to 48% relative to dense storage. Sign-bit packing reduces memory further, at a small cost in time. For BF16, checkpointing uses the least memory at ρ = 0.5, but BitSparse overtakes it at ρ = 0.9. For FP8, BitSparse-sb uses the least memory at both sparsity levels. Checkpointing carries a substantially larger runtime penalty. The other lossless compression methods either have lower compression than BitSparse or run substantially slower. To further investigate the memory–runtime trade-off between BitSparse and checkpointing, we vary the number of compressed layers in our model from 0 to 8. Figure 2 shows the frontier for ReLU2 at ρ = 0.9. Appendix E.7 gives the other three configurations. BitSparse dominates the Pareto-frontier, particularly at higher sparsity levels. Also visible is the drop in VRAM on the last data point caused by lower transient memory usage from our fused pointwise gradient kernel. Furthermore, at high sparsity, our ReLU2 kernel is faster than the standard implementation. We also vary the batch size from 32 to 32,000 (Table 4). As the batch grows, the activations that BitSparse compresses account for more memory so memory savings increase from 6% at 32 rows to 55% at 32,000. Runtime stays within 1% of dense storage from 512 rows upward. BitSparse therefore occupies a different point on the memory–compute frontier from checkpointing: its advantage grows with naturally occurring activation sparsity, while avoiding recomputation. 7
