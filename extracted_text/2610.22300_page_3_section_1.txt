Most related to our distinction between inert vs active facts is work on inference from facts that we will call “entailment.” A fact f is entailed by a set of other facts F if from accepting the truth of F, it necessarily follows that the truth of f has to be accepted. For example, {“John lives in Tokyo”, “Tokyo is in Japan”, “People in Japan speak Japanese”} entails “Where John lives, people speak Japanese” (Feng et al., 2025). This type of entailment includes transitive inference, inverse relations and kinship reasoning and it has been widely studied (e.g., Berglund et al., 2024; Mehrafarin et al., 2024; Efeoglu & Paschke, 2026; Fesser et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Lampinen et al., 2025). There is mixed evidence on how well LMs perform on entailment (Talmor et al., 2020; Clark et al., 2021). Statistical generalization (see §1) differs from entailment in that the underlying statistical rule is not 100% accurate. Its accuracy is a matter of degree: each of the facts given about Mary is additional evidence and decreases the probability of Mary not being a native French speaker. Paraphrases, which can be viewed as linguistic generalization, also have been widely studied (e.g., Elazar et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2024; Behnia et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Similarly, there is work on the acquisition of linguistic constructions if no or little evidence is present in the pretraining corpus (e.g., Misra & Mahowald, 2024; Patil et al., 2024). Paraphrases and the acquisition of linguistic constructions concern variation in how the same underlying fact is expressed linguistically and are thus fundamentally different from statistical generalization of facts. Statistical generalization of facts has received little attention. It is investigated by Lu et al. (2024), but their focus is scaling laws. Wu et al. (2025) make an argument similar to ours (that generalization over facts is important), but they generalize over relation tokens (e.g., from English “is” to German “ist”), which is more a form of linguistic generalization than true fact generalization in our sense. In a survey paper, Chang & Bergen (2024) report that early language models were not able to reliably infer “robins can fly” from “a robin is a bird,” a negative finding. Linear relational embeddings (LREs) would seem to be an obvious vehicle for investigating statis- tical generalization of facts. Indeed, in their original work, Paccanaro & Hinton (2000) motivate LRE as a mechanism for generalizing to unseen relational facts. Recent work in language models investigates LREs as causal mechanisms, asks to what extent frequency predicts relational linearity, develops better probing mechanisms and proposes efficient estimators of LREs (Hernandez et al., 2024; Merullo et al., 2025; Marconato et al., 2025; Valer et al., 2026); but statistical generalization of facts is not considered. In addition to the LRE literature, there has been much work on factual recall and knowledge editing (e.g., Petroni et al., 2019; Dai et al., 2022; Kandpal et al., 2023; Geva et al., 2021; 2023; Nanda et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2022; Cohen et al., 2024). In contrast to our research, this work has focused on under which circumstances facts stated in pretraining data are learned (in some cases including the impact of linguistic paraphrases) or can be edited, not on how those facts, once learned or edited, allow the model to learn new things. Another related area is hallucinations (Rawte et al., 2023; Kalai & Vempala, 2024). By definition, hallucinations are incorrect generations and thus undesirable.
