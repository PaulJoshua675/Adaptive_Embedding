That remainder is ω, and the coefficient that accompanies it is σ, up to normalization. The promise of at most 35 errors ensures deg σ ≤35 and determines this pair uniquely. The baseline next locates the errors by evaluating σ at every possible position. Associate position j with the field point βj = γ−j (since γ is primitive, these points enumerate all 4,095 nonzero field elements). The scan visits them one by one and tests whether σ(βj) = 0. It need not evaluate σ independently each time: advancing to βj+1 = γ−1βj multiplies its degree-r term by the fixed constant γ−r. This efficient scan is known as a Chien search [43]. Finding a root reveals an error’s position but not its value. At βj, the evaluator ω(βj) contains the error value multiplied by the contributions of the other locator factors, while the formal derivative σ′(βj)—the usual coefficientwise polynomial derivative over the field—contains those same factors without the error value. Their ratio therefore isolates ej, up to a fixed factor κj that depends only on the position: ej = κj ω(βj) σ′(βj). (30) This recovery step is Forney’s formula [44]. The circuit immediately uses that value and the matching twelve-bit block cj to apply the phase (24). It then reverses the computation to restore the syndrome and clear its ancillas. The EEA is the dominant cost of this baseline because its classical control flow depends on the syndromes: different inputs require different shifts, swaps, and numbers of iterations. A quantum circuit must instead apply one fixed sequence of gates across the syndrome superposition. The baseline therefore spreads the Euclidean solve over 222 synchronized cycles, controlling each operation so that it acts only when needed. Our implementation of this baseline, based on the authors’ released code [35], uses 5.1 million Toffoli gates and 1,938 qubits at peak, with the forward Euclidean solve and its reversal accounting for 3.8 million of the Toffolis [45]. The discovered circuit changes both stages of this baseline. First, it replaces the Euclidean solve with the arithmetically cheaper Berlekamp–Massey (BM) recurrence and arranges the recurrence so that reversibility does not require retaining much history state. Second, although the decoded error word contains 49,140 bits, DQI extracts only the single bit ⟨c, e⟩2 from it. The circuit therefore computes this phase bit directly from the locator and syndromes rather than materializing the full decoded error, and it precomputes one reciprocal polynomial so that no field division is performed during the scan. An alternative to solving the key equation by EEA is the standard BM algorithm, which recovers the locator from a linear recurrence among the syndromes. We first review how BM reads Equation (29) one coefficient at a time. Write σ(z) = PL k=0 σkzk, where L = deg σ. The coefficient of zr−1 in σ(z)S(z) is the convolution PL k=0 σkSr−k. Because deg ω < L, the key equation requires every such coefficient from degree L through degree 69 to vanish: 0 = [zr−1] σ(z)S(z) = L X k=0 σkSr−k, r = L + 1, . . . , 70. (31) Since we can set σ0 = 1 without loss of generality, and subtraction is the same as addition in F212, every syndrome after the first L satisfies Sr = σ1Sr−1 + · · · + σLSr−L. (32) Thus the locator coefficients give a rule for predicting each syndrome from the preceding L syndromes. We call L the order of this recurrence. BM finds this recurrence by maintaining a candidate locator σ and refining it as the syndromes are incorporated [46]. At the start of round r, the candidate defines a lowest-order recurrence for the already processed syndromes S1, . . . , Sr−1. The round tests whether that recurrence also fits Sr. The discrepancy ∆= PL k=0 σkSr−k is the sum in Equation (31) for round r, computed with the current candidate. It measures the amount by which the recurrence fails: ∆= 0 means that the current candidate already fits Sr, while ∆̸ = 0 requires the candidate to be refined. When ∆̸ = 0, BM needs a correction that fixes the equation for Sr without invalidating any of the earlier equations. For this purpose, it retains an earlier candidate τ as a correction template, together with the nonzero discrepancy δ that candidate produced. At the start of each subsequent round, BM multiplies τ by z, shifting every coefficient one degree higher as the recurrence advances to the next syndrome. Thus, if the template was saved k rounds earlier, it has acquired a factor zk and is aligned with the current syndrome equation while leaving the earlier equations unchanged. BM then scales this aligned template by α = ∆/δ and applies σ ←σ −ατ: at Sr, the correction contributes exactly αδ = ∆and so cancels the discrepancy. On a round with a nonzero discrepancy, BM must also decide whether the current candidate takes over as the template. A candidate of order L that fits n syndromes has predicted n −L of them.
