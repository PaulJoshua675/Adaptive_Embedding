Joint audio-visual embeddings have evolved from contrastive methods [Arandjelovi´c and Zisserman, 2017, Owens and Efros, 2018] to masked autoencoders [Gong et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2023]. PE-AV [Vyas et al., 2025] scales to 100M pairs, achieving state-of-the-art results on audio-visual retrieval. ImageBind [Girdhar et al., 2023] aligns six modalities through image-paired data. Prior work evaluates these models on retrieval and classification, but, to our knowledge, none probes what frozen audio-visual embeddings capture about physical properties, or whether this knowledge can transfer to vision alone. Recent benchmarks reveal systematic failures in physical reasoning. Beyond PhysBench [Chow et al., 2025], Physion [Bear et al., 2021] shows that models fall short of humans at predicting physical outcomes, Physion++ [Tung et al., 2023] extends this to outcomes that depend on latent properties, and CLEVRER [Yi et al., 2020] and IntPhys [Riochet et al., 2021] probe causal and intuitive physics understanding. These benchmarks focus on dynamics prediction, and we complement them by probing continuous material properties. Audio and vision have long supervised one another: ambient sound can train visual representa- tions [Owens et al., 2016b], and visual networks can train sound representations [Aytar et al., 2016]. Knowledge distillation transfers representations across architectures [Hinton et al., 2015] or modal- ities: Gupta et al. [2016] transferred supervision from labeled RGB images to depth and optical- flow representations. Our work differs in targeting specific physically relevant structure rather than general representations, and takes a step toward making acoustically grounded properties partially accessible to vision alone. 3 The Quality Factor: A Continuous Physical Property 3.1 Why Q? The quality factor Q = πf0/β combines resonant frequency (f0) and damping rate (β) into one dimensionless scalar. The damping rate β sets how quickly a ringing object falls silent in absolute time, whereas Q counts how many oscillation cycles it completes before decaying, so a high-pitched glass can fall silent sooner than a lower-pitched metal bowl while having the higher Q. We target this compound quantity for three reasons. Being dimensionless, Q is comparable across objects regardless of size or pitch, so a wine glass and a steel beam can share a Q despite frequencies orders of magnitude apart. It also varies continuously within material categories: a thin metal sheet and a solid metal block are both “metal” yet differ substantially in Q, so predicting it demands more than category recognition. Finally, it has direct physical consequences, determining whether an object rings, bounces, or absorbs energy on impact. These properties make Q a stricter test of physical grounding than frequency, which is largely predictable from visible geometry, or the damping rate alone, which is confounded with object size. 3.2 Extraction Pipeline We extract Q from audio recordings in the Greatest Hits dataset [Owens et al., 2016a], which con- tains 977 videos of objects being hit with a drumstick. Our copy carries annotations for 975, totaling 28,444 individual impacts across 17 material categories. Figure 1 illustrates both the quantity and the method. For each annotated impact, we take the mag- nitude of the analytic signal (the Hilbert envelope) to obtain an amplitude envelope A(t) and fit the decay model A(t) = A0e−βt + C by nonlinear least squares, with β bounded to [0.1, 1000] s−1 and the additive constant C absorbing background noise. The resonant frequency f0 is the dominant peak of the power spectrum above 50 Hz, a floor that avoids low-frequency room noise. Retaining only fits with R2 ≥0.8 leaves 17,958 usable impacts, 63% of the total. Rejections concentrate on granular and scattering surfaces, where the response has no single resonant mode to fit: water retains 24% of its annotated impacts, gravel 33%, and dirt 36%, against 83% for cloth and 91% for glass.
