B RAIN computer interface (BCI) technology establishes a direct communication and control pathway between the brain and external devices by decoding neural activ- ity [1]. It has broad potential in diverse fields such as neurorehabilitation, entertainment [2], and human-machine interaction [3]. Among non-invasive acquisition modalities, electroencephalography (EEG) is widely adopted due to its high temporal resolution and relatively low cost [4]. In motor imagery (MI) BCIs, users voluntarily imag- ine limb movements to elicit event-related desynchroniza- tion/synchronization (ERD/ERS) patterns [5], [6], thereby enabling device control without external stimuli. Recent years have witnessed significant advances in MI- EEG signal decoding techniques, most of which analyze a predefined post-cue segment (typically 0–4 s) for feature ex- traction and classification [7]. Representative approaches range from Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) [8], which learns spatial filters by maximizing inter-class variance, to diverse deep learning methods. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) like EEGNet [9] and M-ShallowConvNet [10] efficiently cap- ture spatio-temporal patterns with depthwise separable convo- lutions or shallow designs. FBCNet [11] performs frequency- band fusion via multi-view convolutions, while ATCNet [12] integrates attention mechanisms with temporal convolutional networks. More recently, Transformer-based models have been explored in EEGConformer [13] and TSLANet [14]. And CSPNet [15] embeds CSP principles within a neural net- work framework. Riemannian geometry-based methods such as manifold attention network (mAtt) [16] characterize EEG patterns on the Riemannian manifold of symmetric positive- definite (SPD) matrices. Although these methods perform well in offline evaluations, their performance in online scenarios are constrained by using a single, shared temporal segment to represent MI activity across trials. For instance, online systems often relies on a sliding window of fixed length (around 1 s) moving along the continuous EEG signal. Some windows may cover only a fraction of an MI event, some may contain excessive pre-event baseline activity, and some may be contaminated by burst artifacts, the majority of which are misaligned with actual neural events. Meanwhile, fixed-length windows restrict the timeliness of the system response.
