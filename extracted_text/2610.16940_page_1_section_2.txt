Nearest neighbor algorithms, and data structures to accelerate/improve them, exist in many forms. There are algorithms for pruning the search space like the KD-tree [1], sketches for calculating approximations of distance functions faster [2], and more. This line of research has been highly impactful in real-world systems, from information retrieval to clustering/visualization and even in Large Language Models via retrieval-augmented generation and vector databases. While approxi- mate methods have dramatically gained popularity in recent years, exact metric-space k-NN remains necessary when the distance itself is the object of interest or approximate recall is unacceptable— for example, in cases like anomaly detection over raw features, protein-structure comparison under graph kernels, or retrieval under Wasserstein distance. However, when we survey papers (see Ap- pendix K) in this broad area, we find that few consider basic details, such as distinguishing between a hold-out set’s query performance and the original training data (26%).
