Figure 1: Running time in seconds vs. search space dimensionality d. The left figure shows the total time to perform 1 000 generations of the algorithm, using the default population size of 4 + ⌊3 log d⌋. The median time over 15 independent runs is shown. The right figure shows the average time per matrix update. When considering the time per update, the standard CMA method is the least efficient among the options. The scaling of the time per update is similar to the Natural Gradient method; the constant for performing a full spectral decomposition of C seems higher than that for computing a matrix exponential. If we compare this with the CMSA method, which uses a Cholesky factorization rather than an eigen decomposition, we already observe a significant speedup. In fact, CMSA with a Cholesky decomposition is only marginally slower than the Cholesky CMA-ES, which doesn’t perform a decomposition. The Matrix adaptation method is slightly faster than the Cholesky CMA-ES. The two fastest methods, None and Separable, are indistinguishable from each other in Figure 1. If we compare these results against the baseline CMA-ES implementation Pycma, we can observe that all methods in ModCMA are significantly faster for d ≤100. However, for larger d, once the eigenvalue decomposition starts to dominate the computation, the runtime becomes similar to that of the Covariance matrix adaptation method implemented in ModCMA. In fact, for d > 500, the implementation in Pycma is notably faster, both overall and per update. This might because the eigensolver of NumPy is more optimized for large matrices than then one included in Eigen.5 Nevertheless, faster implementations, such as the Cholesky CMA, significantly outperform Pycma in runtime across all tested values of d. 8.2 Benchmarking In this section, benchmark data is collected for the benchmark functions taken from the BBOB suite, implemented by IOHexperimenter (de Nobel et al., 2024d).
