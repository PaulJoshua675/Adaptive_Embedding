A weak agent goes through the task until its uncertainty scores trigger initial or step-level escalation; otherwise, the weak agent continues. After escalation is triggered, the strong agent retains control while the weak agent changes its role from an actor to a shadow observer for continued monitoring. The feedback path from weak observer to strong actor enables uncertainty-triggered reflection and intervention. In short, LEASH unleashes the strong agent only when the weak agent’s latent uncertainty signals it is needed, and keeps it on a leash afterward. We evaluate LEASH on OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024) with four open-weight weak agents and Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic, 2026a) as the strong agent. At matched escalation rates, LEASH outperforms ran- dom escalation by 6.55–11.94 points. We further measure how much of the performance gap between the weak agent alone and always-on Opus is recovered by escalation, following the performance-gap metric of (Burns et al., 2023). LEASH recovers 75–102% of this gap, against 37–69% for random escalation, while costing 69–81% as much as always-on Opus. With Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (Qwen Team, 2026a), LEASH matches always-on Opus (73.7% vs. 72.9% success) at 19% lower cost, whereas random escalation at equal cost scores about 9 points lower (Figure 1). Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We formalize weak-to-strong escalation as a budget-constrained sequential stopping problem and show that one latent uncertainty signal can drive both escalation and oversight. • We propose a training-free, scalable uncertainty scoring method that measures how much closer a hidden state lies to the weak agent’s own past failures than to its past successes, and provide theoretical justification for using this score for escalation. • We extend this uncertainty signal to weak-to-strong oversight, in which the white-box weak agent monitors the black-box strong agent through its own hidden states. • On OSWorld with four open-weight backbones, we show that LEASH outperforms random escalation at matched escalation rates and recovers 75–102% of the escalation gap at 69–81% of the cost of using always strong agent.
