2.1 Synthetic Low-Light Image Adaptation The development of LLIE models has been historically hampered by the limited dataset availability and the quality of light-deficient representations. Early efforts such as the SID dataset [31] focused on capturing short-exposure raw images along with long-exposure counterparts. This was a good representation of how a LLIE dataset should look like, however it was limited in terms of the format it used: RAW. The consumer and surveillance hardware operate much more easily using conven- tional formats such as PNG or JPG, which also provide more efficient computational processing. Moreover, the conversion of RAW images into other encodings fails to replicate the natural noise and illumination patterns captured in the low-light conditions, the quality of the resulting data hin- dering the supervised learning process of LLIE models. More recently, the problem with data limitation was fixed with the appearance of paired real-world datasets such as LOL [32], LOL-v2 real [33], SDSD-in/out [34], and LOL-Blur [35]. Another field of concern, however, lies in the reliance of synthetic low-light adaptation methods. Widely-used current approaches, such as the work of Yang et al. through their LOL-v2 synthetic dataset [33] have provided a very solid foundation for how synthetic low-light images should be constructed: follow- ing a real-world dataset illumination distribution extracted from multiple datasets and delivering the result into an easy-to-use encoding format. 2.2 Low-Light Image Enhancement Classical Methods. LLIE has been firstly addressed using plain-enhancement [36] which focused mainly on histogram equalisation [37] and gamma correction [38, 39]. These methods amplify the brightness and contrast of underexposed images through pixel intensity transformations. Because of the complex and uneven way illumination interacts with different areas from the image, these models struggled to model the light projection process, resulting in perceptually inconsistent outputs containing over-enhanced regions and amplified noise. The later introduced Retinex theory [40] aims to decompose the image in two components: re- flectance, which represents the intrinsic scene information and illuminance, focusing on lighting variations. To better account for the illumination effects on the local and global structures, LLIE- methods have employed the theory as the underlying assumption behind the image architecture [41, 42, 43].
