Figure 1 previews the boundary errors of small predictors trained on each encoding; Figure 2 shows why the first byte can hide or expose the required distinction. The worked example in Appendix C illustrates the cluster fragmentation that motivates our targets. Our motivating application is byteification, the conversion of a pretrained subword language model into a model that reads and emits bytes. Bolmo surrounds the parent transformer with local modules and trains a boundary predictor on the parent’s subword segmentation; the global transformer then operates on the resulting patches (Minixhofer et al., 2025; 2026). Byteification thus removes the tokenizer from the model’s interface but transfers its segmentation criterion along with the weights: the patches are still learned from the parent’s cuts (Section 5.2). Bolmo raises the question of what models inherit from the input encoding and suggests alternatives to UTF-8 (Minixhofer et al., 2025, Section 8); Haltiuk (2026) reports nearly inherited Bolmo boundaries and proposes separating boundary placement from language modeling. We focus on how to construct cluster-aligned targets and expose the distinctions needed to predict them with limited lookahead. The targets either include every grapheme boundary or retain only the parent-tokenizer cuts that already coincide with one. We use grapheme clusters be- cause their boundaries are representable in every encoding, position-only cluster targets are invariant to span-preserving edits, and cluster continuations are where encodings differ in what the first symbol reveals (Section 3); the choice does not presuppose that clusters are better modeling units. We train small causal boundary predictors on matched FineWeb-2 text (Penedo et al., 2025) under UTF-8, UBE, and UBE-BE, a big-endian variant with UBE’s routing and stream length. UBE differs from UTF-8 in both stream length and byte order; UBE-BE keeps UBE’s length but reverses the bytes within each UTF-16 code unit, allowing a byte-order comparison at fixed stream length. For the every-cluster target on the 17-language FLORES-200 panel (NLLB Team et al., 2022), one-symbol lookahead yields 8.76% balanced error at scalar ends with UTF-8 and 0.06% with UBE. UBE-BE gives 8.62%, implicating byte order be- yond stream shortening alone. False cuts inside scalars are measured separately; a rule-based mask can remove them without changing any decision at scalar ends (Section 4). On constructed pairs with identical left context whose next scalars lie on the same 256-codepoint page but require oppo- site decisions, the trained UBE predictors answer all 17,204 pairs correctly at one-symbol lookahead in each seed; UTF- 8 and UBE-BE cannot, because both continuations begin with the same symbol. Korean and Chinese provide a complementary test: every evaluated cluster is a single scalar, so the retained-parent task selects among inter-character boundaries. In this sep- arate extension, UBE reduces total retained-parent errors relative to UTF-8 by 72.91% and 35.29%, respectively, at one-symbol lookahead. These totals include false cuts in- side scalars, and every seed favors UBE over both UTF- 8 and UBE-BE in each language. Grapheme boundaries here coincide with scalar ends, which every encoding ex- poses, so the gain concerns subword selection (Section 4.1). In eight more scripts, UBE reduces retained-parent errors by 67.32–99.55% at one-symbol lookahead, and where a script’s main Unicode block lies on one 256-codepoint page, the same-length UBE-BE control stays close to UTF-8 (Sec- tion 4.2). 2
