Extension to Multiple Languages Because both the speech encoder and text embedding model support multiple languages, SSEmb can be adapted to a new language by training an adapter gθ on ASR data in that lan- guage. For English, we use a two-layer MLP comprising a projection from ds to d, a GELU activation, a second d- dimensional projection, and layer normalization. For lower- resource languages, where limited supervision makes it par- ticularly important to exploit the pretrained encoder represen- tations effectively, we use a deeper convolutional adapter with N = 3 residual blocks. Each block combines a causal 1-D convolution for capturing local temporal patterns with a feed- forward network consisting of two linear projections and a GELU activation, with dropout applied within the block. 3. EXPERIMENTS We use the Audio Transformer encoder from Qwen3-Omni- 30B-A3B-Instruct [1] as the speech encoder. For the text retriever, we adopt Qwen3-Embedding [8] at three model scales: 0.6B, 4B, and 8B parameters. Both the audio en- coder and the text retriever are frozen during training. For English, SSEmb is trained on the 190-hour clean subset of LibriTTS [9]. For low-resource languages, we use subsets of Common Voice [10] and KsponSpeech [11]. We evaluate speech embedding quality using recall and nDCG@10 on speech-to-text retrieval. As no established benchmark exists for this task, we convert queries from tex- tual retrieval benchmarks into speech using an existing TTS model [12]. Although synthesized queries may not fully cap- ture the variability of natural speech, they provide a controlled and reproducible evaluation protocol [5]. For English, we use HotpotQA [13], MuSiQue [14], and the English subset of Mr. TyDi [15]; for other languages, we use the corresponding Mr. TyDi subsets. We additionally evaluate spoken-document retrieval on SpokenSQuAD [16] in both Text-to-Speech and Speech-to-Speech settings. We compare SSEmb with a cascaded system that first transcribes speech queries using whisper-large-v3-turbo [17] and then performs retrieval using the same Qwen3-Embedding model. We also compare against SONAR [18], a multilingual embedding model trained for text-to-speech translation that has also demonstrated effectiveness in retrieval [19]. 4. RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 4.1. Main Results Table 1 presents the results on English benchmarks of SSEmb with Qwen3-Embedding as the backbone at three model scales. SSEmb consistently retains the majority of the cas- caded baseline’s performance across all benchmarks and model sizes. SSEmb also surpasses SONAR by a wide mar- gin on all benchmarks, highlighting the advantage of align- ing to a retrieval-specialized embedding space over using a general-purpose cross-modal representation. SSEmb consis- tently shows lower latency than its cascaded counterparts, showing the effectiveness of end-to-end speech retrieval. Table 2 presents the results of SSEmb across six lan- guages (Korean, Japanese, Thai, Indonesian, Russian, and Arabic) using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B as the base model. SSEmb achieves performance comparable to the cascaded pipeline without any retrieval-specific supervision, demon- strating that speech-transcript alignment generalizes beyond English. Notably, SSEmb surpasses the cascaded baseline in Thai, likely because higher ASR error rates propagate tran- scription errors into retrieval, highlighting the advantage of end-to-end speech retrieval when ASR is less reliable. Table 3 shows the performance of SSEmb on spoken doc- ument settings. In the Text-to-Speech setting, SSEmb outper- forms the cascaded baseline across most metrics and achieves performance comparable to WavRAG, despite using a smaller model. In Speech-to-Speech setting, SSEmb closely matches the cascaded baseline, demonstrating that the adapter-based alignment transfers effectively to spoken document encoding without additional training.
