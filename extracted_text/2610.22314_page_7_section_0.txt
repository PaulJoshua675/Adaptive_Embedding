Appendix C details the taxonomies, and selection strategies for the benchmark. Persona We begin by sampling personas from Nemotron-Personas-USA (Meyer and Corneil, 2025), a dataset of synthetically-generated personas grounded in real-world demographic, geographic and per- sonality trait distributions for the United States. We define five demographic attributes for the per- sona: age band (six bands, from 18–24 to 65+), gender, relationship status (single, partnered or post- relationship), employment status (employed, student, unemployed and seeking work, or not in the labour force) and living situation (alone, with a partner or family, with others, or unstable). Age, sex and marital status are read from the record directly. Employment is derived from occupation and age whereas living situation is sampled on marital status and age. Additionally, each persona is attributed a level (low, mid, high) on each of the Big Five Personality traits (John and Srivastava, 1999). Clinical Anchor Given the Nemotron persona, we sample a clinical anchor that is composed of one or two presenting problems, together with the life domains in which the problems impair functioning. Ev- ery persona has a Primary Presenting Problem, drawn from the core categories (i.e., mood and emotions, well-being and functioning, interpersonal concerns, personal growth). A persona may also have a sec- ondary presenting problem. This can either be a second problem from the core categories, or one from a secondary-only group (i.e., chronic pain, chronic illness or physical symptoms, sexual concerns, housing or financial strain, parenting or caregiver strain). The taxonomy of presenting problems and life domains (Table 16) was also developed by the same clinical team who worked on MHQR (§3). It is grounded in Bakker’s presenting-problem framework (Bakker, 2019) and expanded using CORE-A (Evans et al., 2000), the Bern Inventory of Treatment Goals (BIT-T) (Grosse Holtforth and Grawe, 2002), and research on how adults use mental health apps and AI tools (Luo et al., 2025; Callahan et al., 2026; Fürtjes et al., 2024; Ueda et al., 2026). Case formulation Our case formulation framework was developed by PhD-level licensed psycholo- gists and draws inspiration from Patient-Ψ (Wang et al., 2024), which structures the formulation using Beck’s cognitive-conceptualization (Beck, 1976). This framework assigns each patient one of seven core belief clusters organized according to Beck’s cognitive triad (Table 21) depicting core beliefs of the self (helpless, unlovable, worthless), the world (unsafe, exploitative), and the future (unchangeable, futile). We extend this framework by incorporating a three-level quality of life metric (poor, moderate, good) and the Five Ps framework (Macneil et al., 2012) (presenting, predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective) and derive a taxonomy for each of the items. GLM-5.2-FP8 writes the case formulation in a single structured call, conditioned on the persona and clinical anchor. ■Presenting Problem (Table 16): a short clinical summary of the presenting problem drawn from the clinical anchor and an overarching goal. ■Predisposing Factors (Table 17): one or two earlier-life vulnerabilities, such as criticism, emotional neglect, loss or a family history of mental illness, written as a short history. ■Precipitating Factors (Table 18): one recent seminal event, such as a bereavement, a relationship ending, a health event or a financial shock, placed in time and described through what changed afterwards. ■Protective Factors (Table 19): one to three resources that support the patient’s well being, such as a person who checks in, a role that still holds, or a belief that things can change. ■Perpetuating Factors (Table 20): one to three coping behaviours, at least one of them maladaptive, such as avoidance, withdrawal, reassurance seeking or going over things repeatedly, each tied to a concrete situation in which it shows up. Conversational style In order to increase diversity of writing styles, we assign each patient a conver- sational style as independent knobs that are rendered as behavioural instructions in the system prompt: register (formal, plain or casual); verbosity (low, medium); frustration reactivity (low, medium, high); and a coping archetype. Archetypes (Table 22) capture coping styles patients frequently showcase, such as minimizing problems, deflecting questions, or focusing on worst-case scenarios. 4.2 Simulation Harness The harness runs each patient through an arc of sessions with the counsellor LM under test (Figure 1). Within a session, patient and counsellor LMs alternate turns, and every patient turn is produced in two steps: a move tool call, then the verbalisation.
