Step-level routing in agentic tasks. In sequential environments, determining when to escalate an action to a stronger model is typically guided by process-level verifiers and trajectory-level rewards [17, 20, 41], or frameworks for cost-aware decision support policies [21]. Because these approaches rely on explicit step-level correctness labels or complex episode-success evaluations, they are difficult to scale in open-ended domains. We contrast these paradigms with our agentic experiments, demonstrating that our framework can route actions autonomously at each step by predicting offline action disagreement, entirely bypassing the need for process verifiers or trajectory rewards. 3 Fundamentals of Weak-to-Strong Routing In this section, we study the problem of weak-to-strong routing jointly with the allocation of local inference computation.2 We first study this problem at the population level, assuming access to the underlying probabilistic quantities, which allows us to characterize the optimal solution and identify the fundamental principles governing sampling, selection, and routing. In the next sections, we use these principles to develop practical methods that do not require access to such population quantities. Setup and sampling. Let (P, Y, Yw, Ys) follow a joint distribution, where P ∈P is a prompt, Y ∈Y its correct answer, and Yw, Ys ∈Y are the weak- and strong-model responses, respectively.3 A sampling policy σ : P →N+ specifies the number of weak-model responses generated for each prompt. Given P, let Yw,1, . . . , Yw,σ(P) denote these responses, and define the resulting history as Hσ = (P, Yw,1, . . . , Yw,σ(P)). Selection and routing. Given the sampled weak responses, we define two policies. The routing policy decides whether to settle for one of the weak-model responses or query the strong model, while the selection policy determines which weak-model response to return if we settle for the weak model. Formally, π(Hσ) ∈[0, 1], ϕ(Hσ) ∈{1, . . . , σ(P)}, where π(Hσ) is the probability of querying the strong model and ϕ(Hσ) is the index of the selected weak response. Together, (σ, ϕ, π) determine how much local inference to use, which weak response to return, and when to seek strong-model assistance. Our goal is to maximize the accuracy of the final response while controlling the use of both weak- and strong-model inference. We impose separate budgets: Bw on the expected number of weak-model samples and Bs on the expected frequency of strong-model queries.
