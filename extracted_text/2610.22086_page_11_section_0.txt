B Annotation Details We developed two schemas: one applicable to all instruction-following datasets, and one dedicated to multi-dialogues, which requires evaluation of dialogues. The complete annotation schema is sum- marized in Table 7. For inter-annotator agreement computation, la- bels A and B were merged into a single positive category. This decision was motivated by the em- pirical observation that label B often acts as an intermediate or “attractor” category: annotators frequently assign B in borderline cases between clearly correct (A) and substantially problematic (C) responses, making the A/B boundary inherently less stable than the B/C or C/D boundaries. Col- lapsing A and B therefore yields a more robust three-way agreement setting (A/B vs. C vs. D). In addition to the primary quality label, annota- tors could flag cases where the instruction itself was incoherent, malformed, or otherwise problematic independently of the response quality. Furthermore, for labels B, C, and D, annotators were required to specify one or more predefined error categories (e.g., hallucination, format error, unnatural phras- ing, etc.) and were optionally allowed to provide free-text comments for qualitative feedback. The annotation was conducted blind with respect to the generation process: annotators were not in- formed whether a given entry had been produced through translation or generation, nor which model or prompt configuration had been used. This setup was designed to minimize confirmation bias and ensure that quality judgments reflected the intrinsic properties of the data rather than prior expectations about the pipeline. C Inter-Annotator Agreement: Detailed Analysis This appendix provides a detailed examination of inter-annotator agreement patterns, focusing on datasets with lower agreement values and identify- ing the underlying sources of disagreement. C.1 Multi-FLAN: Synthetic Reasoning Ambiguity Multi-FLAN shows agreement values of 0.93 (French) and 0.90 (German), below the threshold observed in most other datasets. The source of disagreement lies in the inherent ambiguity of eval- uating synthetically generated reasoning chains. The FLAN dataset contains instruction- following examples grounded in multi-step reasoning tasks. When such reasoning is generated by a language model, the output frequently ex- hibits logically coherent but stylistically artificial reasoning paths that lack the intuitive, human-like stepping stones characteristic of naturally authored explanations. This creates a legitimate grey zone in annotation: annotators may reasonably differ on whether a formally sound but awkward reasoning chain qualifies as satisfying from a training data perspective.
