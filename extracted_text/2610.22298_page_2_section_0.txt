We refer to cases from this series as “NEJM cases.” Subsequent studies reported strong performance on challenging clinical cases across multiple specialties [Rutledge, 2024, Hirosawa et al., 2024]. In the study by McDuff et al., the Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer (AMIE) system was evaluated on 302 NEJM cases and demonstrated strong performance both when independently generating differential diagnoses and when used by physicians as a decision-support tool [McDuff et al., 2025]. These findings suggest that contemporary LLMs are already capable of making a meaningful contribution to diagnostic reasoning in complex clinical cases. However, a clinical second opinion is not reducible to generating a differential diagnosis or selecting the most likely diagnostic hypothesis. A second opinion may change the representation of the patient’s condition, identify missing or contradictory information, reprioritize diagnostic hypotheses, alter the diagnostic workup or treatment strategy, and highlight previously underestimated risks. Studies by Payne et al. and Meyer et al. have shown that repeat expert review may lead to changes not only in diagnosis but also in treatment, with management changes in some settings occurring more frequently than diagnostic changes; moreover, it is not always possible to determine objectively which of the two opinions was more accurate [Payne et al., 2014, Meyer et al., 2015]. Thus, for LLMs, high diagnostic accuracy alone does not imply a high-quality clinical second opinion: diagnostic agreement does not fully capture factual grounding, the coherence of reasoning, the quality of diagnostic and therapeutic planning, uncertainty, or clinical safety. One approach to improving LLM performance is to explicitly structure the reasoning process. In radiology tasks, separating the structuring of clinical information from subsequent diagnosis generation improved accuracy compared with conventional zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting [Sonoda et al., 2025]. A two-stage diagnostic framework in which a distinct verification stage was introduced between the preliminary and final diagnoses also produced moderate, model-dependent improvements on MedQA and NEJM cases, while increasing answer consistency and reducing the frequency of several reasoning errors [Shao and Zhang, 2025]. However, the evidence remains heterogeneous. Prompts designed to imitate intuitive, analytical, Bayesian, or differential-diagnostic styles of physician reasoning did not produce consistent accuracy gains, particularly for stronger models [Savage et al., 2024]. More generally, the presence of an elaborate textual rationale should not be treated as evidence that the internal process leading to the answer was itself correct: chain-of-thought explanations can retrospectively rationalize decisions that were in fact driven by information not acknowledged in the explanation [Turpin et al., 2023]. Structured clinical reasoning should therefore be evaluated as a distinct empirical intervention rather than assumed to be superior merely because the response is longer or appears more “physician-like.” A second approach is to distribute analysis across multiple agents representing different medical specialties. Its natural clinical analogue is the multidisciplinary team (MDT), in which specialists from different disciplines contribute comple- mentary information to diagnosis and treatment planning. Studies of real-world MDTs, particularly in oncology, suggest that team discussion can alter diagnosis and management, although the magnitude of the effect varies substantially across studies and depends on information completeness, team structure, workload, leadership, and case complexity [Lamb et al., 2011, Basta et al., 2017, Koˇco et al., 2021, Soukup et al., 2020]. This logic has already been transferred to LLM-based systems. MDAgents dynamically selects between individual and collaborative modes depending on estimated medical complexity [Kim et al., 2024]. Multi-Agent Conversation (MAC) employs multiple physician agents and a supervising agent that iteratively discuss clinical cases [Chen et al., 2025]. Model Confrontation and Collaboration (MCC) implements structured debate among heterogeneous LLMs [Sun et al., 2026]. EvoMDT combines specialized agents, retrieval, and a coordinating component [Liu et al., 2026a]. These systems demonstrate the feasibility of multi-agent medical reasoning and motivate the use of a multidisciplinary LLM council, but they do not by themselves establish that inter-agent interaction is the causal source of performance improvements. This distinction is particularly important because multi-agent systems generally consume more computational resources than a single generation. Repeated sampling and self-consistency can improve reasoning performance without any message exchange between agents [Wang et al., 2023], while Self-Refine improves responses through repeated cycles of critique and revision performed by the same model [Madaan et al., 2023]. In non-medical settings, multi-agent debate has improved reasoning quality and factual accuracy [Du et al., 2024]; however, compute-matched analyses suggest that its advantage over self-consistency may be substantially smaller than its advantage over a single model response produced without iterative critique or inter-agent exchange [Wunderlich et al., 2026]. Deliberation itself may also introduce new classes of errors: consensus pressure can suppress a correct minority position, motivating consensus-free and trajectory-aware approaches [Cui et al., 2026]. Role assignment constitutes another independent variable. Specialized physician roles are intuitively appealing, yet studies of role and persona prompting indicate that assigning a role label alone does not guarantee improved objective performance and may make outputs sensitive to clinically irrelevant persona attributes [Zheng et al., 2024, Luz de Araujo et al., 2025]. A rigorous medical evaluation should therefore attempt to disentangle the effects of additional sampling, role diversity, aggregation, and inter-agent interaction itself. 2
