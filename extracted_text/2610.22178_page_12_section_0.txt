Preprint 8 DISCUSSION A vanilla VLA. REKI follows a standard VLA recipe: a VLM’s last hidden states are read by a flow-matching action expert, pretrained in a single stage on public robot, egocentric human and vision–language data, and then finetuned on each benchmark. It uses no proprietary data (cf. Yuan et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026), no world model (cf. Chen et al., 2026) and no additional training stages. Even so, it can compete with, and often outperform, VLAs of similar size pretrained on in-house data, and it leads the compared VLAs pretrained on public data out of distribution (Fig. 4), where pretraining adds 13.8 points on Clean2Rand and 4.3 on LIBERO-Plus (§6.1). Many of the strongest entries on current leaderboards take the other route: they are world-action models built on video generation models (Chen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026; Song et al., 2026) or VLAs pretrained on large proprietary data (Xiaomi Robotics Team, 2026; GigaBrain Team et al., 2026). These models set the current state of the art, but they can be costly to run (§6.3). Their pretraining is also hard to reproduce, which makes it difficult to separate the effects of data scale, model size, modelling choices and additional supervision such as world modelling. We intend REKI to serve as an open reference on which each of these factors can be tested in isolation. An efficient open baseline. We release REKI with its full pipeline, from data curation to deployment, and the checkpoints of every benchmark, so that new architectures, data mixtures or training objectives can be pretrained and compared against it under fixed conditions. The simplicity of the recipe and the efficiency of the code keep such comparisons affordable: finetuning REKI takes 8 H100 GPU-hours on our real-robot tasks and 40 to 400 on the benchmarks (Tables 3 and 11). The memory adds no cost at training (§4.2) and less than 1% of the inference time, so REKI is an efficient starting point for studying memory in embodied AI. In the released code, REKI runs entirely as CUDA graphs, memory included, and predicts an action chunk from the LIBERO inputs in 21.8 ms on one H100. It stays the fastest of the compared models even when their released code is compiled (§6.3). Why inference speed matters. Inference speed matters not only at deployment, where it bounds how quickly a policy can react (§6.3), but also for training. VLAs are increasingly post-trained with reinforcement learning, in simulation (Li et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025a; Zang et al., 2026) or in learned world models (Zhu et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2026), where the policy is queried for every action chunk of every rollout, so that its inference time is multiplied across the whole training run. At 21.8 ms per chunk, REKI offers a strong open starting point for such post-training and lowers the barrier to it, a step towards making it accessible beyond the few groups with large compute budgets. Memory. In REKI, a few register tokens in the action expert summarise each frame and stay in the expert’s cache, so the VLM still sees each frame only once. Even this short memory helps: with three frames, success rises by 1.5 to 5.5 points on LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin and RoboCasa365, and by 12 to 17.5 points on the longer real-robot tasks. Most of the gain comes from a single past frame, which in particular reduces repetitive oscillations between two poses, suggesting that a little temporal context is enough to tell apart observations that look alike (§6.2). A memory of a few seconds therefore appears sufficient for most tasks in standard benchmarks. Whether the registers can also carry the much longer context of tasks that depend on events far in the past remains to be tested. 12
