We test general- ization: train on 16 materials and evaluate on the held-out 17th. Audio embeddings predict Q for unseen materials (ρ ≈0.60; Table 2), and joint embeddings reach 0.49–0.57. This is physical generalization: a probe never trained on the held-out material still ranks its impacts by how much they ring, using only their audio embeddings. 5.2 Within Materials, and Which Component of Q Two checks ask whether these correlations are really about physics. The first computes ρ within a single material category, where category identity supplies nothing. The modalities separate sharply: PE-AV audio reaches 0.67 on metal and 0.57 on wood, whereas every visual encoder, including PE- AV’s video branch, is at or below 0.36 on metal and close to zero (|ρ| ≤0.19) on wood, ceramic, and plastic. CLIP scores 0.06, 0.03, −0.14, and −0.19 on metal, wood, ceramic, and plastic. Base- line vision barely distinguishes physical variation among objects of the same material. Table 7 in Appendix B gives the breakdown. The second asks which component of Q is responsible, since Q = πf0/β compounds a spectral term with a damping term, and across our impacts Q is strongly rank-correlated with f0 (ρ = 0.87) and moderately anti-correlated with β (ρ = −0.54). An encoder that merely recovered pitch would therefore score well. Probing the targets separately (Appendix A) shows that the damping term is the one audio captures best: PE-AV audio reaches ρ = 0.729 on β against 0.700 on f0, and the gap widens where category identity stops helping, with β holding at 0.686 on unseen materials while f0 falls to 0.485. Vision shows the opposite pattern at much lower levels, favoring f0 when pooled (0.347 against 0.221), which is consistent with pitch being partly inferable from visible size. The reading we place on Q therefore survives being tested directly, and β is the sharper target. 5
