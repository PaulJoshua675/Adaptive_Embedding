A behavioral assessment, conducted over 30 test episodes, measures the time spent on the food, the time spent with both robots on it, and the number of times a robot leaves the food for at least half a second. It also measures signal specificity, that is, the probability of emitting signals on the food and off it. In the EASA variants, it records which channel is active while the robot is on the food and how frequently avoidance is triggered when the partner is within 15 cm. Finally, we tested the champions' robustness in 50 episodes under conditions they had not encountered during development. These included a 1.5 m arena with 90-second episodes, motor noise with a different gain for each wheel in every episode and additional noise at each control step, and sensor noise three times greater than that of Webots. 4. Results 4.1 Performance by condition Figure 4 shows the evolution of fitness for all conditions in the small arena. In its three runs, GRU reached a mean population fitness close to 0.5 by the end of evolution. The EASA variants fell below this, with the hand-set priority variant having the lowest fitness. With a compass, the population reached a fitness of 0.9 in fewer than 40 generations. Table 3 summarizes the champions' test over 100 new episodes. The three runs of GRU scored 0.588, 0.574, and 0.600, with a mean of 0.587 and a standard deviation of 0.013. The three runs of the EASA evolved condition scored 0.362, 0.356, and 0.369, with a mean of 0.362 and a standard deviation of 0.007. The difference between conditions was clear both between runs (Welch's t-test, p = 0.0001) and between paired episodes, where GRU outperformed EASA evolved by 0.225, equivalent to about 13.5 s more on the food per robot (Wilcoxon, p = 5 × 10-11). With hand-set weights, EASA eat scored 0.336 and EASA safe 0.257. GRU with compass scored 0.928.
