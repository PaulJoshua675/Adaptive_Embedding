Page 8 of 20 Naganawa Discover Networks (2026) 2:41 4.2 Nouns associated with question engagement The keyness scatter in Fig. 3 shows that high-engagement questions (many answers and replies) and low-engagement questions (typically answered once, if at all) are separated primarily by scope. These are question-level lexical associations. High-engagement questions cluster around broad, debatable, civic and infrastruc­ ture nouns: “car” (z = 6.5), “war” (z = 4.9), “tax” (z = 4.8), “party” (z = 4.7), “shinkansen” (z = 4.3), “road” (z = 4.2), “expressway” (z = 4.1), “child” (z = 4.1), and “highway” (z = 4.1). Low-engagement questions, conversely, are dominated by narrow, transactional nouns: “logistics” itself is the strongest single low-engagement noun (z = − 9.7, the corpus’s own search keyword), alongside “product” (z = − 6.6), “Taobao” (z = − 6.5, a cross-border e-commerce marketplace), “company” (z = − 6.3), “warehouse” (z = − 6.2), and “center” (z = − 6.1). Inspection of the Japanese originals of Taobao-related questions confirms the interpretation: they overwhelmingly concern individual cross-border purchases — stalled parcel tracking, customs holds, forwarding agents, refund requests — that is, sin­ gle-answer transactional queries rather than discussion starters. The top-noun lists are robust to the split threshold: replacing the 75th/25th percentile split with a tertile split leaves 18 of the top 20 high-engagement nouns and 16 of the top 20 low-engagement nouns unchanged. Notably, “logistics,” “center,” “warehouse,” and “distribution” — nouns associated with a best answer are associated with a low-engagement question here. Since the two comparisons are computed on different units (answers vs. questions), this is not a within-post trade-off; rather, it suggests that the same operational vocabulary plays dif­ ferent roles depending on where it appears.
