We therefore keep a shared length codebook and the existing linear within-interval decoder. Testing the 30-by-32 proposal as a model. A new L32 mean model uses 30 positive log1p intervals and 32 fine cells per interval (960 leaves), preserving all other 72K settings and the exact non-numerical stage texts and mappings. On those training labels, oracle MAE falls from 0.006591 to 0.003891 m and P99 error from 0.045625 to 0.023542 m, but worst-case error rises from 0.7475 to 0.9800 m. The new numerical candidate text adds 527.43 tokens on average; the longest checked scalar sequence is 2,068 tokens, within the 4,096-token budget. Coverage and average quantization improve at a real input-cost increase. The learned model reduces VST-300 MAE from 0.335420 to 0.327732 m, only 2.29%, and lowers all five clas- sification benchmark scores. The much larger oracle gain does not translate directly into prediction accuracy. This motivates reducing fine-stage difficulty rather than adding still more representational resolution. Reducing the fine-stage candidate count. The 4/8 design in Equation (3) keeps the same coarse intervals and range but reduces positive leaves from 960 to 216. In the full length census, 108K positive labels use four fine candidates, 1.5M use eight, and 382 terminate at zero. Both the 32-fine and 4/8 representations meet the specified max(1 cm, 5% y) oracle tolerance on every audited label, with no overflow. Coarser cells increase oracle MAE from 0.039102 to 0.154041 m and maximum error from 21.39 to 59.55 m. The 4/8 codebook exchanges quantization resolution for a smaller candidate set. A second independently initialized model changes only these fine cardinalities. Choice, point, count, and angle implementations remain fixed; no length batching optimization is introduced in this quality comparison. The estimated scalar sequence length is bounded by 1,514 tokens. Training takes 1,369.33 seconds versus 1,604.98 for the prior 30-by-32 run under their recorded timing scopes.
