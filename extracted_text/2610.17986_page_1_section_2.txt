Progress in instance-level image retrieval is measured by growing the gallery. To separate strong em- beddings from weak ones, benchmarks add large sets of distractor images: revisited Oxford/Paris add a million (R1M) (Radenovi´c et al., 2018), Google Landmarks indexes millions (Weyand et al., 2020), and ILIAS (Kordopatis-Zilos et al., 2025) adds one hundred million. This works: perfor- mance keeps dropping as the gallery grows, which is exactly what makes these benchmarks discrim- inative. The price, however, is paid in indexing. Encoding 100M images through a ViT-L backbone costs on the order of 556 GPU-hours and produces ∼200 GB of descriptors for one model. The field treats this as the unavoidable cost of a meaningful comparison. That cost is paid again for every method, ablation, resolution, or backbone. Most of that cost is unnecessary. A natural shortcut is to evaluate against a small random subset of the distractors. This shortcut is distrusted because the resulting number is optimistic and, taken at face value, can mis-rank methods. So practitioners either pay the full price or retreat to smaller galleries. These are either ad-hoc random subsets, which are optimistic and not comparable across papers, or hand-built proxies such as mini-ILIAS (Kordopatis-Zilos et al., 2025), which report their own number rather than the web-scale one. The shortcut is salvageable because the gap between the small- and large-scale metric is not mys- terious.
