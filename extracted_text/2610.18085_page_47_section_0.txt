Global gradient clipping rescales all gradients by one common factor, set by their total norm, so the gradients enlarged by an orbit edit can shrink the gradients of all other blocks. The following subsections expand on this discussion, covering adaptive and sign-based optimizers, natural drift and weight decay, initialization, and training stability. H.1 Adaptive Normalization and Trajectory Equivariance Adam and AdamW normalize gradients using per-parameter moment estimates. With covariantly transformed moments and ϵ = 0, their normalized directions are unchanged by reciprocal rescaling. Equivariance requires the parameter displacements to scale with the parameters, which a shared learning rate does not provide (Theorem 2). Theorem 5 extends this obstruction to the standard block-local rules considered here. For ideal degree-zero rules on corresponding histories, Corollary 6 shows that the distortion in the log-ratio of relative data updates is half the distortion of gradient descent. This factor concerns the data-update term under the stated assumptions; full increments can also reflect weight decay, a fixed positive ϵ, and differences in clipping. Theorem 2 supplies a mechanism for a residual surgery gap even with correctly transformed moments. The depth and λ sweeps measure the training response, while the state-preserved, stale-state, and partial-state experiments (Appendices F.8, F.9, and F.10) examine the additional role of moment history. Adaptive-optimizer qualifications. Reset or stale moments and time-varying orbit moves can break the correspondence between current gradients and past statistics. A fixed positive ϵ must also be transformed according to Theorem 2 to preserve trajectory equivariance. Global gradient clipping generally changes its common clipping multiplier under a nonuniform diagonal rescaling of the gradient. Decoupled weight decay alone commutes with a fixed diagonal rescaling when ηω is unchanged. If learning rates are transformed to restore update equivariance, the decay coefficients must also be transformed to keep this product fixed. H.2 Sign-Based Optimizers and Soft Rebalancing Lion computes its update sign from a weighted combination of the current gradient and its momentum buffer, an EMA of past gradients. A positive scalar rescaling of both the gradient and the buffer leaves this sign unchanged. As in Example 3, this sign invariance produces the same numerical displacement, while a rescaled parameter trajectory requires a rescaled displacement. Sign invariance does not imply immunity to a symmetry tilt, even before accounting for clipping or a stale momentum buffer. Theorem 5 covers Lion directly as a degree-zero rule. The hard- and soft-rebalancing Lion comparisons (Appendices F.3 and F.4) measure the resulting training sensitivity: The λ = 0.2 tilt degrades Vanilla Lion training, and rebalancing recovers PPL in those comparisons. The Lion state ablation in Appendix F.5 reports a higher unperturbed mean PPL and greater seed variation for hard SAGGS than for Soft or state-covariant rebalancing. Soft SAGGS limits the correction at each event by applying only an α fraction of the log-norm correction, as in Eq. 19. Optimizer updates can introduce new imbalance between events. The synthetic study in Appendix G.13 separately reports a sampled sign-response diagnostic. For sign-based optimizers, we use α = 0.5 as the default, retain the λ = 0.2 protection, and assess the unperturbed cost with paired controls at λ = 0.
