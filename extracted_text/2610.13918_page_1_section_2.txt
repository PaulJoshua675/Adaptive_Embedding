Sleep disorders affect an estimated 10%–30% of the global adult population, contributing substantially to cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, affective disorders, and cognitive decline [3, 30]. Clinical polysomnography (PSG) serves as the diagnostic benchmark for evaluating sleep pathology, categorizing sleep into discrete 30-second epochs comprising wakefulness, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) stages N1, N2, and N3 slow-wave sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [3]. Despite the widespread clinical adoption of macroscale PSG metrics such as total sleep time (TST) and sleep efficiency (SE), these indices frequently fail to correlate with patients’ perceived daytime impairment or neurocognitive recovery [14, 20]. This divergence is evident in paradoxical insomnia, clinically designated as sleep state misperception. Patients suffering from paradoxical insomnia report profound subjective insomnia and daytime exhaustion, despite objective overnight PSG recording normal sleep duration, normal sleep onset latency (SOL), and preserved macro- architecture [14, 20].
