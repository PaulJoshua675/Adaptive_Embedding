This gap raises a fundamental question: does stochastic synaptic credit assignment impose a limit on attainable classification accuracy, or can improved estimator stability and extended training overcome it? Here, we show that extended large-batch training brings WP to approximately 97% test accuracy on MNIST, approaching the early BP reference of 97.5–98% in a fully connected network with one hidden layer. The remaining accuracy gap is approximately 0.5–1 percentage point. Training efficiency remains substantially different: under a ten-epoch budget, WP achieves 90.2%, compared with 97.4% for the reported BP reference. We further show that learning-rate reduction and averaging independent perturbation estimates can stabilize WP training in networks with five hidden layers. Finally, a finite- horizon analysis establishes conditions under which population WP can approximate a gradient-descent trajectory arbitrarily closely. These findings demonstrate that high classification accuracy is attainable through model-free, perturbation-based synaptic learning, while identifying the training conditions and computational costs that govern its practical performance [4]. 1.1 Perturbation learning: foundations and recent developments Scalar-feedback learning has a substantial history in model-free learning, stochastic error descent, and statistical gradient-following algorithms [2, 3, 5]. Simultaneous perturbation stochastic approximation provides a related finite-difference route to optimization [6], while early neural-network work connected weight and node perturbations and their computational costs [7]. Conductance-perturbation models extended these ideas to spiking networks and birdsong learning [8, 9]. Population averaging was subsequently used to reduce variability in perturbation-based learning [10]. The relative efficiency of WP and node perturbation (NP) is not determined by parameter count alone. Analyses in linear networks identify dimension-dependent learning costs [11], whereas Züge et al. show that trial duration and effective task dimensionality can make WP comparable or superior to NP [12]. The 2023 journal article succeeds the 2021 preprint used in the thesis and is reproduced with its supplement in Züge’s 2025 dissertation [13]. For NP, Hiratani et al. identify diffusion-driven instability under model mismatch and a stabilizing role for normalization [14]. Dalm et al. relate NP to directional derivatives and combine this perspective with input decorrelation, reporting substantially improved multilayer learning in their revised 2026 preprint [15]. For MNIST, Züge et al. report that their fully trained 784→100→10 network reaches 92.3 ± 0.2% with WP versus 97.7 ± 0.2% with SGD at B = 1,000 after 50,000 updates (five-run means and standard deviations; supplementary Table 2) [12]. The no-hidden-layer control has a smaller gap at a lower accuracy (Fig. S11); its teacher-target variants are different tasks. The dissertation retains these experiments, without adding a long-budget MNIST result that closes the hidden-layer gap [13]. Our study extends the batch-size and training-budget range; it does not establish superiority under a matched cross-study protocol. Recent work includes the Dopamine optimizer for XOR and time-series tasks [16]. Gaussian zeroth-order optimization [17], population-based evolution strategies [18], and forward-evaluation language-model fine-tuning [19] provide broader context, but not matched from-scratch MNIST benchmarks. Forward gradients use automatic differentiation [20] and are distinct from derivative-free loss-difference estimators. 2 Materials and Methods 2.1 Task and architectures MNIST contains 60,000 training and 10,000 test images with ten digit classes [21]. Following the thesis [4], images are flattened to 784 components, scaled to [0.01, 0.99], and processed by fully connected hidden layers with hyperbolic-tangent activation and biases, followed by a ten-unit softmax output. The shallow architecture is 784 →100 →10. The deeper configuration, called Scen1 in the thesis, has five hidden layers of 100 units each.
