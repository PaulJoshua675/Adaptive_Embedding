2.1 Dense Retrieval and Embedding Models Modern dense retrieval commonly relies on bi- encoder architectures that map queries and doc- uments into a shared embedding space, where retrieval is performed through vector similar- ity (Karpukhin et al., 2020). Early embedding ap- proaches such as Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) demonstrated the effectiveness of contrastive representation learning for semantic retrieval, followed by stronger instruction-tuned and large embedding models including INSTRUC- TOR (Su et al., 2023), E5 (Wang et al., 2022), and recent LLM-based embedding systems (Muen- nighoff et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Recent studies further explore using LLM hidden states directly as embeddings (Ma et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025) or leveraging in-context learning to improve representation quality (Li et al., 2025a). Beyond supervised retrieval data, dense retrievers have also been trained using unsuper- vised contrastive objectives and synthetic supervi- sion (Lee et al., 2019; Izacard et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2025). De- spite strong performance, most embedding models remain fundamentally single-pass encoders that construct representations without explicit semantic refinement or intermediate reasoning. 2.2 Reasoning for Retrieval Recent work increasingly incorporates reasoning into retrieval systems through query rewriting, decomposition, iterative retrieval, and reasoning- augmented reranking (Qin et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Asai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b; Long et al., 2025). For example, ReasonIR (Shao et al., 2025) synthesizes reasoning trajectories and hard negatives to train retrieval models on reasoning- intensive benchmarks (Hongjin et al., 2025). More recently, reasoning has been incorporated directly into embedding formation: multimodal methods such as RITE (Liu et al., 2025b), LREM (Tang et al., 2025), and UME-R1 (Lan et al., 2025) gen- erate reasoning before constructing retrieval repre- sentations, with further gains from inference-time scaling through sampling multiple reasoning trajec- tories (Muennighoff et al., 2024). However, reasoning is not uniformly benefi- cial for retrieval. Query expansion has long been known to suffer from query drift (Mitra et al., 1998; Carpineto and Romano, 2012), and recent work shows that LLM-based reasoning can hurt dense retrieval due to semantic drift (Fan et al., 2026; Kotte, 2026). Similar observations have emerged for chain-of-thought prompting more broadly (Liu et al., 2025a; Hassid et al., 2025; Sui et al.). Despite these findings, existing methods either supervise reasoning only through final retrieval outcomes or select among complete trajectories via best-of-k, providing no direct supervision over intermediate refinement states nor adaptive reasoning allocation at inference time.
