Observations. Figure 7 shows the projections. Under all three colorings, the [PHYSC] embed- dings form markedly clearer structure than the raw channels, and each of the three carries a distinct implication for this work. Separation by user position indicates that the representation resolves where a link terminates, which is a prerequisite for placing the evidence an observation carries at the correct location in the scene. Separation by dominant propagation direction indicates that the representation disentangles the an- gular components of the channel — the departure and arrival directions that determine which struc- tures a link interacts with — rather than retaining the raw superposition in which those components are mixed. Separation by scene identity is the most directly relevant here: observations taken in the same environment are encoded to nearby points, even though the users occupy different positions and therefore observe different channels. The representation thus carries information about the envi- ronment that produced a measurement and not merely about the measurement itself, which is what makes a set of embeddings from one scene an informative conditioning set for the posterior over that scene. Relation to the conditioning argument. The raw channels contain at least as much information as the embeddings computed from them, so the contrast in Figure 7 is not one of information content but of accessibility. In the raw space, observations that share an environment are not neighbors, and a decoder conditioned there must learn the mapping from channel structure to scene structure before it can exploit the conditioning at all. In the representation space, that structure is already exposed. This is the geometric counterpart of the learnability argument of Section 3, and it is consistent with the behavior of the two conditioning schemes in Section 4: the diffusion decoder conditioned on raw measurements does not improve as observations accumulate, while the same decoder conditioned on [PHYSC] embeddings does. Position information is essential because a channel measurement reveals propagation-induced geometric information but does not by itself anchor that infor- mation to an absolute location in the scene. The user position maps the propagation evidence to specific scene cells, enabling the model to spatially localize the structures associated with each observation.
