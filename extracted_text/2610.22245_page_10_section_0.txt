Supervised reference: For benchmark with ground-truth labels, we train a Proxy reward linear probe bfc. Requiring privileged supervision unavailable in open-ended tasks, it serves strictly as a supervised upper-bound reference rather than a fair label-free competitor. Evaluation metrics. We evaluate cascade performance by plotting task success (final-answer accuracy for single-turn benchmarks, and end-to-end trajectory success for agentic environments) against the realized routing budget (the fraction of escalated queries or actions). To isolate the quality of the routing signal, we fix sampling and selection across all baselines and, at each routing budget, escalate the instances with the highest scores. This allows us to compare routing signals under the same sampling and selection policies without introducing error from threshold calibration. Selection ablations are deferred to Appendix A.3. 6.1 Agentic Environments: Routing Without Ground Truth In multi-step environments, an agent completes a goal through a sequence of actions. Let Ht = (p, y1, o1, . . . , yt−1, ot−1) denote the trajectory history at step t, comprising the prompt p, past actions yi, and observations oi. Rather than escalating entire trajectories, we formulate routing at the action level: given Ht, the router decides whether to execute the weak model’s action locally (yt w) or defer that specific step to the strong model (yt s). Defining step-level correctness in sequential environments is fundamentally difficult. Because tasks admit multiple valid paths, and expert evaluation is costly to scale[32]. Furthermore, locally plausible actions may cause delayed downstream failures, making long-horizon credit assignment ambiguous [11]. Consequently, correctness-supervised routers and automated LLM judges remain notoriously unreliable in agentic domains [42, 54]. While correctness is ambiguous, step-level disagreement remains objective and easily verifiable. Let T (Ht, y) denote the transition triggered by action y (e.g., a parsed tool call or state change). Two actions are equivalent if they induce identical transitions: A(yw, ys) = 1{T (Ht, yw) = T (Ht, ys)}. Relying on observable mechanics rather than subjective evaluation enables effortless data collection from offline rollouts, entirely bypassing human labels and complex reward engineering. Datasets and models. We evaluate two interactive environments, generating K = 10 samples per step for training targets and multi-sample baselines. In BFCL Multi-Turn [36] (160 train / 40 test tasks), Qwen3-8B routes to GPT-5-mini; action agreement requires identical function calls and arguments. In WebShop [48] (2,000 train / 500 test), Qwen3-8B routes to a task-specialized RL policy. To prevent distribution shifts by test-time strong-model interventions, we collect training data via an iterative, DAgger-style schedule [26] (Appendix A.6). Figure 2 shows both disagreement-based signals consistently outperform the best label-free baseline. Crucially, contrasting the two environments reveals how teacher capability dictates the value of the self-disagreement correction. In WebShop, where the strong model is highly capable, corrected disagreement significantly outperforms raw weak–strong disagreement. This reinforces our population- level finding: under a stronger teacher, the self-disagreement penalty prevents wasteful queries and the approximation approaches optimal routing. Conversely, in BFCL, the strong model itself struggles; here, the self-disagreement correction provides negligible benefit, rendering the gap between the two scores statistically insignificant.
