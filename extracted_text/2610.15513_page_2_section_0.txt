Iterative refinement methods such as PAIR [9] and TAP [28] address some of these limitations but optimize a single objective without population diversity, ignoring transferability, stealth, and test-case length. The current state of the art in raw effectiveness is Andriushchenko et al. [4], who report 100% attack success rate (ASR) across Llama-2/3, GPT-3.5/4, and Claude via adaptive random search with log-probability-guided optimization. From a software-testing standpoint, their approach has three limitations that rule it out for realistic black-box deployment scenarios: it requires gray-box access to token probabilities that most production APIs do not expose; it relies on manually engineered templates crafted per target model; and it consumes roughly 10,000 queries per behavior, which makes continuous-integration testing impractical. Other recent methods, including, AmpleGCG [23], and AutoDAN-Turbo [25], either require privileged access, demand per-model manual engineering, or depend on powerful auxiliary models for effective mutation. This paper introduces REPRO (Reflective Evolutionary Prompt Optimization), a pure black-box adversarial prompt optimizer built around a population-based evolutionary loop with structured self-refinement, Bayesian strategy selection via Thompson Sampling, and multi-objective Pareto selection over attack success, cross-environment transfer, detectability, and prompt length. The underlying design philosophy is that optimization intelligence should emerge from algorithmic structure rather than from auxiliary model capability, so that the entire pipeline runs with only a quantized open-weight model on a single consumer GPU. Three elements of REPRO depart from prior work. Semantic crossover is, to our knowledge, the first LLM-guided multi-parent recombination operator in adversarial prompt optimization: it synthesizes new attacks by combining complementary techniques drawn from different strategy families. Structured failure analysis replaces the free-form self-reflection of prior iterative methods with a stateful, domain-specialized signal that classifies refusals into a taxonomy of defense types and routes each to ranked counter- strategies, so that feedback encodes attack-defense interaction knowledge rather than requiring the attacker model to rediscover it. A bi-level formulation separates population evolution from adaptive strategy selection, a decomposition motivated by the discrete and combinatorially large natural- language attack space, where principled exploration control is a prerequisite for sample-efficient search rather than a convenience. Section 1.2 states each precisely and Section 4.4 reports what the ablation does and does not establish about them. The remaining components (evolutionary population management, Thompson Sampling bandit selection, LLM-as-judge scoring, and iterative self-refinement) are composed from established techniques in evolutionary computation, bandit theory, and automated prompt optimization [1, 18], specialized here for the adversarial objective regime. Empirically, REPRO achieves a 100% attack success rate against all evaluated target models across three standard red-teaming benchmarks (AdvBench, JailbreakBench and HarmBench), matching the effectiveness of Andriushchenko et al. while operating in a pure black-box setting without logit access or manually crafted templates. It does so using only a quantized open-weight attacker model on a single consumer GPU. Measured across 211 behaviors spanning five target models, REPRO terminates with a median of 24 target queries per behavior, with over half of behaviors completing within 24 queries, compared to up to 10,000 target queries per behavior for prior gray-box state of the art. REPRO also maintains diverse non-dominated solutions along a Pareto front over attack strength, transferability, and stealth, a capability single-objective methods cannot express. The experiments in this paper exercise only the attack-success objective, so we report this as an implemented mechanism rather than an evaluated result (Section 4.1.3).
