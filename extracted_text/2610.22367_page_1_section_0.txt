Lukin1, 2, 5 1Harvard Quantum Initiative for Science and Engineering, Cambridge, MA 02138, USA 2Department of Physics, Harvard University, Cambridge, MA 02138, USA 3Anthropic STEM Fellows Program 4Anthropic 5QuEra Computing, 1380 Soldiers Field Road, Boston, MA 02135, USA Significant efforts are currently being directed toward the implementation of large-scale quantum information processors, but realizing useful quantum advantage also requires the development of efficient quantum algorithms. While for many practical problems the dominant resource costs are associated with a small set of computational primitives, optimizing them in the quantum setting presents unique challenges, including a reversibility constraint absent from ordinary classical computation. Here we introduce an evolutionary framework in which large language models (LLMs) propose circuit designs and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them. We apply this framework to tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation, and number theory. In seven of the eight tasks, the discovered circuits significantly reduce both Toffoli and qubit counts relative to the state of the art. In the eighth, the search discovers a new reversible architecture for prime-field modular inversion. By handling reversibility differently, the architecture makes divsteps—a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic—viable and substantially improves the Toffoli–qubit trade-off for modular inversion. These results open the door for evaluator-guided LLM co-design of new algorithmic structures for quantum computation. Computational primitives based on classical function evaluation play a central role in a wide range of quantum algorithms. Such primitives include decoding routines for optimization [1], numerical functions for quantum simula- tion [2], arithmetic routines for number theory [3], and quantum read-only memory (QROM), which coherently loads classically specified data into quantum registers [4]. To operate on quantum data, these functions must be compiled into circuits that can be executed coherently on quantum hardware. This imposes two constraints absent from ordinary classical programs. First, because the in- put may be in superposition, the circuit cannot inspect an intermediate value and select an ordinary classical control-flow path. Data-dependent behavior is instead expressed through controlled operations within a fixed gate sequence, so every branch contributes to the circuit’s cost even if it applies only to some inputs. Second, the computation must be reversible: intermediate information cannot simply be overwritten or discarded. Temporary values must eventually be uncomputed, returning their qubits to their initial state and preventing residual infor- mation from remaining entangled with the output. Until they are uncomputed, these values occupy additional qubits. At the same time, when bounded error is permit- ted, machinery needed only for rare or atypical inputs can sometimes be omitted, reducing resource requirements while keeping the failure probability controlled. Efficient coherent compilation is of major practical importance, and recent reductions in resource requirements have come from coordinated changes to arithmetic algorithms, data representations, and their reversible implementation [5, 6]. Automated circuit op- timizers therefore typically preserve correctness by re- stricting the search to predefined equivalence-preserving rewrites [7], limiting them to variations of a fixed design. Here we introduce a framework that pairs large lan- guage model (LLM) proposals with an independent eval- uator that verifies and scores each candidate. We focus on a class of quantum circuits that compute a classical function f: for an arbitrary input |ψ⟩= P x,y αx,y |x⟩|y⟩, the circuit must act as |ψ⟩7−→P x,y αx,y |x⟩|y ⊕f(x)⟩, where |x⟩and |y⟩are computational-basis states of the input and output registers, respectively. By linear- ity, this coherent action is correct if the circuit per- forms the required map on every computational-basis input: |x⟩|y⟩7−→|x⟩|y ⊕f(x)⟩. This basis-state re- duction makes candidate circuits amenable to program- matic evaluation, since every allowed operation maps a computational-basis state to another computational-basis state, possibly changing its phase, but never creating a superposition [8]. The action of a candidate on any chosen input can therefore be simulated by tracking a bit string and its phase, rather than 2n amplitudes. Resource ac- counting is also simple: we use the Toffoli count T and the peak qubit count Q, and rank circuits by their product, the TQ-cost. Together, these properties let us build an efficient, programmatic evaluator that checks the correct- ness and resource cost of any candidate circuit design. Each search begins from a hand-written task specifi- cation and a blank program; the specification states the required circuit behavior but provides no implementation guidance.
