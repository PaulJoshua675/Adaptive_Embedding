We stratify queries by historical frequency into low (0–3), medium-low (4–99), medium (100–999), and high-frequency (≥ 1000) buckets. As shown in Table 7, the strongest business-value gains arise at the long-tail and head regions, while the engagement benefit extends to medium-frequency traffic. On long-tail traffic, WeGR significantly improves Order by 3.24% for low-frequency queries and improves both Order and CVR by 3.86% and 3.58%, respectively, for medium-low-frequency queries. These conversion- oriented gains suggest that the learned SID space can transfer col- laborative and semantic structure to queries for which direct behav- ioral evidence is limited. For high-frequency queries, WeGR yields significant gains of 3.51% in Order and 2.73% in CTR, indicating that the model also effectively exploits abundant interaction signals for head traffic. Medium-frequency queries exhibit a significant CTR improvement of 0.99%, despite statistically insignificant changes in Order and CVR, suggesting that increased engagement does not yet translate into downstream conversion within this bucket. Overall, WeGR exhibits complementary effects across the traffic distribution: it primarily strengthens conversion outcomes in the long tail, improves both engagement and orders at the head, and provides a more moderate engagement benefit in the middle. We emphasize that this analysis identifies where the online gains arise; establishing the precise mechanism behind these heterogeneous treatment effects requires further controlled investigation. We assess the cross-scenario transferability of DHPO through an independent evaluation in WeChat e-commerce live- stream search, with its SFT-initialized counterpart serving as the reference. As reported in Table 8, DHPO yields consistent im- provements across all twelve metrics on the 18K order- and click- supervised evaluation sets. The gains are particularly pronounced at shallow retrieval depths: HR@6 increases by 7.07% and 5.95%, while MRR@6 increases by 7.82% and 6.12%, under order and click super- vision, respectively. Moreover, the persistent MRR improvements across cutoffs indicate that DHPO refines the relative ordering of be- haviorally preferred items throughout the retrieved list, whereas the larger gains at 𝐾= 6 suggest a stronger concentration of such items near the top ranks. The consistency of these effects in a distinct pro- duction domain supports the central premise of DHPO: preference alignment can be transferred via behavior-grounded, dynamically expanded optimization horizons without a scenario-specific reward model. As this study isolates the policy-optimization stage, the ev- idence pertains specifically to the transferability of DHPO rather than that of the complete WeGR framework.
