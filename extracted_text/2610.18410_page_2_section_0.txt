Our central hypothesis is that graph-modified embeddings are more useful for guiding the expansion of retrieval than for measuring relevance to the original query. The best-performing strategies therefore assign distinct roles to the two sets of embeddings. An initial search over the original embeddings retrieves semantically relevant results (seeds). Their graph-modified embeddings are then aggregated to guide the selection of candidates that are both relevant to the query and related to the seeds through the graph. Finally, the original embeddings of the selected candidates are used to reformulate the query for another search over the original embeddings [8, 9, 10]. We refer to this use of graph-modified embeddings to guide retrieval as graph conditioning. To evaluate these strategies, we introduce a synthetic professional-network benchmark in which the corpus, graph, questions, answers, and supporting evidence are generated from the same underlying facts. The benchmark includes single-fact, relational and collection, and extremisation questions, together with exact gold answers and complete gold-evidence sets containing the intermediate and competing records required to establish each answer [12]. We select configurations on a development corpus and report results on separately generated validation data (Section 3.1). All methods use the same original embeddings, cosine similarity, and the same 40-node retrieval limit. Multi-stage controls without graph conditioning allow us to distinguish improvements produced by graph-modified embeddings from those produced by multi-stage retrieval alone. None of the graph-modified representations improves direct retrieval over the original embed- dings in our experiments. The original embeddings recover 73.1 % of the gold evidence, whereas the five transformations recover between 52 % and 72.9 %. In contrast, multi-stage retrieval improves performance across all five transformations, and the choice of retrieval strategy has a greater effect than the transformation used to construct the graph-modified embeddings. The strongest configuration improves gold-evidence recovery relative to plain vector retrieval by 25.9 % on extremisation questions and by 10.0 % on relational and collection questions (19.2 % across the two groups). It also matches the gold-evidence recovery achieved by plain vector retrieval with 40 nodes after returning only 24, corresponding to 40 % fewer returned nodes. Contributions. • We provide a controlled comparison of multiple graph-based embedding transformations and retrieval strategies under a common cosine-similarity framework, without requiring graph access at query time. • We introduce a synthetic benchmark with questions of different relational complexity, exact gold answers, and complete question-specific supporting evidence. • We show that retrieval performance depends more on how graph-modified embeddings are used than on the particular transformation that produces them, and that they are most effective as intermediate signals within a multi-stage retriever. • We introduce a retrieval strategy in which graph-modified embeddings guide exploration while the original embeddings preserve semantic alignment with the query, and we quantify its performance. 2
