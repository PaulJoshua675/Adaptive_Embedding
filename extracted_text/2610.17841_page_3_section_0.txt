Evaluation of ten off-the-shelf sparse and dense retrieval models, without fine-tuning, shows that aggregate ranking performance alone can mask important weaknesses. Dense encoders achieve substantially higher ranking quality than BM25 [36] (nDCG@10 = 0.87 vs. 0.77), yet the best model achieves only 0.51 accuracy on four-way hierarchical relevance classification. Model scale is also not consistently predictive: the 4B Qwen3 [42] encoder slightly outperforms its 8B counterpart on sibling discrimination. Attribute-level evaluation further localizes these differences: models per- form strongly on product type and gender (> 0.93) but sub- stantially worse on material (≈0.52) and style (≈0.43). Finally, a human study comparing benchmark queries with real user queries shows comparable perceived naturalness and strong intent clarity, supporting Fashion-RQBENCH as a proxy for real-world fashion search. Our contributions are: • We repurpose RQ-VAE semantic identifiers as an auto- matic annotation mechanism and introduce an attribute exclusivity loss that encourages quantization levels to encode coherent attributes. Prefixes define relevance sets, prefix length controls granularity, and shared-prefix length defines graded relevance. • We introduce a cluster-conditioned query generation pro- cedure that captures each cluster’s attribute distribution and its relational differences from parent and sibling clusters, enabling automatic generation of shopper-style queries. • We introduce FASHION-RQBENCH, comprising 588 clusters, 5,380 attribute-enriched queries, and 44,446 products, with deterministic graded relevance and four complementary evaluation tasks. • We propose weighted query-relevance and per-attribute metrics that distinguish overall intent satisfaction from fine-grained attribute satisfaction. 2. Related Works Fashion Retrieval Datasets and Benchmarks. Fash- ion retrieval involves fine-grained attributes such as product type, color, pattern, material, occasion, season, and price. Existing resources address different aspects of this prob- lem. DeepFashion [22] and DeepFashion2 [11] provide large-scale benchmarks for fashion recognition, detection, segmentation, and retrieval; Fashion200K [12] and Shop- ping100k [3] support attribute-based search; FACAD [53] focuses on fashion captioning; and Fashion Product Im- ages [2] provides the catalog schema used in our work. Language-conditioned retrieval is studied primarily through composed image retrieval, including FashionIQ [51], and more general benchmarks such as CIRR [23], CIRCO [4], and LaSCo [20], with fashion-oriented encoders includ- ing FashionCLIP [6], Marqo-FashionSigLIP [16], and GCL [57]. Most existing fashion benchmarks use single-target rele- vance, whereas open-ended product queries can correspond to a set of valid items; CIRCO [4] is a notable exception, but at modest scale. Graded set-valued relevance is avail- able in product search: ESCI [33], for example, provides exact, substitute, complement, and irrelevant labels, but re- quires 2.6M manual judgments for 130K queries. Manual construction also fixes the query distribution and catalog snapshot, making large-scale regeneration costly. Outside fashion, QUEST [25] automatically constructs set-valued queries from Wikipedia categories, but relies on an exist- ing taxonomy. In contrast, FASHION-RQ derives both rel- evance sets and queries from the catalog’s learned semantic structure, avoiding manual relevance annotation and prede- fined taxonomies. Generative Retrieval and Semantic Identifiers. Genera- tive retrieval directly decodes item identifiers from queries. DSI [40] and NCI [49] established this paradigm, with hier- archical identifier construction shown to be effective. Sub- sequent approaches learn identifiers through discrete auto- encoding [39] or residual quantization for relevance-aware, prefix-scorable representations [55]. In recommendation, TIGER [32] represents items using SIDs produced by an RQ-VAE [19, 45]; its coarse-to-fine codebooks induce a prefix hierarchy, while Singh et al. [38] demonstrate bene- fits for cold-start and long-tail recommendation. Recent work explicitly shapes this hierarchy. HiD- VAE [9] supervises quantization levels with hierarchical item tags and introduces a uniqueness objective to reduce code collisions. These approaches, however, use seman- tic identifiers primarily as representations for downstream retrieval or recommendation. FASHION-RQ instead repur- poses the hierarchy as an annotation structure: prefixes de- fine product groups at different granularities and shared- prefix length provides graded relevance. We further intro- duce level-wise attribute supervision with an attribute ex- clusivity loss to encourage semantically coherent clusters, using the resulting tree to construct the benchmark rather than the retrieval identifiers themselves. Automated Query and Relevance Construction. Syn- thetic query generation reduces the cost of manual anno- tation. Doc2Query [30] generates queries for documents, while InPars [5] and Promptagator [8] use LLM-generated query–document pairs for retrieval training. These ap- proaches are primarily document-conditioned: a query is generated from an individual document and typically inher- its a single positive target. Rahmani et al. [31] extend synthetic generation to evaluation, using LLM-generated queries and judgments to reproduce retrieval rankings, but the resulting relevance labels remain pairwise LLM assess- ments.
