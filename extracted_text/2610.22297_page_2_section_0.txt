Private Retrieval Augmented Generation via Random Projection A PREPRINT In Section 4, we empirically show that enforcing differential privacy guarantees may not provide better practical protec- tion against data extraction and can instead harm utility. In summary, our RP technique significantly improves utility (over 50% on average across datasets) over the baseline methods without degrading empirical privacy performance. 2 Related Works Private RAG. Prior work shows that various RAG architectures can reveal sensitive personal data (e.g., emails, phone numbers, and URLs) from the external datastore [3, 5, 8, 9, 10]. Existing defenses, such as DP-based sampling and aggregation [6, 11] and synthetic data [12], reduce efficiency on large datasets and often require extra validation or training. Moreover, Zhao and Zhang [13] show that synthetic data alone does not fully prevent memorization or leakage. He et al. [14] apply local differential privacy to sensitive content like addresses. However, privacy can involve entire sentences or paragraphs beyond tokens, making simple masking insufficient. Thareja et al. [15] mix original and sanitized documents, which requires identifying all private tokens and is impractical for large-scale RAG. Privacy-Preserving Random Projection. Certain random projection operations are a common technique for re- ducing dimensionality while preserving some similarity measures with high probability (e.g., based on the Johnson- Lindenstrauss lemma [16]). Prior works have shown that methods built on top of these projections can achieve differential privacy (DP) for specific applications [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. As illustrated in Figure 1, our algorithm is compatible with both RAG architectures. In this work, we evaluate our method as an empirical defense against data extrac- tion attacks. We consider a black-box setting in which the attacker has access only to model API responses, while model parameters, the random projection matrix, logits, embeddings, and latent representations remain inaccessi- ble [26, 27]. The attacker submits queries to the RAG sys- tem using the API service and attempts to extract sensitive information in the datastore from the generated response through one round or multiple rounds. This is a common protocol in modern RAG applications where companies provide consulting service through LLM with an internal datastore while only text answers are exposed to users. The threat model also aligns with the threat scenarios considered in prior work on RAG privacy [3, 4, 28]. In short, we assume the attacker knows that RP is used but does not have direct access to the specific random matrices, with the projection matrix remaining undisclosed. Attackers are free to conduct repeated-query (Section 4.2) or multi-round adaptive attacks (Section 4.3). As network- and system-level attacks are out of scope and access to embeddings is restricted, we do not consider white-box attacks, embedding inversion, projection-matrix inversion, or side-channel attacks requiring side information or access beyond the LLM’s text responses.
