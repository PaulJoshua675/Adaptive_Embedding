In hierarchical byte-level language models with learned patching, a boundary predictor groups the input into patches for the global transformer. The character distinctions needed to predict these units depend on the writing system and the segmentation target. Consider a grapheme cluster, Uni- code’s approximation to a written character, which may combine a base with dependent marks. After identical left context, one-byte lookahead at a codepoint boundary in UTF-8 can reveal the same byte for a mark extending the current cluster and a letter starting the next. The required de- cisions then remain indistinguishable even when the target segmentation is unambiguous. Byte-level models can pro- cess text in any language out of the box (Xue et al., 2022); at limited lookahead, however, the encoding does not expose every script’s distinctions equally. When clusters consist of single codepoints, the task changes: a subword boundary predictor must select which inter-character boundaries to retain. These tasks require different distinctions, motivating us to study encoding choice in relation to both the script and the boundary target. The encoding affects both how densely codepoint bits are packed and which bits reach the predictor first. For a Unicode scalar (a codepoint other than a surrogate) that takes three UTF-8 bytes, the pattern 1110xxxx 10xxxxxx 10xxxxxx carries 16 codepoint bits and 8 length/continuation bits (The Unicode Consortium, 2025). The same scalar occupies 16 bits in UTF-16 without those markers, so UTF-8 is inherently less compact for such scalars. We use the existing Universal Byte-Level Encod- ing (UBE; Kim & Jung, 2026), which retains one- and two-byte UTF-8 encodings and uses UTF-16 little-endian (UTF-16LE) for longer ones. For three-byte UTF-8 scalars, UBE therefore shortens the representation to two symbols and presents 8 low codepoint bits in the first symbol, where UTF-8 presents only 4 high bits. Within a known 256- codepoint page, that byte identifies the codepoint.
