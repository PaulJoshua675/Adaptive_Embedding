Tracking the number of goals achieved is an effective measure of how well a policy can stably transition an object across regions of the body. Despite noisy commands, WBD largely recovers the clean experts’ mean goal-reaching performance across training objects at both plotted noise levels (Fig. 4). Averaged over these two noise levels and all five guidance values, mean time to failure increases relative to the noisy expert by 70% on training objects and by 158% and 71% on the held-out sphere and cube, respectively. Training objects are weighted equally. At the lower noise level and guidance weights of 1–20, WBD even exceeds the clean experts’ time to failure, by 25–35% for the training-object mean and by 109–120% and 25–47% for the held-out sphere and cube, respectively, showing that the WBD controller maintains stable object holding beyond what the uncorrupted experts achieve. Real-World Experiments We evaluate a WBD controller trained on the full 15- object WBD-EX trajectory dataset on two on-body repo- sitioning tasks and a long-horizon shelf task (Fig. 6). For repositioning, a size 3 basketball or a cardboard box (15 cm cube) starts in a two-handed front grasp. The shoulder tap task succeeds when the object contacts the shoulder link, and the underarm stow task succeeds when the object is held stably under one arm, with its center approximately as far forward as the shoulder link. A round trip additionally requires returning to a stable two-arm front grasp. We report region-reaching success, full round-trip success, and mean time to each endpoint over successful trials (Table I). The shelf task requires picking the same cardboard box from a shelf, stowing it under one arm, stably carrying it while walking, un-stowing it, and placing it on a table. Pick and un- stow require a stable two-arm front grasp, and stow uses the underarm criterion, except that the free arm may stabilize the box while walking. We report success counts for each subtask: pick, stow, carry, un-stow, and place (Table II). All trials are run by a single skilled human teleoperator with 8+ hours of training on these tasks. We use an Xsens motion capture suit to track motion beside and behind the operator, where standard VR headset tracking is limited. The controller can also accept commands from VR headsets and trackers, although limited tracking coverage can restrict dexterity. The operator indicates the desired motion, while WBD refines the arm targets to maintain object support as the arms and torso reconfigure. For the long-horizon task, the operator switches the guidance weight between 10 for on-body repositioning and 100 for pick-and-place, balancing stable support with responsive command following. We compare WBD refinement of motion retargeted by General Motion Retargeting (GMR) [50] against direct GMR control and SONIC [43]. For WBD and direct GMR, GMR retargets the Xsens motion to G1 upper-body targets, and Adaptive Motion Optimization (AMO) [3] controls the lower body and balance. SONIC runs its public checkpoint in VR three-point mode, tracking Xsens head and wrist poses while its kinematic planner controls the lower body. The real-world results suggest that maintaining object support through contact transitions is a central bottleneck (Tables I and II). GMR let the operator move the arms toward the relevant body regions, but stable contact during reposition- ing remained difficult; SONIC additionally struggled with reachability in constrained regions. Across the three tasks, counting complete on-body round trips for both objects and full shelf sequences, the WBD controller completed 45 of 50 trials (90%), compared with 9 of 50 (18%) for GMR and 2 of 50 (4%) for SONIC. In simulation, cube WBD-EX experts have lower mean regional route completion than sphere experts (Table VIII).
