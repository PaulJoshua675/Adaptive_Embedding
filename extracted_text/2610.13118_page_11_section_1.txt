The best individual of each generation was selected on the basis of only six episodes, so its fitness estimate is biased upward, since it may have been partly lucky. For each run, we choose a champion from the best of the last ten generations, evaluating them in 12 validation episodes that were not used during evolution. The one with the highest average fitness is the champion. Each champion is tested in 100 new episodes with three versions of the signal. In the normal version, the signal functions as in evolution. In the disabled version, no packets arrive. In the random version, packets arrive as expected, but with a randomly chosen direction, preserving the information that the partner emitted a signal while destroying information about its location. Since all three versions use the same episodes, we compare them using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Comparisons between conditions with three runs use Welch's t-test on the average of each run. The disabled version must be interpreted with caution. If a network transmits almost continuously, its peer may rely on that input as if it were constant, and removing it could disrupt the network for reasons unrelated to the information itself. Therefore, we interpret the disabled version in conjunction with the random version and signal specificity.
