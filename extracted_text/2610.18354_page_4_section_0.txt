Related work addresses LLM evaluations by adapting cross-disciplinary methods such as signal processing [8], experiment-design theory [14], and psy- chometrics [15]. They find that in tradi- tional evaluations (e.g. question-answering benchmarks), even a modest amount of replication (e.g. N ≈5) substantially sta- bilises the estimates. We argue that due to its interactive opponent-conditioned struc- ture, strategic LLM evaluation is inherently noisier and requires a higher number of replications. To quantify the noise in strate- gic play, we define a method to measure the levels of signal and noise across games by adapting Heineman et al. [8]’s work. See Appendix B for further details. 3.4 Evaluation Metrics To measure strategic performance, we capture the game utility vi, associated cost ci, and their trade-off represented as the net gain gi. The game utility vi is min-max normalised (ˆvi ∈[0, 1]) based on its game-specific bounds (e.g. the economic profit in Cournot Duopoly, or the number of winning battlefields in Colonel Blotto game). The associated inference cost ci is represented by the per-model token usage cost (in dollars $) using the OpenRouter1 listings as market proxies. This cost is min-max normalised (ˆci ∈[0, 1]) based on the portfolio-bounds (minimum and maximum per-token price observed across the model portfolio). Finally, we define net gain gi as the weighted sum of normalised utility ˆvi and cost ˆci gi = ˆvi + λ(1 −ˆci) 1 + λ , gi ∈[0, 1], (2) where λ represents the cost trade-off weight and is fixed at λ = 1 across our runs. Any game episode is replicated across N seeds to account for stochastic noise. Each of the N seed runs yields a single sample, from which we compute the mean ¯X, the standard error (SE), and the 95% confidence interval (CI).
