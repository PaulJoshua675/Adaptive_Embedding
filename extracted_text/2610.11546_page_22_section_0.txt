In all scenarios, a broken brace is assumed to contribute zero stiffness while the structural mass remains unchanged. The corresponding theoretical inter-story stiffness reductions for each damage scenario, as computed in the original study, are presented in Table 8. It is noted that structural damage can in principle introduce non-negligible coupling between lateral and torsional stiffness terms (k(f) 1 , k(f) 2 , and k(f) 3 for f = 1, . . . , 4). However, as demonstrated in the original benchmark study [48], the magnitudes of these off-diagonal coupling terms remain substantially smaller than the primary diagonal stiffness components under the considered damage scenarios. Accordingly, the identification process retains its focus on the 12 principal stiffness parameters, which are the most damage- sensitive and physically interpretable quantities, while coupling terms are excluded. This simplification helps improve computational tractability and identification robustness, particularly under the constraint of incomplete modal information adopted in this study. The identified averaged results of the 12 stiffness parameters (i.e., k(f) 1 , k(f) 2 , and k(f) 3 for f = 1, . . . , 4) across the 5 damage scenarios, achieved from the DRL-DCO agent-inference phase, are presented in Figure 9, alongside the corresponding baseline-condition values and theoretical damaged reference values for the 5 damage scenarios. For Damage Scenarios 1 and 2, which involve complete brace removal at the first floor and at both the first and third floors, respectively, the DRL-DCO algorithm identifies all affected stiffness components with close agreement to the reference values across both scenarios; for examples, 58.43 MN/m versus 58.37 MN/m for k(1) 1 , 19.81 MN/m versus 19.67 MN/m for k(1) 2 , 80.75 MN/m versus 81.30 MN/m for k(1) 3 in Scenario 1. Additionally, the unaffected parameters remain consistent with the baseline values. These results demonstrate the algorithm's reliability in accurately localizing and quantifying relatively severe, multi-directional damage. For Damage Scenarios 3 and 4, which involve single-brace failure at the first floor and combined single- brace failures at the first and third floors, respectively, the lateral stiffness components are identified with reliable accuracy, while slightly larger discrepancies are observed in the torsional components. In Scenario 3, the identified value for k(1) 2 is 55.39 MN/m against a reference of 55.84 MN/m, while k(1) 3 is identified as 202.21 MN/m against a reference of 209.12 MN/m. In Scenario 4, the corresponding identified values are 55.02 MN/m versus 55.84 MN/m for k(1) 2 , 203.69 MN/m versus 209.12 MN/m for k(1) 3 , 95.95 MN/m versus 94.54 MN/m for k(3) 1 , and 204.34 MN/m versus 210.77 MN/m for k(3) 3 . These deviations are attributable to a known limitation of the simplified shear-type identification model: by treating lateral and torsional stiffness as fully uncoupled, it cannot reproduce the coupled modal interactions present in the high-fidelity benchmark FEM, whose off-diagonal stiffness contributions are implicitly reflected in the reference torsional reductions. This modeling discrepancy is a structural characteristic of the identification setup rather than a deficiency of the DRL-DCO algorithm itself, as the same discrepancy would affect any optimizer using this identification model. Damage Scenario 5, involving a partial one-third stiffness reduction in a single brace at the first floor, represents the most challenging case due to the small magnitude of the induced stiffness change. The DRL-DCO correctly identifies the damage-affected DOFs and the direction of stiffness reduction in all affected parameters, though with modest numerical deviations. For examples, the identified value for k(1) 2 is 65.71 MN/m, slightly higher than the referenced damaged value of 63.88 MN/m, while the torsional component k(1) 3 is identified as 219.79 MN/m, lower than the referenced value of 225.34 MN/m.
