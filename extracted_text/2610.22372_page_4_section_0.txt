We address this gap with LIBERO-PeRM, which provides a controlled taxon- omy of observable preferences in simulation and five preference-aware task suites targeting key personalization aspects. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [2, 4, 6, 53, 54, 55] [56] have shown strong reasoning and generalization abilities by leveraging VLM backbones [57, 58, 59], large-scale multimodal instruction datasets [60, 61, 62], and diverse robotic datasets [7, 32, 51, 63] [64], alongside advances in VLM-based spatial reasoning [65], policy guidance [66, 67], and tactile- and force-aware robot learning [68]. However, existing benchmarks [23, 27] mainly measure task success, leaving preference alignment underexplored despite its importance for home robots, where users may specify not only what task to perform but also how it should be executed. In this work, we introduce LIBERO-PeRM, which evaluates five key personalization topics through preference-injected trajectories, natural-language preference instructions, and ground-truth preference satisfaction matrices. For hierarchical VLAs [69, 70], LIBERO-PeRM further provides subtask decompositions and subgoal-image annotations, covering the key signals needed to evaluate personalized manipulation. 3 The LIBERO-PeRM Benchmark This section introduces the four components of LIBERO-PeRM. Section 3.1 defines what preferences are; Section 3.2 describes the procedural preference data generation pipeline; Section 3.3 defines the five task suites used for benchmarking; and Section 3.4 defines the evaluation methodologies. 3.1 Preference Formulation Preference describes the relative desirability among alternatives [71]. Personalized robotic manipula- tion, therefore, requires a policy to select, among feasible trajectories that complete a requested task, one that also satisfies the user’s preference. A preference is a variable independent of the task goal: it constrains how the same goal is achieved rather than replacing the goal. Natural language is our interface for both variables, but this independence distinguishes the setting from merely redefining a language-conditioned task. We organize preferences by the part of execution they constrain: motion, skill, or subtask-level choices. Specifically, they are: • Motion Preference. This type of preference constrains the physical properties of the trajectory while preserving task completion. For the task “put the mug on the table”, the preference “avoid a particular area of the table” induces a trajectory-level distance constraint, requiring the robot to keep the end-effector and mug away from that region during execution. We denote this kind of preference as Pmotion. • Skill Preference. This type of preference alters the execution behavior of a specific skill c while preserving task completion.
