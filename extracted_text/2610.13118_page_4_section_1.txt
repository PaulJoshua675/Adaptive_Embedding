2.1 Evolution of communication in robots Early models of artificial life gave agents a dedicated signaling channel from the outset. Quinn [18] challenged this practice. A predefined channel presupposes precisely what it aims to explain, and his model showed that coordination behaviors without a dedicated channel can become communicative. Marocco and Nolfi [3] evolved simulated robots that solved a collective navigation task and developed a non-trivial signaling system. In their work, communicative and non-communicative behavior adapted to one another, such that they could not be understood separately. Another line of research explored the conditions that favor the emergence of reliable signals. Floreano et al. [2] evolved foraging robot colonies in an environment containing both food and poison, and found that communication emerged rapidly when individuals were related or when selection operated at the colony level. When the robots' interests conflicted, selection favored the suppression of information [5], and the degree of relatedness influenced the reliability of the signals [19]. Wischmann et al. [6] further demonstrated that populations initiated under identical conditions can develop different signaling strategies due to historical contingency. The theory of the evolution of communication adds a caveat. Sending a signal is pointless if no one responds, and responding is pointless if no one sends a signal. As a result, a population that does not communicate tends to remain that way. Scott-Phillips et al. [20] showed that this state of non-interaction is evolutionarily stable, so communication may not emerge even if it benefits both parties. In this study, we adopted the conditions that the literature identifies as favorable, a homogeneous team with team-level selection, to isolate a distinct factor: the informational value of the signal.
