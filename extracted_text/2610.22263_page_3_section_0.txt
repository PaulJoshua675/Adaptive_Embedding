2 Importance Sampling in Policy Optimization The training objective in (2) involves an expectation over the prompt distribution D and the dis- tribution πθ(· | x) over possible responses. Because D is unknown and the space of responses is prohibitively large to enumerate, it is intractable to compute the expectation exactly. Instead, the expectation is typically replaced by a sample-average approximation using sampled token sequences. A naive application of sample-average approximation to (2) requires these samples to be drawn from πθ. However, modern post-training systems for reinforcement learning typically use separate engines with different design objectives. A training engine, built on PyTorch FSDP (Zhao et al., 2023) or Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019), supports efficient gradient computation and stable updates to θ, whereas an inference engine, e.g., vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023) and SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024), prioritizes generation throughput. Their distinct numerical implementations can yield different token probabilities even with identical policy parameters. This discrepancy is referred to as training– inference mismatch (Liu et al., 2025a). For a given parameter θ, let πθ and ˆπ denote the policies implemented by the training and inference engines, respectively. When samples are drawn from a different policy, importance sampling is a common technique for obtaining an unbiased estimate. It works by weighting the samples using an importance sampling ratio. Common formulations use two types of importance sampling ratios. One is sequence-level importance sampling, which uses πθ(y | x) ˆπ(y | x) = |y|−1 Y t=0 πθ(yt | x, y<t) ˆπ(yt | x, y<t) . The other is token-level importance sampling, which uses the individual ratios πθ(yt | x, y<t) ˆπ(yt | x, y<t) , t = 0, . . . , |y| −1 rather than their product as a whole. As shown below, the two types of importance sampling ratios lead to different estimators, which are unbiased for different quantities. While sampling a response y from πθ must be done sequentially, scoring πθ(y | x) when y is given can be parallelized. Only one forward pass is required to compute all the |y| quantities πθ(y0 | x), πθ(y1 | x, y0), . . . , and this is typically much faster than sampling.
