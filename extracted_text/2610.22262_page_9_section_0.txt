Because LCV and Lpers were always added together, these results describe their combined effect; the study does not separate their individual contributions. Interactions are modest and interpretable. Once fixed-pair supervision is present, the local-maximum loss is largely redundant: their interaction is unfavorable on all four distances, and adding the local-maximum loss to S+G+L+FP lowers dB by only 0.8% while raising both Wasserstein distances by 1.6–1.8%. This is consistent with fixed-pair supervision already constraining the maxima that Lmax targets. The level-set loss interacts favorably with fixed-pair supervision on the Wasserstein distances, which is why G+L+FP is marginally better than the final G+FP on W2,∞and W2,2 (by 0.2– 0.4%) but marginally worse on dB and MT (by 1.2% and 0.3%). Gradient and fixed-pair supervision contribute partly distinct sig- nals. Adding gradient supervision to FP mainly improves the ac- cumulated distances (W2,∞by 5.6%, W2,2 by 6.6%, dB by 3.9%) at a small MT cost (1.1%); adding FP to gradient supervision mainly improves dB (by 15.5%) and MT (by 6.6%). 6.4 Choosing the final objective No configuration is best on every distance (Appendix A): G+FP has the best mean dB, G+L+FP the best W2,∞and W2,2 among the learned configurations, the pretrained GAN the best W2,2 overall, and FP alone the best MT. We use G+FP as the final objective be- cause it gives the best overall balance across the four distances: among all 18 methods it ranks first on dB, second on W2,∞, third on W2,2, and second on MT, whereas G+L+FP ranks 4/1/2/4 and FP alone 5/8/8/1. G+FP lies on the PD–MT Pareto front for every PD distance (Fig. 5), as does FP alone, which remains the better choice when MT is the priority. G+FP is also simpler than the larger fixed-pair configurations: removing the speed and level-set losses from S+G+L+FP slightly improves all four mean distances (by 0.4–1.2%). This choice was made after examining the results on the same 168-field benchmark on which they are reported; with no separate validation split, the benchmark served for both evaluation and con- figuration selection. To limit the resulting optimism, we report all 16 configurations, and the conclusions of Sec. 6.2 hold for each of the five fixed-pair configurations: each lowers all three mean PD distances by 28–35% relative to the control, improves mean MT by 5.6–7.0%, and lowers all three PD distances relative to the CNN on 147–153 of 168 fields. 6.5 Persistence and merge-tree agreement are comple- mentary Mean PD and MT improvements do not imply that the two descrip- tors agree field by field. Of the 150 fields on which the final model lowers all three PD distances relative to the CNN, MT also im- proves on 91 (60.7%) and worsens on 59. Relative to the control, MT improves on 119 of the 156 fields with all-three-PD improve- ment (76.3%). Some of the largest PD gains coincide with MT losses: on Sample 84, for example, the final model lowers dB by 55.2% relative to the CNN while MT distance increases by 8.0%. Correlations across methods and fields show the same structure. Across the 18 method means, dB and MT are strongly correlated (Pearson r = 0.85), whereas W2,∞and W2,2 are essentially uncorre- lated with MT (r = −0.13 and −0.18). The distinction is sharper when competing reconstructions of the same field are compared: across methods within individual fields, the median Pearson cor- relation with MT is 0.70 for dB but approximately zero for W2,∞ and W2,2 (−0.02 and −0.07), and after removing additive field and method effects it is 0.73 for dB versus 0.18 and 0.14. Bottleneck distance and MT thus tend to rank competing reconstructions simi- larly, whereas the accumulated Wasserstein distances often provide different evidence. One plausible explanation is that dB and MT are both sensitive to dominant structural discrepancies, whereas W2,∞ and W2,2 accumulate many smaller pairwise mismatches; we offer this as an interpretation rather than an established mechanism. In either case, PD and MT are complementary descriptors rather than interchangeable measures of one “topological quality.” 6.6 Fidelity and physics/domain behavior Table 4 compares the final model with the pretrained CNN and the control on the non-topology measures. Fine-tuning with the recon- struction loss alone improves direct fidelity substantially but wors- ens spectral, gradient-distribution, and component-structure mea- sures relative to the CNN. The control is best on direct fidelity: PSNR, speed SSIM, speed MAE/RMSE, and WPD MAE. The fi- nal model sits between the CNN and the control on these measures. It gains 1.30 dB PSNR over the CNN, about half of the control’s 2.60 dB gain, and 0.052 in speed SSIM, about 72% of the control’s gain. The final model therefore retains a substantial share of the fidelity benefit of fine-tuning, but not all of it. On structure-sensitive measures, the final model is best of the three: it substantially reduces WPD bias and WPD distribution er- ror, gradient MAE and gradient-distribution error, and component- curve error, and it slightly improves PSD log-L2 relative to the CNN.
