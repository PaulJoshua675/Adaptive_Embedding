Other basins. Banach’s theorem establishes local uniqueness only: with σmax(J) > 1 there is no global Euclidean contraction. For 64 images at ticks 8, 24 and 40, we iterated the frozen map from random states at the typical scale of zt and at three times it, and from other images’ states at the same tick (1152 runs of each kind). All 3456 runs converged, and all reached the fixed point obtained from zt (median relative distance 2 × 10−7). We found no second basin. The lag against the contraction rate. Over 1536 image–tick pairs (128 images), the distance to z⋆ t−1 shrinks across one step by a median factor 0.11 (interquartile range 0.06– 0.22), against ρ(J) = 0.14 (0.08–0.33) at the same fixed points and 0.10 for the linearized map ∥Je∥/∥e∥; per pair, shrink and ρ(J) are correlated (Spearman 0.66). The lag is the contraction at work. The fixed point itself moves by a median 37 per tick, 1.08 times the pre-step distance; this motion, set mainly by the memory (§4), is the part of the trajectory the contraction does not imply. Appendix B. Balanced amplification: the Schur form of the tick map Pseudospectra and a random-matrix null. The object behind the transient stretching is the ε-pseudospectrum of J (Trefethen and Embree, 2005): because J is non-normal, its pseudospectrum reaches well outside the unit disk even though its spectrum sits at radius ρ(J) ≈0.15, which is what allows the one-step Euclidean growth and the lag. Repeated multiplication by a fixed random matrix is on its own enough to organize structured neural codes (Hannagan et al., 2018), so a random matrix is the natural yardstick for an operator applied repeatedly. To ask what produces this non-normality, we compare the measured σmax(J)/ρ(J) (of order 30) against two same-size nulls. A Ginibre matrix (i.i.d. Gaussian entries) gives σmax(J)/ρ(J)≈2 by the circular law, so a generic light-tailed random matrix is far more normal than J. What produces the non-normality. An entry-shuffle of J—which preserves J’s heavy- tailed entry distribution but destroys its learned arrangement—gives a median σmax(J)/ρ(J) of about 13 over 60 image–tick pairs, already six times the Gaussian value; the measured ≈30 adds a further factor of about 2.3 on top. Both ingredients therefore matter: the heavy- tailed entry distribution accounts for most of the gap from a Gaussian matrix, while the specific learned arrangement still contributes a substantial additional amount. Either way J is sharply distinguished from a Gaussian matrix of the same size. Part of the non-normality is architectural—an untrained CTM gives σmax(J)/ρ(J) ≈7—and training multiplies it; training increases it on the small models too, but less (§8).
