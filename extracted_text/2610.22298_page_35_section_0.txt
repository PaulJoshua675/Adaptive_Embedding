Pathological Differential Diagnosis Based on the clinical and sonographic presentation, the primary considerations include: 1. Asymmetric/Prepubertal Gynecomastia: The most common breast pathology in males. The presence of a contralateral breast bud supports an underlying bilateral process with asymmetric clinical presentation. Expected histology would show ductal epithelial hyperplasia with periductal stromal edema and fibrosis, depending on the phase (florid vs. fibrous). 2. Juvenile (Cellular) Fibroadenoma: Rare in prepubertal males but well-described. The discrete, well-circumscribed, oval sonographic appearance is classic for a benign fibroepithelial lesion. Histology would demonstrate a biphasic proliferation of stromal and glandular elements. 3. Secretory Carcinoma (Juvenile Breast Carcinoma): A rare, low-grade malignancy that can occur in children. It must be excluded in any pediatric male breast mass because it can present with deceptively benign, well-circumscribed imaging features. 4. Other rare entities: Pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia (PASH), phyllodes tumor, or benign mesenchymal lesions (e.g., lipoma, granular cell tumor) are less likely but remain in the differential. Resolution of Diagnostic Approach: Tissue Sampling There is a divergence in the preconcilium opinions regarding the necessity of a biopsy. One opinion suggests that the clinical and sonographic findings alone are sufficient to diagnose prepubertal gynecomastia without tissue sampling. However, the safer and more robust pathology consensus is that a progressively enlarging, discrete, unilateral breast mass in a prepubertal male warrants definitive tissue characterization. Relying solely on imaging risks missing rare but clinically significant entities, such as secretory carcinoma or juvenile fibroadenoma. Therefore, tissue diagnosis is recommended. Preliminary Pathologic Impression and Recommendations The imaging features are reassuringly benign, favoring an epithelial- stromal process such as asymmetric gynecomastia or a fibroadenoma. However, a definitive benign diagnosis cannot be rendered without histopathologic examination. Recommendations: • Tissue Acquisition: An excisional biopsy is preferred to evaluate the complete architecture, though an image-guided core needle biopsy is an acceptable alternative.
