Each iteration of Equation (60) is an integer division. The quotient ai of the two current remainders drives both updates: the remainder update subtracts ai copies of one remainder from the other, and the coefficient update adds ai copies of one coefficient to the other. The quotients depend on the input and vary in size, so the circuit must compute each one coherently before it can apply it. It performs each division by binary long division. We call one elementary move of this division a step; a division whose quotient has ℓbits takes 4ℓsteps. The total over an inversion depends on the input and is at most 1,616 for the prime p, so the baseline schedules 1,616 steps. The baseline circuit exploits the fact that the remainders ri shrink as the coefficients ti grow. It stores a remainder and a coefficient from opposite ends of the same register, and stores the succeeding pair in a second such register. We call values that share a register in this way packed. Four short counters record where the four values begin and end. The circuit processes these packed values by scanning across their possible bit positions. At each position, it uses the counters to decide whether that bit belongs to the value currently being processed; we call this check a counter test. If it does, the circuit performs the required one-bit arithmetic; otherwise it does nothing. The fixed circuit must include this test and arithmetic for every position that any input could require, even though a particular input uses only a much smaller range of positions. Each step runs several scans, one each for the remainder update, the coefficient update, and the rearrangement of the packed values. The path that the boundary between a remainder and a coefficient traces during an inversion is the input’s Euclidean walk. Each use of the inversion performs an in-place division or multiplication. For the slope, a forward pass replaces w by its inverse, one multiplication forms the quotient in an accumulator, an X-basis measurement erases the old numerator, and the reverse pass restores w. The second inversion runs the same sequence in the opposite order to erase the slope. Every step consists of in-place reversible operations on the packed registers and counters, so the reverse pass is the inverse circuit and needs no stored history. By contrast, the divstep circuit must recover a decision at every step. The discovered circuit keeps the baseline’s packed register layout and reduces the cost of its schedule mainly in three ways. First, it omits scan positions that inputs rarely use. Second, it removes selected counter tests. Third, it recompiles the scans of a step together so that they share intermediate results. The first two changes fail on rare inputs, which count against the 2 × 10−4 failure budget per point addition. The third is exact. Fitted windows and the schedule cut. The baseline circuit includes arithmetic at every bit position required by its worst-case bounds. Most inputs occupy a much narrower range at each step. The specialized schedule therefore replaces each worst-case range with a fitted window: a range chosen separately for each step and fitted to the positions that sampled walks use. The same fit shortens the schedule from the worst-case 1,616 steps to 1,432 steps; we call this shortening the schedule cut.
