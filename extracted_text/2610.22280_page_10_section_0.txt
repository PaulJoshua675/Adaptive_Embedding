We further examine the tasks on which post-handoff mon- itoring fires in Table 5. Shadow observer triggers concentrate on particularly difficult trajectories: always-on Opus succeeds on only 51.6% of triggered tasks, compared with 74.5% of escalated tasks without a trigger. On the triggered subset, the Shadow-enabled policy achieves 64.5% success, com- pared with 48.4% without Shadow. These suggest that the Shadow observer identifies cases where difficulty persists even after escalation, as we expected. 4.5. Sensitivity Analysis Which tasks to escalate. We first examine whether uncertainty helps identify which tasks should receive strong-model assistance. Figure 4 varies the escalation budget and compares LEASH with random task-level routing at matched escalation rates. The random baseline is evaluated at the same operating points, shown by the hollow markers; its near-linear trend follows from uniformly allocating a fixed fraction of tasks to the strong agent. Across varying escalation rates, LEASH consistently achieves higher task success, indicating that its gains come from selectively allocating strong-model computation to more informative tasks rather than simply increasing strong-model usage. When to escalate. We next examine whether the timing of handoff matters once a task is likely to require escalation. We focus on an escalation-prone subset consisting of the tasks most frequently escalated by LEASH and compare LEASH with a shuffled-timing baseline. As shown in Figure 5, LEASH achieves 64.6% task success at $0.67 per task, compared with 60.4% at $0.79 under shuffled timing. This 4.2-point improvement, together with 15% lower cost, suggests that effective weak-to-strong escalation depends not only on selecting the right tasks, but also on transferring control at the right point during execution. Reference bank size. We examine the effect of reference bank size on initial-stage uncertainty estimation.
