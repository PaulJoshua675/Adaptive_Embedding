We argue that papers evaluating the performance of nearest neighbor dependent systems need to document and evaluate these factors carefully, or give a justified reason why only a subset matters for their use case. We support that argument by developing RUSTKNN as an example, re-evaluating the methods behind the seminal cover tree data structure—a structure with multiple proposed vari- ants and one of the few with both empirical success and theoretical grounding for reducing nearest neighbor queries. We re-implement a cache-efficient cover tree with three query settings (single, batch, and dual-tree). Working in a compiled language allows both effective runtime evaluation and easy isolation of six micro-optimizations used in the literature (five of which we ablate), along with poorly documented nuances of the cover tree “base” parameter, which to our knowledge has never been independently tested. Our contributions are: 1. Optimization ablation. We quantify the individual and cumulative impact of five micro- optimizations via feature-flag ablation, revealing that early-exit distance computation is the only one with a consistent effect, and that its benefit grows with dimension (none on corel, d=32; 1.31× on covtype, d=54; and 1.49× on mnist, d=784), while the traversal-level optimizations sit within about 10% of 1.0× (Section 4.2). 2. Query mode × k interaction. We show that the optimal query strategy depends on both k and the dataset: single-tree search is fastest at k=1 on mnist yet 3.3× slower than dual-tree search at k=10 on that same dataset, and choosing the wrong query mode costs up to 7.5× in runtime (Section 5.2). 3. Base parameter analysis. We show that the conventional base 1.3 is not a safe default for dual-tree search: the optimum is 2.0 on four of seven datasets at k=1 (five at k=10) and 3.0 on the two smallest, and the default is 1.6× slower than the optimum on covtype, the largest dataset (Section 5.3). 4. All-NN vs. held-out divergence. We demonstrate that the standard all-nearest-neighbor benchmark produces different implementation rankings than held-out evaluation, with im- plications for how the community evaluates nearest neighbor methods (Section 5.4). In doing so, we find that no single method dominates; the results are dependent on dataset and query settings, and thus far more complicated and involved than the literature has previously implied. This complexity exists within just the cover tree literature alone, and we find it instructive to the questions we may have of the broader literature.
