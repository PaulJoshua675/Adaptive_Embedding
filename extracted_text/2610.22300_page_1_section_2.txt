Imagine that the pretraining text describes Mary as having grown up in France, attending French schools, writing books in French and working in French-speaking institutions, but never states her native language. A language model should still assume that Mary is a native speaker of French. We call this statistical generalization of facts. The model infers a new fact from facts it knows, by a statistical rule that the pretraining text rarely states explicitly and that is not always true. It differs from entailment, where the new fact follows necessarily from the known ones, and from paraphrase, where the same fact is merely worded differently (§2). Statistical generalization is a large part of what makes language models useful beyond recalling what they were told. Prior work has asked whether models draw such inferences from facts they are trained on (Feng et al., 2025; Treutlein et al., 2024), but, to our knowledge, no prior work has asked which of a model’s facts support them. Not all facts a model knows support such generalization equally well. We distinguish active facts, which support generalization beyond their own recall, from inert facts, which the model can recall but which do not support such generalization in a given task.
