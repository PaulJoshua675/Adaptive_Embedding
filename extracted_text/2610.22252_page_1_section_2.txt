Contextual bandits provide a framework for sequential decision-making in applications such as recommenda- tion systems [24, 25], medicine [5, 7, 32], finance [31, 42], and operations research [4, 16]. Linear reward models are particularly useful because they allow information to be shared across contexts and actions through a fea- ture representation. A variety of learning approaches have been developed, including optimistic algorithms for linear bandits [1] and methods using neural representations [8, 39, 41]. Different training procedures, model architectures, and hyperparameter choices can produce many candidate policies. However, it is often unclear which of these policies will perform best in the population where it will be deployed. Consider a clinician choosing among several pretrained models that recommend treatments from patient measurements. Querying these models on existing patient records may be inexpensive, whereas evaluating their recommendations requires administering treatments and observing outcomes. A similar question arises when a recommendation platform enters a new market: a policy trained for one population may perform differently in another, and several candidate policies may need to be evaluated on the new population. In both examples, the goal is to assess a collection of fixed policies while limiting the cost of collecting reward observations. Estimating each policy’s expected reward to a prescribed accuracy supports decisions such as selecting a near-optimal policy or identifying policies whose values exceed a desired threshold. A direct approach evaluates each candidate separately. However, this requires a separate reward budget for every policy, even when their decisions overlap or their feature representations allow observations to be shared.
