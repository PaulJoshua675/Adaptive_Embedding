Large language model (LLM) agents operating over ex- tended time horizons require persistent external memory to accumulate knowledge beyond the ephemeral context win- dow (Sumers et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2026). As these systems grow more complex, with over a dozen distinct architectures published since 2023 (Latimer et al. 2026; Packer et al. 2024; Rasmussen et al. 2025; Chhikara et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2026), the question of how to evaluate them becomes pressing. A survey of current benchmarks reveals a systematic lim- itation: every existing evaluation measures memory quality extrinsically, by posing questions to the agent and grading its responses (Maharana et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2026; Hu, Wang, and McAuley 2026; Uddin et al. 2026; Liu and Yadav 2026; 1https://github.com/amazon-science/feb-30-benchmark ∗Corresponding author. Jung et al. 2026). This produces a single composite score that conflates memory, retrieval, and generation quality. When the system fails, one cannot identify which component is respon- sible; when it succeeds, one cannot confirm the memory is actually correct (Toma and Matei 2026). We identify two concrete failure modes that extrinsic eval- uation cannot detect: • False positives (LLM compensation): A strong gener- ation model can produce correct responses despite in- correct or incomplete memory state, by reasoning over partial retrieval results at inference time. Extrinsic evalu- ation scores this as success. • False negatives (retrieval bottleneck): A memory sys- tem may correctly store and update facts, yet fail to re- trieve them for a specific query due to embedding mis- match or retrieval cutoff. Extrinsic evaluation blames the memory system for a retrieval failure. We propose intrinsic evaluation, direct comparison of the memory store contents against known ground truth at each stage of knowledge evolution, as a complementary evaluation paradigm. The relationship mirrors unit versus end-to-end testing in software engineering: intrinsic evaluation checks the memory component’s internal state directly, while ex- trinsic evaluation only observes end-to-end behavior, so a passing end-to-end test can mask a broken component just as a correct component can fail end-to-end due to an integration bottleneck. A parallel from NLP history motivates the same shift: in the 2010s, word embeddings were evaluated only through downstream task performance until intrinsic evalu- ation (analogy tasks, similarity correlations) enabled faster progress by isolating representation quality (Baroni, Dinu, and Kruszewski 2014; Schnabel et al. 2015).
