We first simplify our selection problem by playing against a fixed unknown opponent, to try and infer strategic learning signals against a stationary fixed LLM. Table 3 indicates that not all traditional methods transfer equally to the strategic selection setup, with the LLM Selector scoring the lowest overall. Upon closer inspection, we find that it also falls behind nearly every fixed model across most games (Appendix D.3). The Causal LLM Router and RouterDC show relative improvement but are still beaten by several fixed models in multiple games. We attribute this to the fact that strategic settings require special treatment of the game context and the learning signal. Methods that rely on query embeddings for semantic similarity matching (Causal LLM Router, RouterDC), or on LLM reflection (LLM Selector), fail to surface implicit strategic signals. A simple bandit algorithm using Thompson sampling achieves the highest net gain among all selectors in every game, despite relying on nothing more than per-round outcome feedback. However, even in Cournot Duopoly, where the Thompson sampler proves most dominant, it fails to beat the best fixed model (Llama3.1 8B), indicating significant room for improvement. We believe a better selector algorithm should learn to directly infer the latent, hidden per-game strategic signal, rather than relying solely on observed game outcomes. An Interactive Learning Problem. In practice, opponents can also use a selector that adapts its policy each round based on the observed game outcome. To capture this setting, we extend our evaluations and play against adaptive selector opponents. Table 4 reveals that multi-armed bandit algorithms remain dominant. However, the non-stationary adaptations (Sliding-Window TS and Discounted TS) fail to exploit the underlying concept drift in adaptive settings to outclass the plain TS. Similarly, an adversarial variant (EXP3) performs noticeably worse, on par with embedding-based methods. Hence, we note that simple multi-armed bandit adaptations are not sufficient to capture the implicit learning signals shaped by repeated strategic interaction. 5 Lessons Learned 5.1 How to Learn from Strategic Context?
