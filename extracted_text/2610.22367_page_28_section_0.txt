Making this reduction reversible is difficult because its control flow is input-dependent: which line of Equation (55) applies changes from one step to the next, and the reduced pair does not in general reveal which line produced it. A quantum circuit therefore cannot simply run the reduction backward to restore its input and clear its workspace; it must preserve enough information to identify each step. The prior constructions do so using the dialog introduced by Khattar et al. [13]. The direct approach would update the coefficients that become α and β alongside the two values throughout the reduction. The dialog avoids holding these growing coefficients in memory. Its first pass updates only the pair being reduced and records a compact symbol identifying each subtraction, swap, and halving choice. A second pass replays the same transformations to construct the required coefficient. Initializing this replay from the numerator a = Qy −Py produces aβ = aw−1 mod p directly, without ever storing w−1 in a separate register. The simple binary updates and the replay trick reduce the gate count, but the complete dialog record requires hundreds of additional qubits. Divsteps Rather than reorganize the binary-gcd constructions above, the search finds a new reversible prime-field inversion architecture built on divsteps [15, 53]. In the divstep recurrence of Equation (6), a small signed counter z, called the half delta, replaces the repeated magnitude comparisons in Equation (55): the sign of z and the parity of one value alone determine the next update. We call the choice of update at a step its decision. Appendix A proves that every decision can be computed exactly from the registers as they stand after the step. When a decision is cheap to decode, the circuit decodes it from the updated state. Otherwise, the circuit stores the decision as a single bit on a fixed-size history stack. We call the sequence of divsteps for one input a walk. The point adder uses this inversion twice, once to form the slope λ and once to erase it after the coordinate update. Each use runs a forward walk and a reverse walk, for four walks in total. Unlike the quotient-based extended Euclidean algorithm (EEA) of SI II.2, which divides one remainder by the other at every iteration, a divstep never computes a multi-bit quotient or adds a multiple of one full register to another. A divstep walk needs more steps than the quotient-based EEA needs divisions, but each step is much cheaper. Reversible divsteps were previously developed over binary fields and polynomial rings [13, 54]. There, each step shrinks the polynomial pair by one coefficient, and the freed space can hold that step’s record. Integer divsteps free no such fixed space, so a stored history costs additional qubits. To our knowledge, this is the first reversible divstep inversion circuit over a prime field. We recall the notation of the main text. The walk state is (z, f, g, fv, gv), and each step applies one of the three updates in Equation (6). Two bits label the update: s records whether the step swaps the roles of f and g, and b records whether the incoming g is odd. To run a step backward, the circuit must know s and b, and the updated state does not hold them explicitly. Cheap tests on a few bits of the updated state recover s at every step and b at about two thirds of the steps. We call the remaining decisions ambiguous. Each ambiguous decision has a depth j, the number of trailing zero bits of the updated coefficient f ′ v, and we write f ′ v = 2jq with q odd. A swap followed by a run of ambiguous decisions is an excursion, and the depth counts the steps of the excursion so far, with the swap as step 1. The cost of a depth-j decoder grows with j. The cutoff jstore is the depth from which the circuit stops decoding: it decodes an ambiguous decision at depth j < jstore and stores one at depth j ≥jstore. The cutoff jstore trades qubits for Toffolis: a higher cutoff decodes more and stores less. At jstore = 0, the circuit uses only the cheap tests and stores every remaining decision, requiring 198 history qubits for the 533-step schedule and 1,065 qubits in total. The discovered circuit chooses the intermediate cutoff jstore = 5: it decodes depths two through four with the rules given below. Storing only the deeper decisions shrinks the history stack from 198 to 60 qubits. At the low-qubit extreme, jstore = 9 decodes through j = 8 and reserves 24 history qubits. Varying the cutoff and the workspace produces a family of circuits that spans from 3.6 million Toffolis and 1,031 qubits to 7.9 million Toffolis and 893 qubits; an additional higher-qubit configuration uses 3.3 million Toffolis and 1,191 qubits.
