Figure 6: Operational AI forecasts differ from HRES-fc0 in storm intensity, not in how wind follows pressure (2025). Each source contributes minimum pressure and maximum wind speed at timesteps matched to IBTrACS observations, pooled over all lead times. (a) Contours of the pooled joint distributions, with marginal densities. (b) The same points ranked within each source and converted to standard normal scores, so all marginals are identical and the contours show only the dependence. FGN and GenCast have weaker winds and shallower pressures than their fine-tuning reference, HRES-fc0, with marginals close to ERA5’s (a), but every gridded source has nearly the same dependence (b). Dashed and solid contours enclose about 70% and 30% of each source’s points; IBTrACS’s sustained winds differ in definition from the others’ gridded instantaneous winds. Physical relationships in learned forecasts. Recent work studying cyclone dynamics and thermo- dynamics has examined AI-based weather models through the lens of cyclone physics: Sahu et al. (2025) find realistic spatial patterns but weak intensity while Ba˜no-Medina et al. (2025) assess the sensitivity of learned and numerical models to initial conditions for extratropical cyclone Xynthia. Our joint wind–pressure analysis complements these studies by providing a distributional diagnostic aggregated across storms and ensemble members, using reanalysis, operational analyses, and obser- vations to identify which physical relationships are preserved and which biases persist. However, agreement with a known physical relationship does not establish the underlying learned mechanism, just as resemblance to pretraining data does not imply a causal effect of the training data. 7
