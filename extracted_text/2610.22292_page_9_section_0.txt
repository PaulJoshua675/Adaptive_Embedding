Preprint 6 DISCUSSION CATCH improves honesty on all three evaluations implemented, where dishonesty manifests due to different causes, outperforming all the baselines. On MASK, SFT on the honest targets reaches only 55% and 60% honesty on Llama and Qwen; adding the dishonest targets (SFT-TA) raises this to 77% and 88%. Prompting with honest persona makes both models achieve similar level of honesty to SFT-TA. However, CATCH is still the most honest on MASK, and this honesty transfers to other benchmarks, while all prompting and SFT baselines do not generalize well to Among Us. The closest competitor to CATCH is StTP (Herbster et al., 2026), the strongest activation-steering method to date for honesty. CATCH outperforms it on MASK, and the two are comparable on Among Us and AuditBench. They also differ in terms of capability degradation. At the steering strength behind its results, StTP lowers the AlpacaEval LC win rate to 8.6% on Llama and 45.2% on Qwen, whereas CATCH stays at parity on both (51.3% and 51.8%). Using a weaker steering strength would lead to less utility degradation, but also to less honesty (Herbster et al., 2026). Our findings differ from that of Wang et al. (2025), who find prompting and fine-tuning to be the most effective mitigations. In our setting, using 200 adversarial targets to train two open-weight models with CATCH gives better MASK performance than in Wang et al. (2025)’s setting, where roughly fifty times as much training data was used to SFT a bigger and more capable model (Claude Sonnet 3.7). We cannot test CATCH on closed-source model for a more direct comparison. We limit our claim to the small-data regime: with a few hundred targets, CATCH is more effective than SFT. Preserving capability during robustness training depends heavily on the choice of utility dataset. Prior work in adversarial training commonly used UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) for this purpose (Bai et al., 2024; Xhonneux et al., 2024; Sheshadri et al., 2025; D´ek´any et al., 2025). With it, Xhonneux et al. (2024) report MT-Bench degradation for every model they train, attributing it to the trade-off between robustness and utility. However, our ablation indicates that much of the cost comes from the choice of utility dataset. Using on-policy utility data in CATCH retains capability for both our trained models to the same level as the base model in AlpacaEval, MMLU and MT-Bench. We also tried CAPO, another adversarial training method, which successfully improves honesty on MASK and Among Us, but is accompanied with degraded capabilities. It also produces behavioural changes: in Among Us, CAPO-trained Impostors decline to play in 35% of games, giving reasons about harm (“I can’t help with that because it involves harming others.”). One possible explanation is that preference optimization can shift probability onto responses the preference pairs (toward and away targets) never specified (Razin et al., 2025). Another reading is that honesty and harmlessness have overlapping representations: Siu et al. (2026) shows that interventions targeting one safety behaviour can affect others. Some limitations exist in our work. CATCH requires white-box access, so it cannot be applied to closed-source APIs. We train LoRA adapters on all linear layers, but prior work on honesty steering finds that the middle layers carry most of the relevant signal (Herbster et al., 2026), so restricting the update would make full fine-tuning affordable and potentially leading to even higher honesty; however, we have not tested this. A possible next step could be to probe model activations to study how CATCH changes the internal representation of deception. Finally, future work could also evaluate CATCH’s robustness to emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2026), for example, by evaluating whether CATCH trained on the base model can still improve honesty on a broadly misaligned model. 7
