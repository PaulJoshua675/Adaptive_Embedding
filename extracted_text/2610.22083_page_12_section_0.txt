Summary Cliff severity separates Binary from everything else, but no further: all seven remaining encodings are equally clean by that measure. Global monotonicity and neighbourhood quality then separate those seven, and they do so in the same direction: Gray’s Hamming balls are as fragmented as Binary’s despite its perfect adjacency. Removing the Ham- ming cliff is therefore necessary for the clustering behaviour sought here, but it is not sufficient. 4.2. Two Trained Values The network was trained on the anchor together with a second value 𝑣2, and tested exhaustively over all 256 values. Fourteen second values were used, listed in Table 8, chosen from the binary form of the anchor, 24010 = 111100002. The first four, 241, 242, 244 and 248, set one of its four zero bits each, so they lie one bit from the anchor in Binary but 1, 2, 4 and 8 units from it in value. The next four, 239, 238, 236 and 232, lie at the same semantic separations below the anchor, where Binary places them up to five bits away; the two groups therefore compare equal semantic distances under very different binary Hamming distances. Five further values, 224, 192, 128, 64 and 0, extend the separation across the rest of the range, and 255 tests the top of the range. Table 8 also shows why the sweep is informative: the same pair of values can be one bit apart under one encoding and many bits apart under another. Under Thermometer encoding the Hamming distance is the semantic separation exactly, and under the hybrids it is that separation scaled by about 1∕𝐵, with a ripple of up to log2 𝐵bits from the fine field that dominates only at small separations. Johnson encoding gives its circular equivalent, so that the value 0, which lies 240 steps from the anchor, is only 16 bits away. Gray encoding, by contrast, stays between one and four bits across the whole range, which is the 𝜌𝑠= 0.374 of Section 4.1 in concrete form. If the network accepted precisely the code words lying between the two trained ones, their number would be the Hamming distance between the two trained code words minus one, 𝑓𝑝= 𝐻−1. Johnson meets this exactly for all fourteen pairs, and Thermometer and the hybrids for eight to ten, exceeding it by one or two in the rest. For Thermometer trained on 240 and 0, the 239 accepted values are exactly the integers between them, consistent with the distance of 240 recorded in Table 8. They are neighbouring activations rather than errors: the network has generalised over the interval its two trained values span. Binary does not obey the relation. At 𝑣2 = 239, a Hamming distance of 5, it produces 30 false positives, an excess of 26; and since 239 and 240 are adjacent, no value lies between them in the range, so none of the 30 is a neighbouring activation. This inverts the apparent ranking by accuracy. Ther- mometer records the lowest mean accuracy over the fourteen pairs (0.824) precisely because it generalises over the largest contiguous intervals, while Binary’s higher figure (0.974) conceals scattered errors. Accuracy is therefore a misleading summary here, and the relation between false positives and Hamming distance is the more informative quantity. 4.2.1. A systematic effect of bit count Under Binary, the required scaling factor varies slightly between second values at equal Hamming distance, and the variation is not noise. It depends on the number of 1-bits in the second code word and not on their position. At 𝐻= 1 the second value differs from the anchor 24010 = 111100002 in a single bit. The four values that do so by setting one of its zero bits, 241, 242, 244 and 248, each have five set bits, and all require exactly 2.96051, although the flipped bit occupies a different position in each. The remaining one- bit neighbour, 224, clears a set bit instead and so has three, and it requires 2.95312 (the search records the factor to five decimals; the tables report it rounded up to two). At 𝐻= 4 the factor rises monotonically with bit count, from 5.13955 for 0 (no set bits) through 5.18480 for 238 (six) to 5.19773 for 255 (eight). Within any Hamming-distance group the spread is at most 1.1%. This follows from the dual-rail training protocol. Bits equal to 1 and bits equal to 0 are presented at different times relative to the teacher spike (Section 3.1), so their synapses are potentiated at different points on the STDP curve and need not acquire identical weights. A code word with more set bits therefore draws proportionally more of its drive from one rail and requires marginally more gain to reach threshold. The effect is an order of magnitude smaller than the differences between encodings reported here and does not affect any comparison, but it accounts for the residual variation within a Hamming-distance group, and it reappears in Section 4.4 where the tolerance threshold falls exactly on a boundary between Hamming distances. 4.3. Robustness as the Trained Set Grows A contiguous cluster of values around 240 was trained, growing from one to eight members, reproducing and ex- tending the progression of Davies et al. (2025). Tables 10 and 11 report the results, and Table A.3 the complete metrics. Binary degrades as that work predicts, and no other encoding degrades in the same way: the few untrained values the other encodings accept lie immediately beside the trained cluster. From two trained values onward Binary produces between 24 and 46 false positives, while Gray, Johnson and three of the four hybrids accept no un- trained value at any trained-set size.
