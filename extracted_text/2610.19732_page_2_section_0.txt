Another line of work consist in trying to derive an analytical, closed-form expression for the spiking time, as it can be done for some specific models (Mostafa, 2017; Comsa et al., 2020; G¨oltz et al., 2021). These approaches substantially reduce average latency and inference cost, but they remain fundamentally empirical: latency is shaped indirectly through training incentives rather than derived directly from the model’s equations, and no a priori bound on worst-case response time can be established without exhaustive testing. To our knowledge, no existing spiking model allows a target latency, or a provable latency bound, to be specified or verified analytically at design time. Moreover, the vast majority of TTFS-based models are trained and tested on static datasets such as MNIST (Le Cun et al., 1998) or CIFAR (Krizhevsky et al., 2009), which seems contradictory with the foundation hypothesis of TTFS, which is the need to treat the temporal information (Mostafa, 2017; Rueckauer and Liu, 2018; Zhang et al., 2019; Chu et al., 2020; Comsa et al., 2020; Kheradpisheh and Masquelier, 2020; Oh et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020; Park and Yoon, 2021; G¨oltz et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021; Im et al., 2022; Nomura et al., 2022; Oh et al., 2022; Lew et al., 2022; Sakemi et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Pes et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024, 2025b; Che et al., 2026; Mirsadeghi et al., 2026). In Bacho and Chu (2023), the authors relax the one spike only constraint of G¨oltz et al. (2021), and apply it to one temporal dataset. However, they study the latency only on neuromorphic MNIST (Orchard et al., 2015), which do not contain temporal information. On a parallel track of research, classic ANNs are trained on static datasets and then converted into an SNN, usually with non-leaky Integrate and Fire neurons (Han et al., 2020; Chowdhury et al., 2021; Garg et al., 2021; Hwang et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2021; Bu et al., 2022; Chowdhury et al., 2022; Datta and Beerel, 2022; Lew et al., 2022; Bu et al., 2023; Ho and Chang, 2023; Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023; Aydin et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025; Ge et al., 2026; Jiang et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These models have reached impressive results in terms of latencies, and were able to compress the time dimension to as few as one timestep only (Chowdhury et al., 2021). However, it is unlikely that these techniques can be applied to datasets built with event-based sensors. Moreover, they introduce an artificial temporal dimension on datas that do not initially contain one (static images). Hence, we cannot conclude that these models actually exploit temporal information. We study here the behaviour of Delay Neural Networks (Gattepaille and Muzy, 2025) in real time, i.e. a spiking neuron and network model whose spike-timing dynamics are constructed so that latency can be expressed and con- trolled analytically. Rather than treating latency as an emergent, post hoc measurable quantity, we derive a closed-form relationship between the synaptic parameters and the resulting spike time, allowing the designer or the learning algo- rithm to (i) predict the exact, or provably bounded, latency of any neuron or decision layer prior to deployment, (ii) enforce a target latency budget by construction rather than through indirect training penalties, and (iii) certify worst- case response time without exhaustive simulation across the input space. This shift from empirical latency measurement to analytical latency guarantee is, to our knowledge, the first of its kind for spiking models in the framework of TTFS applied to event-based datasets. Our contributions are threefold: (1) we formalize the Delay Neural Network (DeNN) in real time as a spiking neuron model whose spike time is analytically derivable, and where applicable invertible, as a function of its input and parameters; (2) we derive formal upper and lower bounds on network latency and show how these bounds can be directly incorporated after training; and (3) we validate the model empirically on a subset of the Google Speech Commands dataset (Warden, 2018), showing that promising accuracy can be achieved while respecting an analytically guaranteed latency. 2
