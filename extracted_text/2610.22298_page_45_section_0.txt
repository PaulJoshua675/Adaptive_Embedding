What would refute: histology showing only usual ductal hyperplasia without a clonal stromal proliferation. Low probability but must be excluded: malignant breast neoplasm (for example, secretory carcinoma, ductal carcinoma, or other rare pediatric breast malignancy). Arguments for: persistence and progressive enlargement of a unilateral, firm, discrete mass. Arguments against: classic sonographic benign features (smooth margins, parallel orientation, no calcifications, no hypervascularity, no spiculation) and the absence of any suspicious clinical findings such as skin retraction, nipple discharge, fixation, or lymphadenopathy. Because this category, even at low probability, can be life-threatening, it must be actively excluded by histologic diagnosis before the mass is accepted as benign. Low probability: estrogen- or hCG-secreting tumor (testicular, adrenal, or germ cell). Arguments for include gynecomastia and adrenarche. Arguments against include prepubertal testes (3 mL) without masses, prepubertal phallus, normal bone age, and absence of other feminizing signs. Confirmation would be elevated estradiol or hCG with imaging-identified tumor; refutation would be normal hormone levels and negative imaging. Low probability: drug-induced gynecomastia from topical triamcinolone. Arguments for include documented exposure. Arguments against include low systemic absorption with topical use and absence of cushingoid features. Confirmation would be regression after discontinuation; refutation is persistence after cessation. The potentially dangerous conditions of malignancy and hormonally active tumors must be excluded first through histopathology and hormone studies. The second action is a focused surgical examination under anesthesia at the time of planned excision to confirm that the mass is indeed discrete, mobile, retroareolar, and not fixed to pectoralis fascia or skin, and to inspect for any clinically occult contralateral lesion. The third action is complete excisional biopsy of the right retroareolar mass through a periareolar or circumareolar incision, with the specimen sent for histopathology in fresh, oriented, and inked form. The expected result is definitive histologic diagnosis distinguishing usual ductal hyperplasia from a fibroepithelial or other neoplasm, and this result will determine all subsequent management. If histology returns as usual ductal hyperplasia/stromal fibrosis consistent with gynecomastia, no further surgical treatment is needed and the family is counseled that recurrence on the contralateral side is possible during puberty. If histology returns as a fibroepithelial neoplasm (for example juvenile fibroadenoma), complete excision is considered adequate therapy provided margins are uninvolved; the contralateral left 'breast bud' is observed. If histology is malignant, the patient is referred immediately to pediatric oncology for staging (chest imaging, breast MRI if indicated, axillary ultrasound, and systemic work-up) and for multidisciplinary surgical-oncologic planning. Treatment: The principal treatment proposed at this preconcilium stage is right periareolar excisional biopsy of the retroareolar mass as a combined diagnostic and therapeutic maneuver, performed under general anesthesia, with the patient supine, the arm on the side of the lesion abducted, and a small inferior or lateral periareolar incision planned to preserve nipple-areolar viability and to allow access to the retroareolar disc. The rationale is that the lesion is unilateral, persistent for 18 months, enlarging, >2 cm clinically, and sonographically solid, and therefore exceeds the threshold for simple observation of prepubertal gynecomastia. The goal of the operation is complete excision of the mass with a small margin of surrounding breast tissue, hemostasis, and preservation of the nipple-areolar complex; criteria for surgical readiness are normal coagulation, normal preoperative endocrine panel (or endocrinology clearance if any value is abnormal), and signed informed consent including discussion of risks of hematoma, seroma, infection, nipple-areolar compromise, scarring, recurrence, and the possibility of finding an unexpected neoplasm. Postoperative care includes a soft dressing, analgesia, activity restriction for 1–2 weeks, and wound review at 1–2 weeks with pathology results; no hormonal therapy is initiated. Effectiveness and safety monitoring is by histopathology of the excised specimen (definitive diagnostic endpoint) and by clinical wound review at 1–2 weeks and again at 3 months to detect recurrence, contour deformity, or contralateral changes.
