Coverage can continue to improve while selected accuracy changes little, as on Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D, indicating that candidate availability and judge selection place different limits on the final result. How coverage translates into selected accuracy. Because a correct selected response must belong to a pool containing a correct response, selected accuracy divided by oracle coverage measures the fraction of covered questions on which selection succeeds. We compute this descriptive ratio from the reported three-seed means; with equal evaluation- set sizes, it corresponds to pooling question–seed outcomes, rather than averaging per-seed ratios. At N = 9 on Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D, the ratio is 37.4/73.7 ≈50.7% for EMO and 32.2/60.3 ≈53.4% for BoN. EMO’s accuracy advantage therefore coexists with a lower fraction of available successes realized by selection: its greater coverage is essential to the observed advantage. On Llama-3.1-8B/MATH-500, both coverage (73.4 vs. 71.9) and this fraction (89.4% vs. 84.6%) favor EMO. The same pattern appears on Llama-3.2-3B/MATH- 500 (Figure 2b): EMO’s coverage is 70.6 versus BoN’s 66.5, and its selected-to-covered fraction is 60.3/70.6 ≈85.4% versus 52.7/66.5 ≈79.2%. These are descriptive relationships, not isolated causal effects of ranking: candidate pools and the questions they cover differ across methods. They show why a larger absolute oracle–accuracy gap need not indicate less useful generation, and why selection quality alone cannot explain all accuracy gains. Repeated sampling and feedback address different limitations. At N = 9 on Llama- 3.1-8B/MMLU-Pro, SC and BoN share 76.4 oracle coverage but achieve 51.4 and 62.5 accuracy, respectively. Thus many available correct responses are not recovered by majority voting, while judge selection realizes more of the same pool’s potential. EMO further raises coverage to 83.2 and accuracy to 68.2 with only five candidates, showing additional value from changing how subsequent attempts are generated.
