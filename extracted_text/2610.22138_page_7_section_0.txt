We ask each agent three things: its own answer, the same with its own mes- sages anonymized, and what the group concluded. The answers barely move (Table 5). Asked for their own view, agents keep 86.4% of their false answers and return to the truth in only 9.1%; final infection falls from 58.8% to 53.1%, still a major- ity. Anonymizing their own messages leaves the result unchanged, so the effect is not mere self- consistency. The two questions do come apart: in 13.7% of cases an agent says the group settled on the false answer while personally holding the true one. The reverse is rare: only 7.6% of agents who personally hold the false answer say the group did not settle on it. False private answers thus reflect conformity to the consensus an agent perceives, rather than merely summarizing the chat. Models differ in how firmly they hold the false answer: asking for their own view lowers final in- fection substantially for GEMMA (69.8%→54.8%) and GEMINI (67.4%→57.4%), but hardly for GPT-OSS-120B (74.0%→71.2%) or MISTRAL (66.4%→66.9%). 4.4 Knowledge does not predict robustness On COUNTERFACT, HY3 and GPT-OSS-120B know the same share of facts before any discussion (85%). Under three attackers, however, GPT-OSS- 120B ends with the false answer on 80% of the facts it knew, HY3 on only 25% (Fig. 4); the two most knowledgeable models thus sit at opposite ex- tremes. Robustness instead appears to be a distinct, model-specific property. The results are inconclu- sive about model size: parameter counts are undis- closed for three of our models, and GPT-OSS- 120B is a mixture-of-experts model with far fewer active parameters. With only six heterogeneous models, this is an existence result. Social cascade. Holding the number of attackers fixed, an uninfected agent’s probability of switch- ing in a round rises with the number of benign peers already converted, from 9% (none) to 66% (five or more), and in every model it rises monotonically up to four converted peers. The adversarial agents start the spread, but benign agents then pass it on. Self-internalization. Consider agents that hold the true answer and see the claim in a round. If they correct it in public, only 2.3% hold the false answer after the round; if they endorse it, 39.7% do, and staying silent or hedging falls in between. Endorsing is followed by more switching than cor- recting in all six models. Part of this gap may reflect timing: an agent can be persuaded by earlier speakers, switch, and only then endorse. Among endorsing agents, 45.5% hold the false answer af- ter the round when all three attackers spoke before them, compared with 24.0% when none did. To make sure the public concession comes first, we examine first speakers, who speak before anyone else in the round, so any switch happens after their turn.
