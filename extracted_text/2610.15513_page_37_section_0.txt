Third, the model’s chain-of-thought reasoning reveals explicit engagement with the harmful task rather than reflexive refusal, illustrating that REPRO’s discovered attacks genuinely bypass safety mechanisms. 5 Discussion We organize the discussion around six key findings from the experiments, followed by limitations, implications for software engineering practice, threats to validity, and ethical considerations. 5.1 Key Findings Algorithmic structure does substantial work. REPRO reaches comparable attack-discovery effec- tiveness with a compact 4-bit Mistral-7B reflective model as with a 4-bit 24B Mistral-3.1-Small, suggesting that the algorithmic scaffolding (Bayesian strategy selection, structured failure analysis, semantic crossover) contributes substantially to effectiveness independent of reflective-model scale. We do not claim that an arbitrarily weak reflective model would suffice; the comparison covers only 7B and 24B instruction-tuned Mistral variants, and probing the lower bound of reflective-model capability is an open question. Black-box can match gray-box ASR at lower query cost. Under the Qwen3 judge, REPRO matches the 100% ASR reported for gray-box methods [4] with a median query cost two orders of magnitude lower and no logprob access. This is evidence that privileged access is not strictly required to drive ASR to the ceiling on our evaluation set under our judge. Two qualifications bound the comparison. The gray-box figure is transcribed from its original paper and was produced under a different judge, and the two methods do not operate under matched query budgets, so the query-cost comparison contrasts an until-success protocol with a fixed-budget random search rather than two methods at a common operating point. Whether REPRO’s attacks and a gray-box baseline’s attacks would be classified identically by an arbiter independent of both searches is discussed in Section 5.4. Current safety alignment is brittle against systematic optimization. The combination of 100% ASR across 36 models and a median query cost of 24 (indicating that over half of all behaviors are solved by the initial population alone, before evolutionary search begins) suggests that current safety alignment provides a shallow barrier rather than a deep one. For the majority of harmful behaviors, a well-constructed initial population drawing on known attack patterns and cached successful attacks is sufficient to bypass safety training without any iterative optimization. The strength of this reading depends on how much of the work the cache is doing: in most runs the initial population is warm-started from attacks that succeeded on earlier behaviors, so the claim established here is that a warm-started population suffices, not that a cold one would. Separating the two would require the cold-start runs described in Section 5.2. The evolutionary loop is primarily responsible for the harder tail of behaviors where simple strategies fail. This finding has practical implications: defenders cannot rely on the assumption that effective attacks require extensive computational search, because a substantial fraction of the attack surface is accessible through straightforward prompt engineering augmented by a modest seed library. Shared cross-behavior attacks are discoverable on the training set. Transfer optimization finds single prompts reaching 100% ASR across all 50 AdvBench behaviors. Because evaluation uses the same 50 behaviors as optimization, this is evidence of multi-behavior coverage on the optimization set, not of universality to held-out behaviors.
