The benchmark structure, shown in Figure 6(a), is a four-story, 2-bay by 2-bay steel frame with a 2.5 m by 2.5 m plan and a uniform story height of 3.5 m. This configuration yields a 12-DOF system, with each floor possessing three degrees of freedom, translational motion in the x and y directions and torsional rotation about the z-axis. Among the various loading and configuration conditions defined in the original benchmark study, this work focuses on Case 4, in which structural asymmetry is introduced by placing an additional 150 kg slab mass eccentrically on the top floor. This eccentricity couples the lateral and torsional responses across all four floors, engaging all 12 DOFs simultaneously and making the modal identification problem considerably more demanding than the symmetric baseline cases, providing an appropriately challenging testbed for evaluating the robustness and accuracy of DRL-DCO in a practical FEMU context. Further details of the benchmark structure and experimental setup are available in Johnson et al. [48]. Two FEMs of the 12-DOF benchmark structure are employed in this case study, each serving a distinct and complementary role. The first is the high-fidelity analytical FEM developed in the original benchmark study, depicted in Figure 6(b), which provides the reference modal data against which the identification results are evaluated. This model captures the full complexity of the structural dynamic behavior, including coupling effects between lateral and torsional modes arising from mass eccentricity, and features a fully defined global stiffness matrix that faithfully represents the system’s true response under both symmetric and asymmetric conditions. A rigid diaphragm assumption is adopted, such that each of the 4 floors is modeled with 3 DOFs, namely lateral displacements in the x and y directions and torsional rotation about the z-axis. The modal frequencies and mode shapes generated from this reference model serve as the target observations for the subsequent model updating and stiffness identification procedures. The second model, referred to as the identification model and shown in Figure 6(c), is a simplified 12-DOF shear-type FEM that provides a tractable parameterization for stiffness estimation. Rather than representing the full global stiffness matrix with its coupling terms, this model characterizes each floor f by three equivalent stiffness parameters, k(f) 1 , k(f) 2 , and k(f) 3 (f = 1, . . . , 4), representing the lateral stiffness in the x and y directions and the torsional stiffness about the z-axis at each inter-story level. Accordingly, a total of 12 stiffness parameters was considered to be identified from the modal data generated by the reference analytical FEM. This simplified modeling approach intentionally omits inter-DOF coupling terms in the stiffness matrix in favor of computational tractability, while preserving the essential inter-story stiffness characteristics that are most directly indicative of structural damage at each floor level.
