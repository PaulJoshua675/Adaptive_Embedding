Substituting this into the completeness identity above and regrouping by attribution index i recovers exactly P i R(H) i , so P i R(H) i = F(x) −F(b) holds at any truncation depth H — provided the deepest tracked layer uses the full derivative, as in Equations 3 and 10. Practical numerical error. The proof above is exact in the continuous limit for any differentiable choice of mechanism ci, since the path itself does not depend on the accuracy of that choice. Two sources of numerical error remain when evaluating it in practice: (i) Riemann-sum discretization over K steps, identical in kind to vanilla integrated gradients, and (ii) the backward finite difference used for ∂γi/∂α (Equation 6), which vanilla IG never needs since its path derivative is closed-form — genuinely new to our method, but vanishing as ∆α →0. Implementation invariance, symmetry, and linearity (below) have no such gap, since they hold exactly for any K. None of our experiments apply post-hoc normalization; all reported attributions are raw. We quantify this residual with the per-sample completeness delta δ(n) = P i R(n) i −(F(x(n)) − F(b(n))). On the synthetic benchmark (K=100), we report it relative to the output dif- ference (Table C.1), computed batch-wise as a weighted absolute percentage error (WAPE), P n |δ(n)|  P n |F(x(n)) −F(b(n))| over evaluation samples n within each seed, then averaged (mean ± std) across seeds. We use WAPE rather than the per-sample mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), since the latter is unstable whenever a single sample’s F(x(n)) −F(b(n)) is near zero; WAPE is the standard fix for this in forecasting [Kolassa and Schütz, 2007].
