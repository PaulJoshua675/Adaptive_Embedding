Each position describes one class of tacitness correctly, and each is narrow in a characteristic way (Table 5). Nonaka and Takeuchi’s conversion is correct for contingently tacit structure. Their exter- nalization works through metaphor, analogy, hypothesis and model, and each of these acts as a new query put to the same memory run the same way; §4.6 shows the programme doing exactly this. Critics read the theory as treating all tacit knowledge as convertible content (Tsoukas 2003). Nonaka and von Krogh reject that reading: the theory “should not reduce all tacit knowledge to that which can be potentially articulated”, because at one end of a continuum lies embodied knowledge that cannot be represented (Nonaka & von Krogh 2009, 642). Their continuum, however, orders knowledge by how far it can be articulated, and movement along it leaves knowledge “transformed and also enriched” (2009, 642). They describe articulation as “more fluid than a discrete shift from subsidiary to focal awareness” (2009, 641), the shift that, for Polanyi, disrupts action. A continuum of this kind cannot say why something stays unstated. It may be that no query has yet reached it, which is contingent tacitness, or that every regime that could state it is one in which it no longer exists, which is regime-bound tacitness. Only in the second case does explication cost what it explicates. Tsoukas is right about regime-bound tacitness and narrow in the opposite direction. He denies conversion for all tacit knowledge, yet contingently tacit structure does become explicit when a new operation is added (§4.6). Collins’s account divides the classes most finely, because his three kinds fall into different classes. Relational knowledge is contingently tacit, somatic knowledge is regime-bound yet articulable at the level of law (§4.4), and collective knowledge lies outside the substrate (§5). What it does not show is the dial that trades the somatic stratum against exact audit. Polanyi’s dictum covers every class at once. What it leaves open is what sets the boundary in a given substrate and what moves it, and that is what the dial of §4.2 supplies. 13
