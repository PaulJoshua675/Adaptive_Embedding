Searching histopathological images is computationally and biologically challenging given the size of WSIs, long tail of rare entities, inter-patient heterogeneity, and processing variability. SMILY was one of the first approaches, comparing representations extracted with a convolutional neural network (10). Yottixel and SISH then introduced strategies to reduce a WSI to a small set of representative patches, thereby facilitating search (11, 12). Our design responds to both constraints with a mixed strategy: on the query side, pathologists choose a query region, which is only a fraction of a WSI limiting computational work. As the composition of a WSI is primarily a property of grossing, a small set of representative regions carries most of the diagnostically relevant signal (26). Our split in homogeneous vs. heterogeneous queries still leaves room for intra-tumor heterogeneity (27). On the archive side, we index all artifact-free patches, which evades expert annotation work and avoids the risk of discarding diagnostically decisive regions. Through product quantization and inverted-file structure, the indices can be queried in standard RAM configurations (25). Our modified chamfer similarity is a middle ground between patch-to- patch and WSI-to-WSI retrieval, balancing speed and accuracy. Prior AI work on salivary gland histopathology has focused on classification tasks, performing well at benign/malignant discrimination but degrading on histologic subtyping (28, 29). Fitting the correct decision boundaries is limited by the number of examples per class, which becomes increasingly difficult in the long tail of a taxonomy (30) and typically requires manual annotation beyond the routinely encoded ICD-O code (31). While AI decision aids improve accuracy when their output is correct, they reduce it when it is wrong (32), a problem particularly pronounced for ambiguous entities like salivary gland neoplasms where a Dutch series recently reported 8.3% discordance on expert review (33). By presenting the pathologist with a shortlist rather than a single label, our approach may be less prone to this confirmatory anchoring and can guide more effective ancillary testing. A content-based retrieval approach in oral pathology was recently shown to increase diagnostic accuracy, with the largest gains in underrepresented tumor categories (34). Despite evaluating a broad cohort of a rare tumor group, we could not represent its full morphological spectrum. All cases originate from a single institution, digitized on the same scanners, which limits external validity. Since we use FMs pretrained on multi-institutional data, we expect the approach to transfer well to other institutions and entities. All diagnoses were made by board-certified pathologists, yet they were rendered over a period of years, not reflecting the recently updated ICD-O-4 classification (35). Algorithmically, product quantization and approximate nearest-neighbor search do not guarantee retrieval of the mathematically most similar case, yet this aligns with our approach of providing pathologist with a set of most similar cases. While we regard pathologist-selected query regions as the best way to use our search engine, an automatic WSI-to-WSI mode is available through our ABMIL model. Its lower performance compared with pathologist-selected queries indicates that it does not identify the most relevant query regions, most likely due to limited training data. In summary, we show that pretrained FMs, without further training, can extend an individual pathologist's frame of reference to that of the department. Future work will test whether the tool measurably improves pathologists' performance and how the approach scales to larger cohorts.
