Preliminaries: Score Distillation Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) [37] uses a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model ϵϕ to optimize a differen- tiable renderer G parameterized by θ. It relies on the ob- servation that the model’s noise prediction at each noise level is proportional to the score of the noisy image distri- bution [43]. SDS uses this score to guide images produced by G without requiring paired target images. For a sampled timestep t and Gaussian noise ϵ, the SDS gradient is: ∇θLSDS = w(t)  ϵγ ϕ(zt(x); c, t) −ϵ  ∂x ∂θ , (1) where x is an image produced by G, zt(x) is obtained by adding noise ϵ to x at timestep t, and c is a condition such as a text prompt. Here, ϵγ ϕ(zt(x); c, t) denotes the model’s noise prediction with classifier-free guidance, and w(t) weights the gradient at different timesteps. SDS is known to produce blurry or oversaturated results, which has motivated numerous follow-up works [9, 32, 58, 63]. Some of these works have identified the high variance associated with subtracting the sampled noise ϵ from the model’s prediction as a contributing factor. In particular, Variational Score Distillation (VSD) [48] addresses this is- sue by replacing the sampled noise ϵ subtracted from the model’s prediction with the prediction of an auxiliary model ϵLoRA fine-tuned on images produced by G. Noise-Free Score Distillation (NFSD) [22] provides a new interpretation of score distillation. Specifically, it decomposes the conditional noise prediction into three components: condition alignment, correction toward the model’s image distribution, and denoising: ϵϕ(zt; c, t) = δC+δD+δN. Under this interpretation, subtracting the auxil- iary prediction in VSD approximately cancels the denoising component, leaving the directions associated with condition alignment and domain correction to guide optimization. While SDS and VSD optimize individual instances for a given prompt, Distribution Matching Distillation (DMD) [56, 57] extends this approach to training a gen- erative model. DMD uses the VSD loss to train a student model, guided by a pretrained diffusion model referred to as the teacher. The student learns to generate images for arbitrary prompts. Our method adopts this teacher–student formulation for training a stable editor. Although these methods were originally formulated for noise-predicting diffusion models, score distillation can also be applied to flow-matching models [17]. This exten- sion relies on converting predicted flow velocities into score estimates. We adopt this formulation in our method. 4. This creates a train–test gap that can cause errors and artifacts to accumulate over successive edits.
