Performance-runtime trade-off. SurvICL lies on the Pareto frontier across C-index and IBS for all dataset-size regimes (Appendix D), and on the frontier for MAE in the small regime and all held-out datasets. SurvICL combines strong predictive performance with competitive runtime. Calibration and time accuracy. SurvICL is among the best-calibrated methods: its average KM- calibration rank (6.0) trails only the KM estimator (4.3), the Cox model (5.0), DeepSurv (5.6) and sksurv GBS-Cox (5.9) (Figure 15). Both KM and the Breslow baseline hazard of the Cox model enforce calibration by construction. In MAE, SurvICL is close to Survival-IC and slightly behind SurvivalPFN, without statistically significant difference (Figure 18). What makes SurvICL work. Figure 4c ablates design choices on development datasets with a shallower model trained for 10K steps, cf. Appendix C for more details. The prior has a substantial impact on performance: by keeping the exact same architecture and only changing the generation process, our proportional-odds prior outperforms Cox (77.6) and mixed priors (84.0). Compared to the prior of the other TFMs, our prior yields better results than SurvPFN (79.0), SurvivalPFN (82.7), and SIC (83.7). This demonstrates that our prior is highly effective, while adding a censoring-time prediction head actually reduces performance (81.0). Administrative censoring and time discretiza- tion have limited effects at this scale; we retain them to represent study-end censoring and finite temporal resolution. The final model uses 999 quantiles for finer resolution, despite a small advan- tage for 99 in the ablation (88.5 versus 87.2). 7 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION Designing Efficient Survival TFMs. SurvICL differs from existing survival TFMs along three axes (Table 1). Output and supervision. Unlike previous survival TFMs that rely on discrete time bins such as TFM4Survival (Kim et al., 2026) and SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026), which use classification or likelihood losses over time bins, SurvPFN (B¨ohm et al., 2026) uses a censored likelihood with a ranking term over 1,000 bins, and Survival-IC (Seletkov et al., 2026) uses the DeepHit loss (Lee et al., 2018) over 10 bins. SurvICL is the first to output a continuous quantile function. Quantile outputs are not new per se: TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) uses a pinball loss for regression, and censored quantile regression (Portnoy, 2003; Pearce et al., 2022) modifies this loss to account for censoring. With latent-time supervision, no such modification is needed, as censoring is handled by the prior. SurvivalPFN also supervises with latent times, but over a time grid and with censoring times as an additional target, which our ablations show to be detrimental. Prior. SurvivalPFN draws times from random noised neural networks with several censoring mechanisms, SurvPFN draws Weibull times from a risk score and a dataset-specific shape, and Survival-IC mixes an extended hazard and a discrete-time model, with covariate-independent and administrative censoring. Our PO prior instead combines a flexible, multi-modal baseline hazard with a random risk function, for both event and censoring times; in our ablations, it outperforms the SurvPFN prior as well as Cox and AFT priors. Backbone and evaluation. Like Survival-IC, built on TabICL (Qu et al., 2025), we reuse an existing architecture, TabICLv2, which is not our contribution. Finally, we provide, to our knowledge, the first comparison of all four survival TFMs, on 63 datasets. Implications. On the small cohorts that dominate clinical research, SurvICL is the first model to rank above the Cox model, the default of practitioners for half a century, without any training or tuning, while remaining among the best-calibrated methods. This makes foundation models a credible default for survival analysis. 9
