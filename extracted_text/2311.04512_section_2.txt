Interaction Models for Motion Forecasting. Complex in- teractions between agents and their surroundings have been the focus of motion forecasting. Early researches [1], [14] mainly represent interactions by hand-crafted energy functions, which cannot capture the rich behavioral strategies in the interac- tions. Recent approaches [2], [12] wildly utilize deep neural networks, including Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) [15], Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) [4] and Conditional VAE(CVAE) [16], to depict the social and environmental interactions for agents. Social LSTM [17] introduces a social pooling layer to learn typical human-human interactions au- tomatically. Evolvegraph [18] captures the future dynamic in- teractions between agents with an evolution interaction graph. VectorNet [5] presents a hierarchical graph neural network to model the high-order interactions among all road components with vector representation. LaneGCN [19] exploits a fusion network to model four types of interactions between agents and lane segments, which is wildly employed by following researches [3], [20]. Since those interaction methods only model the observation interactions over individual agents and fail to reason about their interactions in the future [10], they always produce incompatible future trajectories over multiple agents. Our FFINet introduces a future feedback module to capture the future interaction with initial predictions and performs cross-temporal interactions for comprehensive information extraction. Multi-agent Joint Motion Forecasting. To predict trajecto- ries of multiple agent in a future scene simultaneously, some prior works [9], [20], [21] learn a joint predictor to predict trajectories in a joint space over multiple agents. MPF [8] introduces a probabilistic framework that efficiently learns latent variables to model the multi-step future motions of agents jointly. Sergio et al. [9] propose an implicit latent variable model to characterize the joint distribution over socially consistent future trajectories. SceneTransformer [22] formulates an attention-based encoder-decoder architecture for the joint prediction of all agents, producing consistent futures that account for interactions between agents. Although those models perform joint prediction, they typi- cally select one central agent and normalize all other agents’ information based on this central agent, which harms the performance of models [23]. Therefore, Kofinas et al. [24] propose local coordinate frames pernode-object to introduce roto-translation invariance to the geometric graph of the inter- acting dynamical system. Jia et al. [23] propose a symmetric way of encoding the spatial relationship between elements in the scene, which enables the model to predict multiple agents’ future in one forward without performance drop. HiVT [20] addresses the joint prediction problem by de- composing the absolute coordinates into position vectors and relative information. To predict the initial trajectories of multi- agents in a scene, our FFINet also decomposes the data into position vectors, current positions and relative relationship for comprehensive future interaction extraction. Conditioned Motion Forecasting. For introducing the fu- ture information into models, MTR [25] first predicts the future trajectory of each agent, and concatenates the features of historical and future trajectories as representation of motion embedding. While they ignore the interactions between future trajectories, which makes it hard to deal with the possible future situation between multiple agents. The conditioned prediction methods [26], [27] generally forecast the future distribution of agents conditioned on the planning paths [11] or predicted goals [6], [28], [29]. After explicitly distinguishing the influencer and reactor, M2I [10] predicts the trajectories of the influencer and conditions them to the prediction of the reactor. In practice, predicting the relationships of interactive agents is always tricky in a complex traffic environment. ScePT [30] generates the prediction on the next timestamp for each agent via GRUs, which is further fed into the state vector with latent variable, repeatedly. III. FUTURE FEEDBACK INTERACTION NETWORK A. Overview Most recent works [19], [20] devote to exploring the obser- vation interactions and predict trajectories for a single agent in a single feedforward pass. In contrast, we design a FFINet for trajectory predictions by aggregating the current, historical and future interaction features.To obtain the inputs of FFINet, we decompose the absolute coordinates of trajectories and lanes. As shown in Fig. 2, the architecture of our FFINet is consists of: (1) Spatial-Temporal Encoding is employed to embed the position vectors and current positions of a scene, providing rich motion features for the following cross- temporal aggregation. (2) Cross-temporal Aggregation inte- grates the features subsequently with a current fusion module, observation interaction module, future feedback module and global fusion module. The Current Fusion Module fuses the current position features within the nearest agent-lane pairs. They are further added with corresponding vector features and integrated into the Observation Interaction Module. The Future Feedback Module is equipped with a future interac- tion, a feedback interaction and a feedforward interaction. It propagates the information between future features and observation interaction features of agents. The Global Fusion Module integrates the cross-temporal interaction features of agents and lanes. (3) Final Predictor predicts joint multi- modal trajectories of multiple agents in a single feedforward pass with regression branches and a classification branch. B. Data Decomposition To disentangle the inherent motion of agents and the relative correlation of a scene, as shown in Fig. 3, we decompose the absolute coordinates of trajectories and lanes into position vectors, current positions and relative relationship. Position Vectors. To represent inherent motion dynamics of agents, we vectorize the past trajectory of an agent i with the position vectors vt i = {pt i −pt−1 i }T t=1, where pt i ⊂R2 is the 2D coordinate of agent i at timestamp t. Besides, t ∈[0, T] and t ∈[T + 1, T + Tp] represent observation timestamps and predicted timestamps, respectively. Likewise, for a lane segment ξ, we represent it as the position vector vk ξ = {pk ξ − pk−1 ξ }N k=1 and the center coordinate pk,c ξ = 0.5∗(pk ξ +pk−1 ξ ), where pk ξ denotes the kth coordinates in N points on lane segment ξ. Current Positions. To enhance the current topology struc- ture of a scene and extract the current fusion features, we preserve the current absolute coordinates pT i and pk,c ξ of agent i and lane segment ξ to preserve the initial relative position relationships of the scene. Relative Relationships. To preserve relative spatial rela- tionships between agents for interaction, we employ ∆pij = pt j −pt i and θij = θj −θi to denote the relative distance and heading angle between agent i and agent j at times- tamp t, where θi denotes the orientation of reference vector pT i −pT −1 i . Similarly, ∆piξ = pT i −pk,c ξ and θiξ = θi −θξ denote the relative distance and heading angle between agent i and lane segment ξ. The relative relationships are employed by the relative interaction modules for correlation exploring. C. Spatial-Temporal Encoding With the decomposed data representation, we leverage sep- arate encoders, including trajectory vector encoder, lane vector encoder and current position encoders, to embed position vectors and current positions of agents and lane segments. Trajectory Vector Encoder. Given a scenario with multiple agents, we take the current absolute coordinates pT i of a given target agent i as the origin of the coordinate system. Meanwhile, its heading angle θi is adopted as x-axis positive direction. We uniformly translate all position vectors of agents in this scenario to the origin and rotate them to the x-axis positive direction, which generates the normalized vectors as follows: zt i = R⊤ i (vt i −pT i ), zt j = R⊤ j (R⊤ i (vt j −pT i )), (1) where R⊤ i , R⊤ j ⊂R2×2 are the transpose of rotation matrix parameterized by θi and θj. With the normalized vectors, we firstly leverage a trajectory vector encoder with multi-scale 1D CNN layers to effectively extract the spatial features. Then, we further utilize a linear layer to gather the temporal information and obtain vector feature ev i as follows: ev i = Linear(Conv([zt i, ai]T t=1)), (2) where ai is a 1 × T binary mask to indicate if the position vector at each step is padded or not. [·, ·] indicates the concatenation operation. Lane Vector Encoder. Similarly, in a lane vector encoder, we firstly normalize the position vector vk ξ of a lane segment ξ and obtain the normalized vector zk ξ like Eq.(1). Subsequently, we adopt the Lane Graph Convolution Net- work(LaneGCN) in [19] to extract the topology information of lane segments, which can be formulated as follows: ev ξ = LaneGCN([zk ξ, aξ]), (3) where aξ denotes the attributions of lane segments, including turning and traffic light information. Cross-Temporal Aggregation With the vector and current features of the scene, we design a cross-temporal aggregation, including a current fusion module, an observation interaction module, a future feedback module and a global fusion module, to integrate the historical, current and future interaction features of multiple agents. In this section, we first introduce a relative interaction block, which is frequently reused by the observation interaction mod- ules, future interaction modules and global fusion modules. Then we describe each module sequentially. To explore the spatial correlations among agents and lanes with the decomposed relative information, we design a plug- in base block called relative interaction block. As shown in Fig. 4, we take the interaction between lane segments and their surrounding agents as an example. Firstly, a neighbor agent’s relative distance and angle are encoded and concatenated with the feature of this agent and lane segment. This process repeats on each neighbor agent of the lane segments. Secondly, we aggregate the concatenated features of all neighbors and update the raw feature of this lane segment with them. Given a lane segment ξ and its neighbor agents, this interaction process can be denoted as: oξ = I(eξ, ei, ∆pξi, θξi), ∀i ∈Gξ a2l, (5) where eξ and ei are the inherent motion feature of lane segment ξ and a neighbor agent i. Gξ a2l denotes a set of neighbor agents which connected with lane segment ξ on graph Ga2l. The detailed process can be formulated as follows: eξi = ReLU(MLP2(∆pξi) + MLP2(cos(θξi), sin(θξi))), oξi = MLP1([eξi, MLP2(eξ), ei]), oξ = MLP1(MLP2(eξ) + X i∈Gξ a2l oξi), (6) where MLP2 represents the stack of two linear layers, a ReLU function. Current Fusion Module. To make use of the current topology information of agents and lane segments, we further IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON INTELLIGENT TRANSPORTATION SYSTEMS 5 integrate the current feature between nearest agent-lane pairs, which can be denoted as follows: ep1 i = MLP1([zp i , zp ξi]), ep i = MLP1([ep1 i , MLP1(ep1 i ))]. (7) Similarly, we can obtain the current feature ep ξ for lane segment ξ. Finally, we add the current features to vector features and obtain the inherent motion features of agent i and lane segment ξ, which can be formulated as follows: ei = ev i + ep i , eξ = ev ξ + ep ξ. (8) Observation Interaction Module. The observation inter- action module is leveraged to capture the observed relative correlation between agents and surroundings with the rela- tive relationships and extracted features in Eq.(8). Equipped with our relative interaction block, we follow LaneGCN [19] to construct four types of interactions, including Agents to Lanes (A2L), Lanes to Lanes (L2L), Lanes to Agents (L2A), and Agents to Agents (A2A). According to Eq.(5), it can be formulated as: A2L : oξ = I(eξ, ei, ∆pξi, θξi), ∀i ∈Gξ a2l, L2L : oξ = ϕ(oξ, oζ, ∆pξζ, θξζ), ∀ζ ∈Gξ l2l, L2A : oi = I(ei, oξ, ∆piξ, θiξ), ∀ξ ∈Gi l2a, A2A : oi = I(oi, oj, ∆pij, θij), ∀j ∈Gi a2a, (9) where ϕ denotes the LaneGCN which has the same architec- ture of lane vector encoder. Future Feedback Module. To capture the possible fu- ture interactions and feed them back to the observations, we propose a future feedback module to exert the feedback influence of future interactions on the feed-forward prediction. Specifically, with the vector features of agents obtained from Eq.(2), we first utilize an initial predictor to forecast a single trajectory prediction of each agent on future Tp timestamps. Subsequently, a future encoder is used to embed those initial predictions, which can be formulized as, ˆpc i = Linear(MLP1(ev i )), e f i = Linear(Conv(ˆpc i)), (10) where ˆpc i indicates the predicted initial trajectory, and e f i represents the future feature of agent i. The future encoder has the same structure as our trajectory vector encoder. After obtaining the future feature of agents, we integrate them with the corresponding observation interaction features obtained from Eq.(9) with three types of interactions: (i) Feedback Interaction. We first integrate the observation interaction features of the target agent with the future features of corresponding surrounding agents. According to Eq.(5), this process can be denoted as f b i = I(1)(oi, e f j , ∆pij, θij), ∀j ∈ Gi a2a, where Gi a2a denotes all agents around the agent i within a certain distance threshold, including the target agent itself. (ii) Future Interaction. We subsequently design a future interaction to explore the plausible interaction behaviors be- tween agents, which are vital clues for feedback refinement. Similarly, it can be denoted as f u i = I(2)(f b i , f b j , ∆pij, θij), ∀j ∈Gi a2a. (iii) Feedforward Interaction. We further update the obser- vation interaction features to the future interaction features in a feedforward way, which forms a closed-loop infor- mation propagation between historical and future informa- tion. We obtain the feedforward interaction features f f i = I(3)(f u i , oj, ∆pij, θij), ∀j ∈Gi a2a. Finally, for a given agent i, we add the updated feedforward interaction feature f f i up to the observation interaction features, so as to generate a cross-temporal fusion features for each agent as follows: fi = oi + f f i . (11) Global Fusion. We introduce a simple global fusion module to propagate the context feature into the obtained cross- temporal features of each agent, which can provide a compre- hensive representation of each agent for reasonable trajectory prediction. In particular, taking the observation lane feature oξ in Eq.(9) as well as the cross-temporal fusion feature fi in Eq.(11), we employ the same network architecture with the observation interaction module, and generate the final feature gi for a given agent i. E. Final Prediction Taking the final feature gi as input, our final predictor can generate normalized coordinates of multi-modal trajectories for multiple agents in a single shot. They can be transformed to absolute coordinates with the inverse operations in Eq.(1). Specifically, for providing K possible future trajectories and their probabilities of best-predicted trajectory, we equip the prediction head with K regression branches and a classifica- tion branch. The process can be formulated as follows: ˆpk i = MLP3(gi), sk i = MLP3([MLP4(ˆpk,Tp i ), gi)]), (12) where MLP3 consists of the MLP Block following a linear layer; and MLP4 is the stack of a linear layer, a ReLU function and a linear layer. Besides, ˆpk,Tp i denote the endpoint of the kth predicted trajectory of agent i. Model Training. Unlike the two-stage goal-based meth- ods [6], [32], our FFINet obtains the initial and final pre- dictions in a single shot. Therefore, we calculate the error of initial and final predictions simultaneously. Specifically, we employ Smooth l1 loss Lc reg to measure the regression distance error between the initial predictions and ground- truth trajectories. As for the final multi-modal predictions, we take the winner-takes-all (WTA) [33] strategy and back- propagate the loss of the best-predicted trajectory. Besides, we measure the regression loss Lreg across all time steps, calculate the endpoint loss Lend with the smooth l1 loss and the classification loss Lcls with the max-margin loss, as done in [19]. The total loss L can be denote as follows: L = Lreg + λLend + βLcls + γLc reg, (13) where λ, β and γ are three constant weights.
