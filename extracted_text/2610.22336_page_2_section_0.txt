To be more specific, we identify three properties that a treatment policy learned from data should possess. To begin with, the policy should generalize in the sense that the performance it attains on the patients seen during training carries over to new patients drawn from the same population. From the perspective of contextual and hidden-parameter Markov decision processes (MDPs) [11,19,28,44], the natural goal without interacting with a new patient is a policy that is near-optimal on average over the population [49], and generalization across populations of environments has been studied extensively in deep RL [24,46]. With a simulation model, we can pursue this goal by training on as many simulated patients as needed, far beyond the 17 patients of the pilot clinical trial, provided that the simulated population is broad enough to contain the patients of interest. In addition, the policy should be robust to errors in its observation. In particular, PSA serves only as an indirect measure of the underlying tumor burden, and radiographic progression can occur without any rise in PSA [6]. Consequently, the quantity on which the policy acts may deviate from the quantity it aims to control, while RL policies are known to be sensitive to even small adversarial perturbations of their observations [21,51]. Most importantly, the policy should retain reliable performance under shifts in the patient dynamics. Indeed, the parameters of the model, including the strength of competition between sensitive and resistant cells on which the success of adaptive therapy hinges, are estimated from the sparse PSA records of a small cohort of patients, and are hence subject to considerable statistical error [5,52]. Such shifts are particularly consequential in medical domains, where real patients are scarce and the dynamics of a patient may further change during treatment [1]. To address these requirements, in this manuscript we propose to study adaptive therapy through the lens of robust contextual MDPs. A contextual MDP [19] describes a family of MDPs whose transition dynamics are indexed by a context, drawn from a population and hidden from the decision maker. Adaptive therapy fits naturally into this framework once we identify the context with the patient, namely the initial composition of the tumor together with its competition and growth parameters. Each patient then defines its own tumor dynamics, to be inferred by the clinician from the history of PSA measurements and treatment decisions, and AT50 is one particular policy acting on this history. By viewing the patient as a context, we treat generalization and robustness to shifts in the patient dynamics in a unified manner, since both concern the population from which the context is drawn. Specifically, generalization requires the policy to perform well on new contexts drawn from this population, which can be attained by training on sufficiently many sampled contexts, while robustness requires the policy to remain reliable when the context deviates from this population and thereby shifts the transition kernel that it determines. To attain such robustness, we draw on robust MDPs, in which one seeks the best worst-case performance over an ambiguity set of transition kernels, and which have been studied extensively for standard MDPs [22, 31, 38, 40, 41, 48, 51]. We carry this viewpoint over to the contextual MDP and formulate robustness as a Markov game between the controller and the context, in which the context may deviate adversarially from the population from which it is drawn [29, 32]. To account for errors in the observation, we further train the policy under random perturbations of the measured PSA. Within a model of abiraterone therapy fitted to clinical data [52], which we refer to as the nominal model, the resulting robust policies substantially outperform AT50. In addition, we attain robustness to shifts in the patient dynamics at almost no price in the nominal model, and the resulting policy nearly Pareto-dominates the nominally trained policy, i.e., the policy trained in the nominal model with exact observations, under a broad range of realistic errors in the observation and shifts in the patient dynamics. Our contributions can be summarized as follows. First, we formulate adaptive therapy as a contextual MDP in which the patient is modeled as a hidden context, and find that a policy learned within this formulation substantially outperforms AT50 and general- izes to patients fitted to clinical data. Within the competition model of [52], the learned policy extends the mean progression-free time from 29.58 months under AT50 to 50.35 months, while treating in a comparable fraction of months. In addition, on the 32 patients whose initial tumor compositions are fitted to clinical data in [52], policies trained on a broad synthetic population constructed without reference to these patients perform comparably to policies trained on a population calibrated to them. Hence, generalization to the patients of interest can be attained by training on sufficiently many simulated patients drawn from a broad population, without tailoring the population to these patients. Second, we find that errors in PSA of realistic magnitude substantially reduce the benefit of policies that do not account for them. Specifically, under random errors of up to about 20% of the true PSA value, the nominally trained policy loses a substantial part of its benefit, and AT50 performs only marginally better than continuous treatment.
