Standard expected gradients performs well with just one path point per baseline, since the straight-line path has a constant, closed-form derivative dγi/dα = xi −bi, so a single sampled α already gives this derivative exactly. Our method’s path derivative ∂γi/∂α is instead estimated via a backward finite difference (Equation 6), since hi(α) is generally nonlinear; our method therefore benefits from allocating more of a fixed evaluation budget to additional path points per baseline rather than to additional baselines, since both the finite-difference and Riemann-sum errors (Appendix C) compound when paths are under-sampled. 3.5 Axiomatic properties Since our method belongs to the family of path methods [Sundararajan et al., 2017], it inherits completeness and sensitivity(a) (for the direct term) unconditionally, and implementation invariance, symmetry, and linearity under precise preconditions on the causal graph and surrogate that we formalize in Appendix C. Experimental setup and costs are reported in Appendix E. 4.1 Comparison with causal attributions Conditional SHAP [Aas et al., 2021] and causal Shapley values [Heskes et al., 2020] are the most established approaches for dependency-aware feature attribution. To evaluate how well each method captures ground-truth causal effects, we compare their attributions against a reference causal effect derived from Pearl’s do-calculus [Pearl, 2009]. Among the causal effects this framework gives rise to, the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE) is the most appropriate for local explanations, as it measures the effect of intervening on a single feature while conditioning on the root nodes R: CATEi = E[F | do(Xi = xi), XR\{i} = xR\{i}] −E[F | do(Xi = bi), XR\{i} = xR\{i}]. (9) While several papers have analyzed the theoretical relationship between local attributions and Pearl’s do-calculus [Heskes et al., 2020, Janzing et al., 2020, Ng et al., 2025], to the best of our knowledge no prior work has empirically compared attribution methods against the CATE directly. Doing so gives us an exact ground truth for each feature’s local causal effect, available only in this fully controlled setting. Since the CATE is an effect rather than an attribution (e.g. it need not satisfy completeness), we compare feature rankings via Spearman correlation rather than raw values; the two experiments that follow instead use biological data, where ground truth is global (Dyngen) or approximate (IFN-β). To enable computation of both the CATE and the local attributions, we construct a synthetic bench- mark with a known SCM: the target is a random linear combination of a subset of features, and a Lasso regression model F is trained on the generated data, using the SCM’s structural mechanisms directly for causal Shapley values and our own methods to isolate attribution quality from implementation noise (full details in Appendix F).
