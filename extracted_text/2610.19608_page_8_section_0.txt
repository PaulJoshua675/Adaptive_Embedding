To measure how far that rule matches a human reading, the author labelled a sample of D1 answers by hand on 13 October 2026, blind to the automatic labels. Sample. 24 of the 162 D1 answers, eight per language (English, Estonian, Finnish), drawn by script with the fixed seed 20261013. Within each language the draw was stratified by category: three answers from each of two categories and two from the third, with the category that gives two rotating across languages. The 24 answers were then shown in a shuffled order. Each answer carries its four candidate brands, so the sample holds 96 candidate labels, 32 per language. Blinding. The rater saw each question, its answer text exactly as the rule read it (the non-thought text of the response), and the four candidate brands of its category with their listed aliases, in the fixed order of the request. The packet showed no automatic label, no forecast, no wave and no other answer to the same question. The key that links the packet rows to the automatic labels was kept apart until all 96 human labels were recorded. Question asked of the rater. For each candidate the rater wrote yes, no or unsure. The packet defined yes as an answer that names the brand, or one of the other names listed for it, in any form: an inflected form such as an Estonian or Finnish case ending, a different capitalisation, a spelling slip or a web address. A negative mention counts as yes; a different company with a similar name counts as no. The human definition therefore includes inflected forms, which the automatic rule misses by design. The rater marked no label unsure. Measures. With the human label as the reference, we report for the automatic label, by language and overall: agreement (share of labels on which the two agree), precision (share of automatic positives the rater also marked positive) and recall (share of the rater’s positives the rule found), each with a 95% Wilson interval [10]. The intervals treat labels as independent; four labels share an answer, so the intervals are too narrow. Frozen labels do not change. Each disagreement is listed in the supplement with its type, such as an inflected form or an alias missing from the list. The rule found every brand the rater marked: recall is 37 of 37, and in Estonian and Finnish, where an inflected brand name could defeat a whole-token match, no positive was missed in this sample. Both disagreements are English answers in which the rule marked a brand the rater did not. An AI assistant re-read both answer texts and flagged that in each the brand is the heading of a numbered list item and is named again in its description; the rater then confirmed both as omissions.
