Missing-premise cases fall between these extremes; models also per- form relatively well on queries that exceed SQL expressibility or require external knowledge. Taken together, the pattern suggests that Implicit prompt- ing elicits critique mainly when a flaw appears as an overt incompatibility or recognizable capa- bility boundary. It is much less effective when detecting the flaw requires reasoning about under- specification or absent information, indicating that premise critique remains cue-dependent rather than robust across error forms. 4.4 From Error Types to Failure Behaviors The preceding error-type analysis establishes which premise flaws are difficult to recognize, but it does not explain what models do with those flaws when critique fails. We therefore move from an input-level taxonomy to a response-level account. Whereas an error category characterizes a query flaw in its database context, a failure mechanism identifies the observable operation through which the model makes the query appear answerable. We annotate 8,000 eligible failures with four non-exclusive mechanisms: Assumption Injection (M01) introduces an unsupported interpretation, value, or proxy; Contradiction Reinterpretation (M02) modifies incompatible requirements; Con- text/Evidence Misuse (M03) treats partial schema or example evidence as conclusive; and Explicit Constraint Omission (M04) drops a complete re- quirement. Attribution requires strict agreement between two judges, with strong automatic and human reliability (macro κ = 0.8183 and 0.9434, respectively; see Appendix C for details). Among the eligible failures analyzed across prompting settings, M01 is the dominant failure mechanism (72.60%), followed by M03 (10.75%), while M02 (1.38%) and M04 (0.86%) remain rare. More importantly, Figure 4 shows a marked redis- tribution across prompting settings. From Base to Explicit prompting, M01 decreases from 81.03% to 31.12%, whereas M03 increases from 1.46% to 58.06%; the Implicit setting lies between these end- points. Stronger instructions suppress silent com- pletion of missing query premises, yet the remain- ing failures increasingly arise because models over- interpret limited schema or example evidence when deciding whether, and how, the premise is defective. Despite their different surface forms, these failures reveal a shared limitation in that models tend to produce apparently answerable interpretations without recognizing the underlying premise flaws. 5
