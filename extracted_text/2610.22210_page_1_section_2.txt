Segmentation of thin, elongated structures is particularly vulnerable to connectivity errors, as even a few misclassified pixels or voxels can break an otherwise continuous struc- ture. This issue arises in retinal vessels [21], cerebral vessels [26, 27], hepatic vessels [2], pavement cracks [15], and road networks [7], where continuity is essential to structural in- tegrity. Such local errors can fragment a single structure into multiple components while producing only minor changes in conventional region-overlap metrics [20]. Reliable thin- structure segmentation therefore requires preserving connec- tivity in addition to accurate region and boundary prediction. Centerline- and skeleton-based losses [1, 19, 20] provide direct structural supervision and may additionally incorporate boundary or width information. Target-only methods [13] avoid skeletonizing the prediction, reducing training-time computation and memory overhead. Their supervision, however, remains confined to fixed sup- port derived from the ground truth. More broadly, skeleton- based support depends on the chosen skeletonization pro- cedure [17], and even topology-preserving support may not identify the prediction error responsible for a connectivity break. Global objectives based on persistent homology or component graphs [3, 11, 16, 22] constrain connected com- ponents, loops, and cavities, yet provide limited guidance for repairing a specific local break. Neighbor-, affinity-, and path-based objectives [10, 24, 25, 28] provide localized su- pervision through individual neighbors, selected point pairs, or graph paths.
