A fixed-point curve in the CTM Appendix A. Method and robustness Computing the Jacobian of one thought cycle. To study the CTM’s neural dynamics we freeze the memory at a chosen tick. With the image tokens, the memory accumulators, and the short pre-activation history held fixed, one tick becomes an ordinary map from states to states, zt+1 = f(zt; At), where At bundles everything held constant. We reconstruct this map from the model’s own components and verify that a single reconstructed step reproduces the true next state to within numerical error. Iterating the map gives its fixed point; the Jacobian at the fixed point, obtained by automatic differentiation, gives its local behavior. From that Jacobian we read two numbers by power iteration: the spectral radius, which governs whether the map settles, and the largest singular value, which measures how much it can stretch a direction in a single step. Objects. Fix a tick and its context A. The frozen map f(·; A) : RD →RD of §2 is C1. A fixed point z⋆= z⋆(A) solves z⋆= f(z⋆; A); the Jacobian there is J = ∂f/∂z z⋆, with spectral radius ρ(J) = maxi |λi(J)| and operator norm σmax(J) = p λmax(J⊤J). The map is non-normal when J⊤J̸ = JJ⊤, which permits σmax(J) > 1 ≥ρ(J): transient stretching of some directions despite every eigenvalue decaying. Banach’s fixed-point theorem. If (X, ∥·∥) is complete and g : X →X is a contraction — ∥g(u) −g(v)∥≤L∥u −v∥for some L < 1 and all u, v — then g has a unique fixed point x⋆, and the iteration uk+1 = g(uk) converges to it geometrically, ∥uk −x⋆∥≤Lk∥u0 −x⋆∥. How we apply it. We do not have a global Euclidean contraction: σmax(J) > 1, so f can push nearby points apart in Euclidean norm, and Banach does not apply in ∥· ∥2. Local uniqueness and attraction follow instead from the spectral radius, through a standard fact (Householder): for any matrix J and any ε > 0 there is a vector norm whose induced operator norm satisfies ∥J∥⋆≤ρ(J) + ε. Since we measure ρ(J) = 0.15 < 1, choosing ε with ρ(J)+ε < 1 yields a norm ∥·∥⋆in which J is a contraction; because f is C1, it is then a contraction in ∥· ∥⋆on a neighborhood of z⋆, and Banach applies there. The fixed point is therefore locally unique and the iteration converges geometrically in ∥· ∥⋆— matching the observation that iterating from the current state reaches residual < 10−6 at 99.4% of image–tick pairs (see Estimation below). The non-normality (σmax(J) ≫ρ(J)) is exactly why convergence need not be monotone in the Euclidean norm, and why the state lags the moving fixed point by about one step even while it is strongly attracted in ∥· ∥⋆. Establishing non-normality. A stability check based on random directions is there- fore misleading: it averages f’s action over directions, reporting something close to the typical stretch — about 0.07 here, comfortably below one — and never samples the few directions stretched most. We confirm the stretching is genuine, not an artifact of differen- tiation, by perturbing z⋆along the single most-stretched direction and measuring the effect by finite differences (over 32 fixed points, the finite-difference stretch and the autograd op- erator norm agree to a median ratio of 1.000; median stretch 2.7). Stability of an unrolled map like this must be read from ρ(J) at the fixed point, not from an average stretch. Estimation. We estimate ρ(J) and σmax(J) two ways that agree in magnitude: power iteration at z⋆(used for Fig. 2 and the medians ρ(J)≈0.15, σmax(J)≈3.2, over 3055 fixed points), and exact eigen- and singular-value decompositions of the fully materialized J (used for the null comparisons of Appendix B; medians over 60 image–tick pairs, 20 images at ticks 8, 24 and 40).
