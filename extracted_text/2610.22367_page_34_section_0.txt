We require error below 10−7 radians throughout the domain. The tuned HRS baseline uses cubic fits on 35 intervals and general 44-bit fixed-point arithmetic. It meets the error bound with 16,000 Toffolis and 300 qubits. Locating the interval requires coherent comparisons against arbitrary boundaries, and each evaluation performs three multiplications on 44-bit numbers. The discovered circuit restructures this dataflow with three transformations, illustrated in Figure S3. Power-of-two-aligned intervals. The function arcsin(1/(2x)) changes rapidly near x = 1 and progressively more slowly as x increases. An arbitrary adaptive partition could follow this variation, but reversibly locating one of its intervals would require comparisons against general boundaries. The discovered circuit instead uses narrow intervals near x = 1 and wider ones where the function is flatter, while restricting every interval to the power-of-two-aligned, or dyadic, form [L, L + 2b) with L a multiple of 2b. We call such an interval a leaf, because these intervals are the leaves of a binary tree over the input bits. The leading bits of the input then determine the leaf. Because selection is cheap, a much finer partition is affordable: the circuit uses 345 intervals of widths 28 through 215, whereas the baseline uses 35 arbitrary intervals. The finer partition simplifies the arithmetic in two ways. A quadratic suffices instead of the baseline cubic, reducing the number of multiplications, and each polynomial depends only on the narrow local displacement δ = X −L, reducing its operand widths. A uniform partition at the finest width would require 7,936 intervals (Figure S3(b)), so the dyadic tree obtains these benefits without paying for uniformly fine resolution. Shared local coordinate. After selecting a leaf, a conventional polynomial-evaluation stage would form δ = X −L and rescale it according to the leaf width, requiring arithmetic and temporary storage. Moreover, leaves of different widths initially present differently scaled inputs to the polynomial-evaluation stage. The circuit replaces this coordinate arithmetic with an in-place cyclic shift of the input bits. For X = L + δ in a width-2b leaf, cyclically shifting the low 15 bits produces d = 215−bδ + cL. (62) The varying bits δ move to a common scale, while the fixed leaf bits wrap around to form cL. Because cL is constant within the leaf, the offline fit absorbs it into the coefficients. Thus every leaf feeds the same 15-bit polynomial-evaluation stage without subtraction, arithmetic rescaling, or a coordinate register (Figure S3(b)). For example, in a width-212 leaf, the low 15 bits consist of three bits fixed by the leaf followed by the 12 varying bits of δ. Shifting those bits cyclically left by three places produces d = 23δ + cL. Narrow direct accumulation. For each leaf, the coefficient table defines the integer quadratic qL(d) = g0 −d(g1 −g2d), (63) with intercept g0, slope g1, and curvature g2. A general Horner polynomial-evaluation stage multiplies large fixed-point numbers, retains work registers for those products, and materializes the polynomial value before adding it to the output. The shared polynomial-evaluation stage instead consumes the selected coefficients in place. It first overwrites the loaded slope g1 with the inner Horner value, then accumulates the outer product directly into k: t ←g1 −g2d (stored over g1), k ←k + g0 −dt. (64) No separate register ever holds the completed polynomial or either full product. The products themselves are also truncated: the circuit omits the low-order partial-product columns whose combined effect stays inside the error budget. The offline search fits the integer coefficients gi to the exact updates in Equation (64), including the truncated columns. The worst-case error over all inputs is 9.95 × 10−8 radians. The circuit uses 1,348 Toffolis and 115 qubits, for a TQ-cost of 160,000. Relative to the tuned HRS baseline, this reduces the Toffoli count by 12×, the qubit count by 2.6×, and the TQ-cost by 32×.
