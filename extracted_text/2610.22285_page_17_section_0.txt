D Real Robot Execution Figure 7: The simulated model of the kitchen used to generate the trajectory bank for the real robot demos. A box is used as a visual place- holder for the cat in the actual environment. We validate that the learned rewards translate into sensible physi- cal behavior by executing the top trajectories on the Franka Emika FR3 arm (Fig. 2c). The trajectory bank is generated entirely within a PyBullet model of the kitchen (Fig. 7) and all reward learning is performed offline; only the final selected trajectories are run on hardware. For each of the four methods (Informed Prior, Oracle Prior, Standard, and Random) and each of the three underspecifi- cation conditions (cat, stove, or point underspecified), we take the top-ranked test trajectories after K = 10 queries and execute them on the physical arm. Both Informed Prior and Oracle Prior are visually consistent across all three underspecification conditions. Whether cat, stove, or point was initially underspecified, the top trajectories steer the end-effector away from both the cat and the stove while keeping the knife pointed away from the cat. The Stan- dard baseline mirrors Informed Prior when point was underspec- ified. However, when cat was underspecified, the top trajectories carry the knife substantially closer to the cat; when stove was un- derspecified, they likewise route the end-effector closer to the stove (Fig. 8). In neither case did the preference queries recover the clearance that the demonstrations had missed. The Random base- line follows the same trend: visually similar to Informed Prior when point was underspecified, yet routing the knife closer to whichever of cat or stove was initially underspecified. The similar performance of all four methods when point is underspecified is partly a consequence of the trajectory bank, which contains a wide range of orientations. Since point is only weakly correlated with the distance features and has the widest spread of any feature, trajectories vary widely along point, and comparisons that differ along it are abundant. Trajectories that stay clear of both the cat and the stove at once are less common in the bank, so comparisons that would teach the robot to balance the two are rarer. This is a property of our kitchen, whose reachable workspace is small in xy; a larger scene would leave more room to stay clear of both objects at once. In addition, although the stove-underspecified condition shows a large gap in normalized reward between methods (Fig. 4c), the visible difference between some of the trajectories (Fig. 8) is less pronounced than this gap suggests. The stove feature scores the end-effector’s distance to a single reference point (cooktop center). Because the stove itself is a large object, distance to that point is only a loose proxy for how close the gripper actually appears to the stove. The end-effector can pass near the stove’s physical edge while still registering a sizable distance to its center. 17
