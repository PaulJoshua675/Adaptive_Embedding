Disagreement pattern. The most consistent dis- agreement is between Supported and Partially sup- ported: in 32% of annotator pairs per claim, one annotator marked the claim Supported while the other marked it Partially supported. This bound- ary was under-specified in our protocol: the task statement did not operationalize how much explicit textual evidence was required to justify the stronger rating. Annotator C as strictest rater. Annotator C marks claims Supported far less often than A and B (24.8% vs. 68.6% and 41.9%), while all three judge most claims at least partially supported (88.6%, 93.3%, and 98.1% for C, A, and B). Man- ual inspection suggests C required near-verbatim evidence for Supported, whereas A and B accepted paraphrased evidence. We retain all three annota- tors in the main results. What the agreement numbers mean for the pa- per’s claims. The 94% aggregate support rate quoted in §4.6 is robust under several reasonable agreement assumptions: (a) it remains ≥91% under any single-annotator-out leave-one-out; (b) within-one-rank agreement of 97–98% across all annotator pairs indicates strong convergence on the direction of support, which is the quantity the head- line claim relies on; (c) the 1.3% Contradicted rate, where chance-corrected agreement is highest (AC1 = 0.99), is the single most claim-critical number for paper integrity (it bounds the fabrication rate the auditor failed to catch). A revised protocol with sharper category definitions, an agreement- calibration pre-annotation phase, and a larger pool of annotators is the natural next step for human-eval methodology in this domain. G Human Evaluation Protocol for Audit Decisions Evaluation material and sampling. The eval- uation unit was a single synthetic interviewee re- sponse produced by the SimInter user-simulation pipeline and processed by Stage 4 in the 300-user experiment. These are generated interview re- sponses, not utterances taken from ReDial. Each response was linked to the historical ReDial user whose interactions informed the simulation. We se- lected 105 turns from distinct users, equally strati- fied by the auditor’s final outcome: 35 EXCLUDED, 35 FLAGGED–RETAINED, and 35 UNFLAGGED. The three groups were presented as consecutive 35-case blocks in a fixed order shared by all anno- tators, but the group boundaries and auditor outputs were concealed. Annotation procedure and human reference. Three co-author annotators independently reviewed the same 105 turns (315 judgments in total). They saw the target synthetic response, optional pre- ceding synthetic-interview context, automatically retrieved statements from the matching historical ReDial user’s original conversations, and optional derived statistical-profile information. They did not see the auditor’s action, issue flags, or expla- nations. The three judgment categories were in- terpreted as follows: EXCLUDED, an evidence- grounded problem warrants removing the entire synthetic turn; FLAGGED–RETAINED, a meaning- ful concern exists but does not warrant removal; and UNFLAGGED, no meaningful issue is identified. We used the majority vote of the three annotators as the human reference for each turn. The evaluation unit and reference are at the whole-turn level; they do not validate the correctness of individual auditor flag types. Inter-annotator agreement. All 105 cases re- ceived three labels. Agreement was unanimous on 70/105 (66.7%) and 2–1 on the remaining 35; there were no three-way splits. Nominal Fleiss’ κ was 0.664 (95% stratified case-bootstrap interval [0.572, 0.751]; 2,500 replicates, resampling within the three auditor strata). Within-stratum Fleiss’ κ values were 0.337 (excluded), 0.308 (flagged- but-retained), and 0.268 (unflagged). These lower within-stratum values suggest that aggregate agree- ment is influenced by the differences in label distri- butions across strata. Three-class and filtering accuracy.
