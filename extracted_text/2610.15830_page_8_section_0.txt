5.2 Second-moment exchange reverses which route is more susceptible Adam carries two gradient summaries: a first moment m, which averages signed gradients, and a second moment v, which averages squared gradients and rescales coordinate-wise updates (Kingma & Ba, 2015). We exchange either component between the two route endpoints, leaving each recipient’s model weights and optimizer step count fixed. This tests a component directly rather than inferring its importance from a correlation with formation history. Exchanging m leaves the mean route difference almost unchanged; its change lies inside the pre-specified equivalence margin. Exchanging v reverses the difference, from +0.1968 to −0.1265 (Figure 4a; exact intervals in Appendix F). Thus the second-moment intervention reverses which route is more susceptible to the ten-update pulse. The result concerns this response and time horizon. It does not test accumulating first-moment effects during extended continued training. A prospective follow-up with 40 new seeds yields an inherited-Adam combined-pulse difference of 0.1907. This is the third estimate from a separate confirmatory seed set, following 0.1951 in the matched-endpoint experiment and 0.1968 in the component-exchange experiment. The follow-up also separates the average scaling implied by v from its coordinate-wise pattern. Let ¯av denote the mean of Adam’s bias-corrected second-moment inverse-scaling terms across parameters; ¯av omits the first moment and current gradient and is not an actual update norm. Equalizing ¯av between routes while retaining each route’s v pattern reduces the ten-update response difference from 0.1907 to 0.0875. Replacing each route’s v by a scalar value reduces it to 0.1251; doing both reduces it to 0.0395 (Figure 3a). Conversely, swapping the other route’s v pattern while equalizing ¯av reverses the difference to −0.0110. Mean adaptive scale therefore explains part of the short response difference, while the coordinate-wise pattern also causally modifies the response in the recipient model. The pattern effect is defined in the common parameter coordinates inherited from the shared ancestor and acts together with the recipient weights and current gradients. The post-hoc 2 × 2 recombination and its interaction estimate are reported as exploratory in Appendix F. A post-hoc analysis of the same fixed trajectories connects this preconditioning to realized movement. Over the first ten opposing updates, the acquisition-formed to update-formed ratio is 0.987 for cumulative gradient norm but 1.872 for cumulative parameter-path length. Across seeds, the between-route path-length difference correlates 0.806 with the allocation-response difference; a leave-one-seed-out linear reconstruction using path length reduces squared error by 62% relative to a mean-only baseline. Path length is measured during the intervention, discards direction, and is not itself manipulated. It is therefore a strong mediator candidate rather than an identified mediation effect or an endpoint predictor. The result supports a concrete route from inherited v through coordinate-wise preconditioning to unequal parameter movement, while leaving the full mechanism of allocation change unresolved. The follow-up also changes the temporal interpretation. Inherited Adam produces a large route difference after 10–20 opposing updates. With fresh Adam, the difference is 0.0096 at update 10 but reaches 0.0274 at update 50; calibrated momentum-free SGD similarly rises from 0.0123 at update 10 to 0.0433 at update 50 and 0.0309 at update 100 (Figure 3b). All three differences shrink toward zero by update 500. At that point, the selected allocation endpoints differ by −0.00045 ([−0.00146, 0.00057]). Optimizer condition therefore changes when the route difference appears. The inherited-minus-fresh Adam contrast changes from 0.1811 and 0.2125 at updates 10 and 20 to −0.0067 and −0.0032 at updates 50 and 100. This sign change is not described by simply adding a fixed nonnegative fast optimizer component to the fresh-Adam trajectory. 5.3 Equal mean loss weights can leave different latent susceptibility The original routes differ in both acquisition and update history. To determine whether the susceptibility contrast depends on those route labels, we hold acquisition, mini-batches, update count, and mean target weight fixed. One formation schedule uses a constant target weight of 0.80. The other uses 1.00 and 0.60 for 250 updates each in a seed-specific randomized order. Both average to 0.80 over 500 updates. Their per-step weight variability and realized sequence jointly differ. The five pre-specified endpoint measurements are equivalent, yet the constant schedule retains a smaller total second moment: 0.8215 times that of the variable schedule.
