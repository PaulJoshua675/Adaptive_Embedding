Large language models (LLMs) are increas- ingly used in computational social science to assign scalar scores to social and psychologi- cal constructs. However, studies use different rating scales, and whether their scores remain comparable across scale designs is understud- ied. To address this gap, we define and system- atically investigate scale sensitivity, the varia- tion in LLM-generated scores caused by rating scale design. We distinguish numerical sen- sitivity, changes in normalized scores, from semantic sensitivity, changes in score interpre- tation. To assess both, our experimental design crosses 2 formats (continuous vs. ordinal), 4 ranges (3, 5, 11, and 21 points), and 2 signs (signed vs. unsigned) across 3 tasks (political leaning, sentiment, and toxicity), 2 prompting strategies (zero-shot and rubric-based), and 5 LLMs. We find that changing one design ele- ment shifts normalized scores by about 0.10 on average and changes the category of roughly 20% of texts. Sensitivity is greatest for toxicity, the only unipolar construct examined. Also, rubric-based prompting does not reduce sensi- tivity compared with zero-shot prompting. We provide practical guidance for designing more reliable LLM-based text scoring and highlight the need for robustness checks across alterna- tive scales. 1
