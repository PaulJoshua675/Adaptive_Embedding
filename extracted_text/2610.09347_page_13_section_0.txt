R2 floored at −2 for legibility. 5 Discussion The usual account of a genetic programming system paired with a downstream learner is additive: evolution gets part of the way and the learner absorbs the residual error. The measurements here do not support that account. The order of nonlinearity that evolution must supply changes from six to three on the same target, with the size limit, primitive set, and every search parameter held fixed and only the learner changed. The total structural complexity of the problem is not reduced by the learner; it is paid for somewhere else. What a learner buys is a transfer of budget, and the measurement of Section 4.2 makes that transfer visible directly on the evolved objects, instead of inferring it from error. That gives a criterion where previously there was a benchmark result. It is not that expressive learners suit hard problems. MUX6 is of lower order than the parity targets on which the separation is largest, and on it no learner helps at all; Nguyen-11 is of low order and there a linear learner is significantly better than every alternative. What decides the choice is whether the target admits a low order representation from which a bounded learner can recover it, which is the quantity ∆of Definition 2. Difficulty and the appropriate downstream model are orthogonal, and an experimenter reading difficulty off a benchmark’s reputation will choose wrongly in both directions. The second finding is uncomfortable for the paradigm itself, not for any particular system in it. Genetic programming is often chosen because its models are symbolic and stand on their own. Attaching a learner to improve accuracy systematically removes the selection pressure that would make them do so, and the effect is not incidental: on PAR4 under a random forest, evolution actively moves away from representations that predicted the target unaided. The more capable the learner, the smaller the share of the model that resides in the readable part. This tension is invisible when the downstream stage is fixed to be affine, because then there is nothing for the learner to absorb, and it applies equally to multidimensional systems that pair evolved features with a deterministic classifier or regressor [9, 15]. Two results previously reported for the single-feature form of this arrangement [17] are recovered here and read differently. The pattern already noted, a linear learner dominating one benchmark suite while nonlinear learners take over on a harder one, is reproduced here, but the two suites differ in ∆, not in difficulty; structure was never the binding constraint in the standard suites. That the evolved representation sometimes attains the target unaided is also reproduced, and Table 6 locates those cases precisely: they are the low order corner of the space, they never occur above order four, and the prevailing direction across the full range is the opposite one. Limitations. The parity family raises the target’s order and the relocatable part of it together, since parity is symmetric at every k and hence ∆= k−1. The dissociation of the two therefore rests on MUX6 and on the two continuous targets, which is thinner evidence than the pinning result it accompanies. The program supplies a single feature throughout; multidimensional systems supply several, and the generalisation of Definition 2 to d features is open. The Boolean experiments use full truth tables on eight inputs, which keeps the Fourier analysis exact but limits scale, since larger n would require sampling and reintroduce estimation into the quantity the study is built on. We do not know how to compute ∆for an arbitrary target and rely on families where it is known analytically.
