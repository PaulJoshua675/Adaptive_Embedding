The per-cell marginals πi = p(si = 1|O) make this explicit. For i ∈A the measurements can drive πi toward 0 or 1 as evidence accumulates; for i /∈A the marginal reduces to πi = p(si = 1 | sA), which the measure- ments cannot move, and its variance converges to a prior-conditional floor rather than to zero. A deterministic regressor, which must commit a value to every cell, cannot represent this distinction and produces confident predictions on cells the measurements never constrained; a posterior can, which is what makes it the appropriate object here. Since the likelihood p(O|s) is defined only implicitly through ray tracing and is unavailable in closed form, we learn the posterior directly from paired physically consistent data (s, O) ∼D, as described next. 3 DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY Our objective is to learn the posterior over scenes given the measurements a network already collects. The difficulty is not only that the posterior is high-dimensional, but that a model trained directly on raw channels would have to solve two problems at once. It would have to discover, from finite data, how geometry writes itself into a channel — which structures produce which delays, which angle of arrivals, which signal blockage — and simultaneously learn to generate a coherent scene consis- tent with that mapping. The first of these is the physics of propagation and is shared across every link; the second is the inference problem specific to a given set of observations. Entangling them enforces supervision on rediscovering the underlying physics, and leaves the generative model to place mass over a combinatorially large space of grids with no guidance about where the plausible scenes lie. We therefore propose a hierarchical framework, illustrated in Fig. 1, that separates these two burdens. The first stage learns the shared physical characteristics once: a multi-modal founda- tion model is pretrained to aggregate channel and positional information into a representation that is explicitly aligned, during pretraining, with the scene structure that shaped the observation. Each measurement is thereby encoded into a summary of the scene properties that could have produced it. The second stage performs the inference: the encoded representations of many observations of the same environment are fused as the conditioning set of a diffusion model that learns the posterior over occupancy, and a consensus over its samples yields the reconstructed scene. Because condi- tioning is expressed in a representation space that is aligned with geometrical properties rather than raw channel information, the generative model just resolves which scenes are consistent with the measurements rather than first inverting propagation. 3.1 PHYSICS-GROUNDED REPRESENTATION LEARNING In this section, we discuss the design and implementation of PHOENIX (a Physics-grounded Heterogeneous Observation Embedding for Neural Inference across wireless conteXts), a multi- modal foundation model that learns a unified representation of the physical information governing wireless propagation. The overall pipeline of PHOENIX is illustrated in Fig. 2. At its core, PHOENIX employs a single-stream multi-modal transformer-based feature embed- ding framework to learn global, robust representations of the wireless channel by capturing cross- modality associations among the input data covering the wireless medium, the transmitter-receiver positioning, and the spatial-temporal characteristics of the received signal. Specifically, the chan- nel tensor in the input is converted into amplitude and phase and tokenized over antenna pairs and neighboring subcarriers; the height map is partitioned into non-overlapping patches; and the receiver location is projected to a single token. Each token additionally encodes its physical origin through antenna/subcarrier indices or normalized spatial coordinates, together with a modality embedding.
