Because the weight vectors are summed, an encoding whose trained code words reinforce coherently should require a factor of 𝑓(1)∕𝑛for 𝑛trained values, where 𝑓(1) is the factor required by a single trained value. Thermometer, Johnson and the hybrids stay close to this, exceeding it by at most 4 to 21% depending on the encoding, whereas Binary requires up to 4.03 times that value, at 𝑛= 3. The excess is the “don’t care” cancellation of Section 3.1.2, measured directly: where trained code words disagree, their summed weights cancel, and the gain that compensates for the loss admits the false positives. Gray, though also free of the cliff, does not stay at the coherent value: its factor rises to 1.61 times 𝑓(1)∕𝑛at 𝑛= 8. Its weak global structure is already costing gain, even where it has not yet cost accuracy; Section 4.5 shows the same trend at a wider range. Where the accepted values fall matters as much as how many there are. Every untrained value accepted by Ther- mometer and by the linear hybrid at 𝐵 = 4, the only encodings other than Binary to accept any, lies within four semantic units of the trained cluster, at a median distance of under three: these are neighbouring activations. Binary’s lie at a median of 8.9 and reach 142 units away, with only a quarter falling near the cluster, and are false positives in the ordinary sense. Where the structured encodings accept untrained values at all, they generalise over the neighbour- hood of what they were taught; Binary responds to unrelated values. Gray’s perfect accuracy here deserves comment, given its poor global structure. The trained set is a tight contiguous cluster, precisely the regime in which local smoothness suffices; Gray’s weakness appears in Section 4.2, where the trained values are far apart and its false positives are scattered. Local smoothness is enough for local clustering, but not when the network must discriminate across the range. 4.3.1.
