Text-guided image editing. Early text-driven manipu- lation largely steered pre-trained GAN latent spaces via vision-language models [11, 36]. The advent of diffu- sion models broadened semantic coverage and synthesized complex scenes more flexibly. Subsequent approaches im- proved source-image preservation and fine-grained con- trol through noise perturbation and trajectory inversion [5, 10, 19, 33, 34, 46], alongside spatial blending and cross- attention steering [1, 3, 7, 14, 45]. Recent efforts ex- tend inference-time control to continuous-time flow mod- els through structural layer control [4], flow inversion [20, 40], inversion-free sampling trajectories [23], and text- embedding control for continuous editing [18, 21]. Comple- menting inference-time methods, instruction-tuned archi- tectures learn a direct mapping using synthetic pairs [6], hu- man annotations [59], and multi-task supervision [13, 42]. Emerging state-of-the-art transformer-based diffusion and flow backbones further push visual quality and composi- tional reasoning, demonstrating unprecedented instruction fidelity and fine-grained spatial control [25, 26, 49, 50]. De- spite these single-step capabilities, isolated edit accuracy does not prevent error propagation, identity degradation, or semantic drift when applied iteratively across extended edit- ing sequences. Multi-turn image editing. Maintaining image quality and content consistency across successive edits is a cen- tral challenge in multi-turn image editing, as errors can accumulate over the editing sequence. Agentic frame- works tackle this challenge through planning, memory, and reflection [41, 54], while training-free methods improve consistency through inversion and attention control [62], high-frequency feature preservation [27], inversion shar- ing [38], and low-frequency latent alignment [47]. Other approaches learn sequential editing directly: VINCIE learns in-context editing from video-derived sequences [39], while AnchorEdit combines supervised causal multi-turn train- ing with self-generated histories and identity-anchoring memory [52]. Reinforcement learning methods optimize trajectory-level editing rewards [16] or jointly optimize history-dependent reasoning and image generation [55]. More recently, MT-OPSD distills clean-conditioned editing behavior onto states generated through identity-edit roll- outs [61], which accumulate errors while keeping the in- tended content fixed. Our training instead follows the edit- ing process as it unfolds during inference: successive se- mantic edits with evolving content and preservation require- ments. Through distribution matching, we align the stu- dent’s evolving output distribution, modeled by a learned auxiliary editor, with the target distribution defined by a teacher conditioned on the original image and cumulative instructions, without paired intermediate targets. 2
