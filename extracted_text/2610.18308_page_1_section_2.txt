User simulation has emerged as a complementary methodology in which artificial users interact with a CRS in place of humans, enabling controlled, repeatable exper- imentation at scale (Balog and Zhai, 2025; Zhu et al., 2024b). Early simulators were rule-based or agenda- based (Ruß et al., 2012; Afzali et al., 2023), of- fering controllability at the cost of behavioral real- ism. Large language models (LLMs) have changed this picture, but LLM-based simulators still exhibit characteristic failure modes: repetitive responses, narrow choice ranges, binary reactions, and drift to- ward generic “average user” behavior (Yoon et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024a). We argue that an impor- tant source of these limitations is the user profile that conditions the simulator. The profile bottleneck. Most LLM-based simu- lators condition generation on profiles built from observable signals: ratings, interaction histories, and demographic attributes. Such profiles capture what a user has consumed but leave largely unspec- ified how the user makes choices: whether they satisfice or maximize, whether their choices are driven by mood or social context, and how will- ing they are to accept unfamiliar recommendations. These behavioral dispositions strongly shape con- versational behavior yet are precisely what ratings- based profiles omit (Tigunova et al., 2019; Yoon et al., 2024). A natural alternative—prompting an LLM to infer behavioral attributes from a static profile—has a known failure mode: LLMs tend to produce stereotyped inferences, inflating apparent profile richness while reducing individual fidelity. The same risk applies to self-play dialogue, where two agents can amplify rather than reveal latent attributes, fabricating plausible-sounding traits not grounded in actual behavior. Our approach.
