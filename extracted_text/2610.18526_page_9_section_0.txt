Limitations Our analysis treats retrieval disagreement as a di- agnostic signal, not as an objective that should al- ways be minimized. Different languages may legit- imately retrieve different documents when corpora are not fully parallel, when relevance judgments are incomplete, or when the translated queries em- phasize different aspects of the same information need. This is especially important for Multi-EuP, whose documents are not translation-equivalent and whose relevance judgments are graded rather than one-to-one. We therefore interpret disagree- ment on Multi-EuP as evidence of behavior under realistic multilingual competition, not as a direct se- mantic mismatch in the same sense as on Belebele and XQuAD. The interventions require language identifiers and an external multilingual estimation corpus. We use FLORES to avoid fitting directions on the evaluation index, but this assumes that the language offsets and disagreement directions esti- mated from FLORES transfer to the target domain. Domain mismatch, noisy language identification, code-switching, and languages absent from the esti- mation corpus may reduce reliability. The steering method is training-free, but it still requires esti- mating one direction per language and choosing a scale; these choices may not be optimal for all models, domains, or retrieval depths. We evaluate single-vector dense retrieval at the fi- nal embedding layer. We do not study sparse-dense hybrid retrieval, rerankers, late-interaction models, or full retrieval-augmented generation pipelines, where later components may compensate for or amplify the disagreements observed here. We also focus on top-20 retrieval, which is appropriate for common retrieval and RAG settings but does not fully characterize deeper ranking behavior. Finally, our interventions are diagnostic rather than definitive fixes. Mean removal, subspace re- moval, rotation, and disagreement-guided steer- ing reveal different aspects of the geometry, but none removes multilingual retrieval bias uniformly. In particular, improving recall, improving nDCG, reducing own-language crowding, and increasing cross-language agreement are not the same objec- tive. This trade-off is part of our finding, but it also limits how directly any single correction can be deployed as a universal solution. Ethics Statement This work mitigates a fairness-relevant failure, the unequal treatment of query languages in multilin- gual retrieval, and its gains fall most on the lowest- served languages. Two caveats: our steering cor- rection can raise the English share of results on non-parallel data, which a language-sensitive de- ployment should weigh; and it assumes the query language is known, so noisy language identifica- tion could redistribute rather than remove bias. All datasets are public and contain no sensitive infor- mation. The environmental cost is low: the method trains nothing and runs in minutes on cached em- beddings.
