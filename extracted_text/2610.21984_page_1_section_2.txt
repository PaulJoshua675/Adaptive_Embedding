Autonomous driving planning requires selecting ego trajectories that are safe, rule-compliant, and comfortable under complex multi- agent interactions [2, 6, 8, 11, 13]. Despite rapid progress in learning- based planning, robust closed-loop decision making in dense ur- ban scenes remains challenging [2, 6, 13]. A core challenge is that planning performance depends not only on average-case trajec- tory realism but on learning from a small set of planning-critical trajectories—those arising in highly interactive, safety-sensitive, and rule-constrained scenarios such as merging, yielding, and in- tersection conflicts [2, 6, 13]. In practice, however, most training trajectories are semantically flat, weakly constrained, and low- conflict, contributing limited signals to the decision boundaries that matter most for planning [6]. This long-tail structure makes pure imitation learning insufficient, since abundant easy trajectories dominate supervision, while naive reinforcement learning is also inadequate because generic rewards are difficult to design stably for complex multi-agent interactions. These challenges motivate graph-structured relational modeling to prioritize planning-critical trajectories and support planning-oriented policy improvement. Existing methods improve planning from different angles, but the core issue remains unresolved.
