APPENDIX V WARM-START VERSUS TRAINING FROM SCRATCH Section III-B describes the two-stage procedure: each warm-started target expert is initialized from a pre-trained sphere expert’s complete training state. Here, we compare it against training the same object from scratch. A. Setup and Matched Budgets We compare warm-started and from-scratch experts for six objects: a 100 × 100 × 200 mm prism, cubes of side 140 and 200 mm, a ∅140 × 160 mm cylinder, and spheres of diameter 140 and 160 mm. We warm-start these experts from policies pre-trained on spheres with diameters of 200– 220 mm. All share one training recipe and the randomization of Appendix III-E, and both conditions switch to the second- stage settings of Appendix III at 15,000 total iterations. Budgets are matched at 50,000 total iterations. The warm- started expert is charged its pre-trained sphere policy’s 15,000 iterations plus 35,000 on its own object, so it reaches the comparison point having seen its target object for 15,000 fewer iterations. All six are compared on all fourteen routes. B. Results by Body Region Table IX breaks the comparison down by body region, and Fig. 11 shows the corresponding training progress. All six warm-started experts have higher mean regional route completion, although some regional scores are lower. Shoul- der completion is higher for every warm-started expert. The prism has the largest aggregate difference, from 23.30% to 50.12%, including loop completion of 0.8% versus 18.2%. For cube 200, mean completion across the four back routes is 0.3% from scratch and 12.5% with warm-starting. Both conditions have 0.0% loop completion. Other regions show smaller differences or regressions. The warm-started cylinder has lower back and loop completion, and the ∅160 mm sphere has lower underarm, back, and loop completion despite its higher overall mean. C. Training Cost The 15,000-iteration sphere pre-training cost is shared by all experts initialized from that policy. For N target experts, each post-trained for 35,000 iterations, the average budget is 35,000 + 15,000/N iterations per expert, compared with 50,000 iterations per expert trained from scratch. Counting the six objects here with their two pre-trained sphere policies, the total is 240,000 iterations against 300,000, a 20% reduc- tion in aggregate iterations with all six final means higher. Representative runs on eight NVIDIA H100 GPUs use 15,000 sphere pre-training iterations at approximately 270 H100 GPU-hours, followed by 35,000 post-training iterations per object at 660–815 H100 GPU-hours. Training an expert from scratch for 50,000 iterations costs approximately 1,030 H100 GPU-hours. The shared pre-training contribution is therefore approximately 270/N H100 GPU-hours per target. In these runs, the sphere also has the lowest cost per iteration, complementing its shorter pre-training budget. The sphere’s compact contact patches produce fewer simultaneous con- tact constraints, reducing the simulator’s contact-solver cost. These are GPU costs for the runs that produced these experts. Figure 11 shows several warm-started experts crossing the from-scratch endpoint well before their own budget is spent (the prism after 10,000 target iterations and both spheres within 15,000), suggesting additional savings if post-training stops at matched regional route completion. The initialization transfers the actor, critic, optimizer state, observation normalizers, and curriculum progress together.
