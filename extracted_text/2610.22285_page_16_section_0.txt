• No explanation (Uninformed, Informed): “In each query you will see two trajectories side by side. Click ‘Prefer A’ or ‘Prefer B’ for the one you prefer.” • Explanation (Uninformed + Explanation, Informed + Explanation): the same instructions, followed by “The robot is uncertain about how to handle [feature]. Please focus on this feature when comparing the two trajectories,” where [feature] was “distance to the laptop” or “distance to the human” depending on the task. Preference queries were presented through a PyBullet interface that played the two candidate trajec- tories side by side (Fig. 6). Participants chose between them with “Prefer A (left)” and “Prefer B (right)” buttons, and could re-watch both trajectories as often as they wished with a “Replay” button. Condition order was counterbalanced with a Williams Latin square of size four. The order of the two tasks within each condition was independently counterbalanced so that, within every condition, the participants split evenly between laptop-first and human-first. After each task, participants answered the six 7-point Likert items in Table 1; after each condition, they completed the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) [31]; and at the end of the session, they ranked the four conditions from most to least preferred. We analyzed the objective measure with a 2×2 repeated-measures ANOVA (afex [32]) with prior and explanation as within-subjects factors and partial eta-squared (η2 p) as the effect-size measure. We supplemented the ANOVA with Tukey-corrected pairwise con- trasts among the four conditions, computed on the estimated marginal means (emmeans [33]) with within-subjects Cohen’s dz. These contrasts showed that Informed + Explanation significantly out- performed Uninformed (p < 0.001, dz = 5.84), and that Informed (p = 0.003, dz = 1.36) and Uninformed + Explanation (p = 0.001, dz = 1.55) each significantly outperformed Uninformed, indicating that the prior and the explanation each contribute measurable gains and that combining them produces the largest gain. Informed and Uninformed + Explanation did not differ from each other (p = 0.976), suggesting that the prior alone and the explanation alone provide comparable benefit over baseline. For the subjective measures, each negatively-worded Likert item was reverse-coded and averaged with its positively-worded partner to form the three two-item scales (ease, understanding, and expression). Each scale was analyzed with its own 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA. Beyond the explanation main effects in Table 1, the prior main effect was not significant for any scale (ease: F(1, 11) = 2.20, p = 0.166; understanding: F(1, 11) = 0.83, p = 0.382; expression: F(1, 11) = 1.07, p = 0.323), and the prior × explanation interaction was significant only for ease (F(1, 11) = 6.67, p = 0.025, η2 p = 0.378; understanding: F(1, 11) < 0.01, p = 0.961; expression: F(1, 11) = 0.17, p = 0.690). Perceived workload was measured once per condition and summarized as the unweighted mean of the six subscales on a 0–100 scale. We used a 2×2 repeated-measures ANOVA and supplemented it with Tukey-corrected pairwise contrasts on the estimated marginal means. These contrasts localized the workload reduction to the two explanation conditions. Uninformed + Explanation (M = 17.6, p = 0.044, dz = 0.89) and Informed + Explanation (M = 17.2, p = 0.045, dz = 0.89) each significantly lowered workload relative to Uninformed (M = 24.7), whereas Informed did not (M = 25.1, p = 0.999, dz = −0.05). 16
