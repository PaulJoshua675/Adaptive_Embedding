Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) adapts pre-trained language models to follow instructions via cu- rated prompt-response pairs (Ouyang et al., 2022). English SFT resources span a continuum from fully human-written data—such as FLAN (Long- pre et al., 2023), Dolly (Conover et al., 2023), and Open Assistant (Köpf et al., 2023)—to hy- brid pipelines combining human annotation with synthetic generation, as in Tülu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024). Recent large-scale synthetic ef- forts such as Nemotron (Blakeman et al., 2025) and Smoltalk (Allal et al., 2025) demonstrate the scalability of automatically constructed instruction datasets while explicitly addressing licensing and provenance through permissively licensed sources and models. High-quality multilingual SFT re- sources for European languages remain compar- atively limited, and existing datasets often face restricted licensing or provenance concerns due to reliance on proprietary generative models. The Aya initiative (Singh et al., 2024) provides the broadest multilingual effort to date, though its wide cover- age results in limited per-language depth and vari- able quality in automatically generated portions. EuroLLM (Martins et al., 2024), Tülu 3 (Lam- bert et al., 2024), Salamandra (Gonzalez-Agirre et al., 2025), and Apertus (Apertus et al., 2025) each advance multilingual or open post-training in different respects, yet all include components gen- erated or processed with proprietary models, lim- iting their suitability for provenance-constrained training. The Smoltalk multilingual extension rep- resents one of the few systematic attempts to con- struct permissively licensed multilingual SFT data through translation and open-model generation. Many existing SFT datasets, despite declaring permissive licenses, are built on human-authored prompts paired with responses generated by propri- etary models, which restricts their use in commer- cial settings. Therefore, we erased such content and regenerated it using open-weight, permissively licensed LLMs. Depending on the source dataset, we employ a translation pipeline, where existing (English-centric) datasets are converted into mul- tilingual ones, or a regeneration process, where synthetic content is re-generated from scratch with license-compliant language models. 3.1 Dataset selection criteria Rather than aggregating large volumes of generic instruction-response pairs, we focus on diversity and high quality.
