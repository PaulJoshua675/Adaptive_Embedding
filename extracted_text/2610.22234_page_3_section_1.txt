Hybrid GUI/Code Computer-Using Agents. Traditional GUI-native agents learn screenshot-to- action policies over visual interface states (Qin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Wu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025d), whereas command-line and coding agents operate over files, shells, and programmatic state (Anthropic, 2025; OpenClaw, 2026; Yang et al., 2026b; 2024). Their complementary strengths motivate hybrid computer use. Framework-centric systems (Gonzalez- Pumariega et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2026a) coordinate GUI, code, memory, and recovery through external harnesses, often using strong proprietary models. Their performance is coupled to costly inference and hand-engineered orchestration, making the model’s own hybrid coor- dination difficult to isolate. Tool-call-based approaches (Jia et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2026) expose programmatic operations through predefined, application-specific interfaces, limiting coverage and transfer to available integrations and tool schemas. Some approaches integrate GUI, code, and other capabilities through model fusion (Wang et al., 2025a; Qin et al., 2025), relying on predefined fusion rather than coordination learned within a single policy. Qwen-UI-Agent (Zhou et al., 2026a) also explores training with mixed GUI and code-like action capabilities, demonstrating the practical potential of native hybrid agents. However, existing studies describe data construction, executable environments, supervision, and training configurations at different levels of granular- ity, making controlled reproduction and comparison of learned coordination mechanisms difficult. Our work complements by studying executable application–artifact state construction, decoupled instruction–evaluator synthesis, and more stable optimization for training a single GUI/code policy. Environment Scaling and Task Synthesis for Computer-Using Agents. Realistic long-horizon tasks and interactive environments are essential for training CUAs, yet manual task design and trajectory annotation are expensive and difficult to scale. This makes automated acquisition of high- quality instructions and demonstrations a central problem. One line of work crawls web tutorials and documentation and decomposes them into executable task instructions (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026a). Another line starts from the target environment itself and infers tasks from what the agent can observe or manipulate: Explorer (Pahuja et al., 2025) extracts and refines task in- tents from web entry pages, while OS-Genesis (Sun et al., 2025), GUI-ReWalk (Lin et al., 2025), TreeCUA (Jiang et al., 2026), and AUTO PLAY (Ramrakhya et al., 2026) synthesize tasks through exploratory interaction with the environment. These methods primarily scale task and trajectory synthesis, but they do not substantially scale the underlying interactive worlds, which constrains the diversity of behaviors learned by the agent. Unlike more constrained interaction domains such as web and mobile (Cao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026c; Wu et al., 2026; Tang et al., 2026b; Cheng et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026), PC environments, such as Ubuntu, Windows, and macOS, provide a natural substrate for scalable CUA training through system-level code execution, mature applica- tion ecosystems, persistent file systems, and package managers, enabling verifiable tasks grounded in concrete system state (Sun et al., 2026a; Fu et al., 2026). Recent work has begun to use vir- tual machines to expand desktop environments and synthesize tasks (Wang et al., 2026a; Lv et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026), exploring different parts of desktop-state construction and task genera- tion. Training Paradigms for Computer-Using Agents. CUA training has broadly evolved from imitation-style supervised fine-tuning toward reinforcement learning in interactive environments. SFT-based systems learn action formats, visual grounding, and task execution from human demon- strations, synthetic trajectories, web tutorials, or teacher-model rollouts (Cheng et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026a; Sun et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). Between SFT and online interaction, offline preference-optimization methods such as DPO (Gao et al., 2026) and offline GRPO (Yang et al., 2026c) improve policies from static preference data, but remain limited by the coverage of collected trajectories and cannot directly optimize recovery under envi- ronment feedback.
