To evaluate generalization across different design styles within the same technology node, we test on 7 nm lay- outs from two sources as described in Section 5.2: synthetic layouts and layouts extracted from a RISC-V BOOM core design. Results are reported in Table 1 and Table 2, respectively. In these benchmarks, the ratio of conflict edges to vias is close to 2:1, significantly higher than the 1:2 ratio reported in prior work [6], reflecting the denser conflict relationships typical of advanced-node designs. As shown in Table 1, on synthetic layouts our method achieves about 45× speedup over EILP with only 0.1% increase in cost. On the BOOM layouts (Table 2), our method still provides about 15× accel- eration with around 1.0% cost increase. These results demonstrate that our approach maintains substantial runtime advantages while preserving near-optimal solution quality across different design styles at the 7 nm node. DSA-QPL (5 nm). To evaluate cross-node generalization, we test our method on 5 nm industrial layouts using DSA-QPL. The re- sults are summarized in Table 3. The GNN model employed shares identical weights with that used in Tables 1 and 2, without any retraining or fine-tuning on 5 nm data. Despite the domain shift in both technology node and number of masks, our method still achieves approximately 12× speedup over EILP with only 2% in- crease in cost, further validating the cross-node transferability of the learned GNN model. Performance on Challenging Layouts. Table 4 reports cases across all test sets where existing methods exceed the 20-hour time limit. Our method remains tractable on these challenging instances, whereas EILP or DLXplus exceed the time limit without producing feasible solutions. Even for the instance where DLXplus produces a feasible solution (N5_test_11), our method still achieves a 4.2× lower cost within less than half the runtime. The experimental re- sults demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of our proposed method on layouts that are challenging due to either large scale or complex conflict structures. Generalization Analysis. Our GNN model is trained on 20 synthetic layouts containing about 60,000 vias at the 7 nm node. The model generalizes well across multiple dimensions: (1) to larger layouts, e.g., layouts with over 200K vias, (2) to unseen layouts from diverse origins, including the BOOM core and industrial designs, (3) to a different technology node (5 nm), and (4) to a different mask count (𝐾= 4). This strong generalization mainly stems from our model operat- ing on ILP representations rather than layout-specific geometric fea- tures. The input features are derived from the ILP formulation and the solver, including objective coefficients, constraint biases, con- straint coefficients, and solution-pool frequencies. These features reflect how variables influence the objective and constraints dur- ing optimization, providing solver-level information that is largely independent of the specific layout topology or design style. In addition, the bipartite graph representation of the ILP together with message passing allows the GNN to operate on graphs of vary- ing sizes, enabling the model to transfer to larger layouts. Finally, our framework adopts a GNN-guided local refinement strategy, where the GNN predicts vias that are likely to require reassignment, while the remaining variables are fixed to their initial assignments. The ILP solver then determines the final mask assignments by opti- mizing only over the selected subset of variables. Since the actual mask assignment is still decided by the ILP solver, the solution quality is preserved while the search space is significantly reduced, which further contributes to the robustness of our framework.
