Verification checks whether the goal has been achieved before termination, but such post-hoc remediation can lead to irreversible consequences and costly retries. These limitations motivate a natural design of on-the-fly supervision, where an external agent monitors execution and steers the system back toward its goal when drift occurs, complementing planning and verification. Such supervision is conceptually straightforward but challenging for the LLM backbone. It requires steering intelli- gence, the ability to assess execution against the overall goal, determine when intervention is needed, and generate actions that steer systems back on track. Such a capability is established in other goal-directed systems, such as di- rect supervision in organizations (Mintzberg, 1979), supervisory control of automated systems (Sheridan, 1992), and metacognitive monitoring and control in human cognition (Nelson & Narens, 1990). We bring this principle to LLM agent systems by building a steering agent and improving its steering intelligence through post-training. Specifically, we define an observation space that exposes the task goal and execution context of the agent system to the steering agent. During execution, each observation update triggers a decision about whether and, if needed, how to intervene. These two demands motivate a natural design with fast and slow paths (Kahneman, 2011; Su et al., 2025; Lampson, 1983; Clark et al., 1989). The fast path estimates steering priority through a low-cost single-token prediction. A threshold on this priority controls activation of the slow path, which leverages more costly reasoning to generate steering actions. By separating fast judgment from deliberative generation, this design balances monitoring efficiency and steering effectiveness. We then improve the steering intelligence of the steering agent by jointly training the two paths with reinforcement learning, combining a pairwise ranking objective (Burges et al., 2005) for fast-path steering judgment with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) for slow-path action generation. We evaluate our steering approach on information-seeking search tasks (Mialon et al., 2024; Ho et al., 2020) in both single-agent and multi-agent settings. Our single-agent experiments show that steering is challenging even for a capable LLM backbone, and that reinforcement learning substantially improves steering intelligence. Across seven question-answering benchmarks, zero-shot steering yields a performance gain of only 1.84 points, whereas the RL- optimized steering agent improves performance by 6.66 points with only 0.93 steering actions per task. Furthermore, the steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, transfers to unseen executors, and supports controllable intervention frequency without retraining, with steering at the highest frequency raising the gain to 17.11 points. In the multi-agent setting, we introduce the steering agent into a five-role multi-agent system (Xu et al., 2026). In this more challenging steering task, where zero-shot supervision degrades performance, the RL-trained steering agent achieves a gain of 5.34 points. Our contributions are summarized as follows. • We characterize goal drift in single- and multi-agent systems, where local objectives can displace the overall task goal, and motivate on-the-fly supervision to keep execution on track. • We develop a steering agent with fast intervention judgment and deliberative action generation, and improve its steering intelligence through reinforcement learning. • We conduct experiments on both single- and multi-agent settings, where the steering agent demonstrates effective performance gains, strong generalizability, transferability, and controllability. 2
