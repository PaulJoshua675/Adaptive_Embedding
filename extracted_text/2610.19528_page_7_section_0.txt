We also conduct ablations to study the effect of different components like LLM backbones and evaluators in Appendix B. 4.2 RQ1: Does Selective Retrieval Improve Translation? Tables 1, 2, and 5 (see in Appendix A.1) show a consistent advantage of selective retrieval across idiom, knowledge-intensive, and document-level translation. SRAT1 achieves the strongest COMET, task-specific LLM-judge, and MQM results across all settings. Compared with the strongest SFT base- lines, SRAT improves I5 by 0.096 and MQM by 1.27 points on IdiomKB Zh→En, GRF by 0.019 and MQM by 0.82 points on RAGtrans En→Zh, and GRF by 0.020 and MQM by 0.42 points on Document-MT-LLM. The gains are particularly consistent on metrics that assess semantic adequacy and multidimensional translation quality. Learning when to retrieve matters. The com- parison with SRAT-N and SRAT-A isolates the value of the retrieval decision. Across all three benchmarks, SRAT consistently outperforms both never- and always-retrieve policies on semantic and LLM-judged metrics. Relative to SRAT-A, MQM improves by 20.6% and 17.4% on the two IdiomKB directions, by 8.0–12.8% on RAGtrans, and by 5.4% on Document-MT-LLM. These gains are achieved with substantially different learned retrieval rates: 30.6–77.0% on IdiomKB, around half of the inputs on RAGtrans, and 88.6% on Document-MT-LLM. This variation suggests that the policy adapts retrieval behavior to different translation settings rather than learning a fixed pref- erence for retrieval. Moreover, SRAT-N already provides substantial gains over the unfine-tuned model, while SRAT further improves upon it, in- dicating complementary benefits from RL-based translation learning and selective retrieval. This is expected because our RL reward does not include BLEU, but instead directly optimizes COMET and task-specific LLM judges, whereas supervised fine-tuning on refer- ence translations can favor surface forms closer to the reference. BLEU measures n-gram overlap with a limited set of references (Papineni et al., 2002) and may under-reward valid paraphrases or more natural translations. Prior studies have therefore cautioned against treating BLEU alone as decisive evidence of translation quality (Callison- Burch et al., 2006; Mathur et al., 2020), and human- preferred translations can receive lower BLEU when their wording differs from literal or transla- tionese references (Freitag et al., 2020). We there- fore treat BLEU as a complementary lexical metric and place main emphasis on COMET and LLM- based evaluation. Additional analyses and qualita- tive cases on this are provided in Appendix E.4. 4.3 RQ2: When Does Utility-Aligned Retriever Training Help? We next examine whether the retriever can be better aligned with translation utility. To valiate retrieval quality, we additionally calculate retrieval accuracy: for each retrieval action, we ask Claude Sonnet 4.6 to judge whether the retrieved document is cor- rect and useful for translating the source, given the query, source, reference, and retrieved document. Retriever training is most beneficial when the un- derlying retrieval task is difficult. On IdiomKB, the default similarity retriever already achieves about 95% retrieval accuracy, and joint or contrastive training improves it only to 97–98%, yielding lim- ited downstream gains. On RAGtrans, the default retriever reaches only 56.3% and 72.6% accuracy on En→Zh and En→De. Contrastive training in- creases these to 70.7% and 81.4%, while improving MQM by 6.8% and 7.4%. This shows that utility alignment is particularly valuable when semantic similarity is an insufficient proxy for evidence use- fulness. We show such cases in Appendix E.3. Contrastive training also consistently outper- forms joint optimization. On RAGtrans, it im- proves MQM over joint training from −4.41 to −4.27 on En→Zh and from −5.89 to −5.54 on En→De. This supports our motivation in Sec- tion 3.3.2: joint training shares one trajectory re- ward across many token-level decisions but only a few document selections, weakening retriever credit assignment.
