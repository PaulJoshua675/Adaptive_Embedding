Our work connects research on graph-modified embeddings, feedback-based and multi-hop retrieval, and graph-based retrieval. We also draw on evidence-level evaluation to assess whether these approaches recover the passages needed to answer a question. Encoding graph structure into embeddings. Retrofitting adapts pretrained embeddings using external relational knowledge. Faruqui et al. [3] introduced an objective that balances fidelity to the original embeddings with attraction between connected concepts. Counter-fitting adds attraction and repulsion constraints [4], while Functional Retrofitting introduces relation- specific transformations for heterogeneous knowledge graphs [5]. Retrofitting has also been applied beyond word embeddings, including multilingual sentence representations aligned through Abstract Meaning Representation [13] and sense representations specialised using WordNet relations [14]. Related approaches use entity descriptions and language models to construct representations for knowledge-graph completion, incorporating structural information through different training objectives or architectures [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Another line of work derives node representations from graph connectivity. DeepWalk learns embeddings from contexts generated by truncated random walks [20], while node2vec uses biased walks to capture different network neighbourhoods [21]. These methods are conceptually related to our random-walk transformation, which instead averages existing textual embeddings at walk endpoints without learning new representations from walk sequences. Simple Graph Convolution separates graph-based feature propagation from a learned classifier [22], providing a related perspective on our neighbourhood-based aggregation of existing embeddings. We draw on these ideas to study graph-modified embeddings for evidence retrieval, rather than intrinsic similarity, node classification, or knowledge-graph completion. In particular, we compare their use as direct search representations with their use as intermediate signals in a multi-stage retriever. Feedback-based and multi-hop dense retrieval. Pseudo-relevance feedback refines a query using the results of an initial retrieval stage and then performs a second search. NPRF (neural pseudo-relevance feedback) brought the idea into learned ranking, feeding the top-ranked documents of a first pass into a neural ranking model [8]. More recent approaches apply the same principle to dense retrieval, where the query is a vector rather than a set of terms, and differ in what that vector is allowed to be. ColBERT-PRF keeps a multi-vector query and extends it, extracting representative embeddings from the pseudo-relevant documents and adding them to the query representation before re-retrieval [9]. ANCE-PRF keeps a single query vector but learns an encoder that consumes the initially retrieved passages, leaving the document index unchanged [10]. Multi-hop retrieval extends this sequential approach to questions whose supporting evidence cannot be identified from the original query alone. Multi-Hop Dense Retrieval conditions successive searches on previously retrieved passages to recover chains of supporting evidence [23]. Other approaches use generated text to guide subsequent retrieval. IRCoT interleaves retrieval with intermediate chain-of-thought reasoning steps [24], while Iter- RetGen alternates retrieval and generation, using each generated output to inform the next retrieval iteration [25]. Our method uses offline graph-modified embeddings to guide intermediate retrieval, followed by a Rocchio-style update that blends the query with the mean embedding of retrieved nodes in the original space [26], without query-time graph access or LLM generation.
