All of these read the user request as a trusted input, and all are trained or evaluated on English agent-safety benchmarks [16–19]; none reports sensitivity to the request’s language. We evaluate the four released ones. Cross-lingual safety. Safety classifiers and refusal training degrade in low-resource languages: translating harmful prompts into low-resource languages bypasses GPT-4’s safeguards [14], and multilingual jailbreak rates track language resource level [10]. Multilingual safety benchmarks and moderation tools [22–25] likewise vary the language of the content being judged. Here the content (the tool call and the trajectory) is held fixed and only the language of the principal’s request changes, which isolates the guard’s use of the trusted channel. Prompt sensitivity. Multi-prompt evaluation shows that paraphrasing an instruction shifts LLM scores and sometimes rankings [6, 7], and formatting alone can move accuracy by tens of points [21]; Rabanser et al. [8] measure paraphrase robustness of agents. Our English paraphrase conditions replicate this design for guards, and serve here as the control that shows the language effect is not a wording effect. 3 Setup Benchmark. TS-Bench AgentDojo-Traj [2] contains 1,220 labelled tool-invocation steps (868 safe, 352 unsafe) from 589 AgentDojo [1] trajectories across banking, Slack, travel and workspace environments, with 58 unique user instructions. Each step consists of the user instruction, the history (prior thoughts, actions and observations), the candidate action, environment metadata and a label. We use the released labels unchanged and execute no tool call. Languages. We translate only the instruction field into German, French, Turkish, Swahili and Amharic. The languages were chosen a priori by resource tier in the taxonomy of Joshi et al. [9]: German and French are class 5 (the largest non-English data languages), Turkish class 4, Swahili and Amharic class 2; Swahili is also the low-resource language of MultiJail [10]. The five span five families (Germanic, Romance, Turkic, Bantu, Semitic) and two scripts (Amharic is written in Ge’ez), and German and Turkish are read by the authors, so the pre-registered translations could be human-reviewed. The translation hypothesis and the first three languages were pre-registered; the low-resource pair extends the design with the same generation and validation pipeline and a frozen file before any guard saw it. Translations are produced by Gemini 3.8 Flash (T = 0.3, first of three candidates passing all checks) and must preserve quoted strings, file names, e-mails, URLs, IBANs, dates, times and amounts verbatim, must not add urgency, permission or scope language, and must stay within length bounds. Controls. original and placebo (a trailing space and collapsed double spaces, the tokenisation- noise floor); six English paraphrase conditions: programmatic keyboard-adjacency typos (8% of eligible words, slot spans protected), four LLM styles (lexical, terse, verbose-polite, casual; Gem- ini 3.8 Flash, T = 0.7) and one human paraphrase per instruction written before seeing any LLM output. The paraphrases are held to the same slot and scope checks as the translations. Validation. Every accepted row was judged for task equivalence by two cross-family judges (Claude Sonnet 5, judging translations on its own back-translation; Gemini 3.1 Pro). Every one of the 580 pre-registered rows was read by a human (0 rejected, 1 repaired); the 116 low-resource rows were accepted or repaired from the back-translation under a fixed rule (Appendix D; 4 Amharic rows repaired). A materialisation check asserts that history, action, environment and label are byte-identical across conditions. Release.
