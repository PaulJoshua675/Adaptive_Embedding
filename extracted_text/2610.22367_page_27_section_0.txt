PRIME-FIELD MODULAR INVERSION We benchmark modular inversion within a complete arithmetic circuit: point addition on an elliptic curve over a 256-bit prime field. Each point on this curve is a pair of integers (x, y) satisfying the curve equation y2 ≡x3 + 7 (mod p), (51) where p = 2256 −232 −977 is a 256-bit prime number. The curve defines point addition, an operation that combines two points P and Q into a third point R = P +Q on the same curve (note that this addition is not the usual coordinate-wise addition). Geometrically, one draws the line through P and Q, finds its third intersection with the curve, and reflects that point across the horizontal axis. Algebraically, for the inputs considered here, with Qx̸ = Px, one first computes the slope λ ≡(Qy −Py)(Qx −Px)−1 (mod p), (52) where (Qx −Px)−1 denotes the inverse modulo p. The coordinates of the sum are then Rx = λ2 −Px −Qx, Ry = λ(Px −Rx) −Py. (53) We study the reversible point adder [52]: given an input point P and another, classically supplied point Q, it performs |P⟩7−→|P + Q⟩. (54) The modular inverse required by Equation (52) is the dominant cost of point addition: once the slope λ is known, the remaining coordinate updates require only a fixed sequence of relatively cheap modular additions and multiplications. In contrast, modular inversion follows a long input-dependent sequence of arithmetic transitions, which the circuit must later recover to reverse the computation and clear its ancillas. Prior point-addition constructions [8, 38] use the binary greatest common divisor (gcd) algorithm for modular inversion. The connection between gcd and inversion comes from expressing the gcd in terms of the original inputs. Because p is prime and w = Qx −Px̸ = 0, there are integers α and β such that gcd(p, w) = 1 = αp + βw. Reducing this identity modulo p gives βw ≡1, so β ≡w−1 (mod p). The extended binary-gcd algorithm finds these coefficients by tracking expressions for the two current values u and v as integer combinations of p and w. Binary gcd rests on two elementary identities. If u is even and v is odd, then gcd(u, v) = gcd(u/2, v); if u and v are both odd, then, for u > v, gcd(u, v) = gcd((u −v)/2, v).
