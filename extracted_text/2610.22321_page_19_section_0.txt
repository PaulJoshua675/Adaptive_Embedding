arXiv preprint B.3 HUMAN ANNOTATION AND SCORE CALIBRATION In human annotation, we found that score-only judgments showed noticeable subjective variation and made disagreements difficult to verify. We therefore design a 39-label taxonomy that asks anno- tators to record explicit consistency states and failure types before score calibration, reducing uncon- strained subjectivity while preserving observable information about event presence, timing, audio roles, and cross-modal relations. The taxonomy follows the same three dimensions as AVC-Reward: global labels cover narrative, affective, and environmental consistency; temporal labels separately describe physical interactions, speaker events, and music/rhythmic events, including missing, hallu- cinated, off-screen, and narrative-layer cases; fine-grained labels describe material, intensity, spatial, motion, and causal-response attributes. Table 7 summarizes the complete annotation space. Annotation protocol. Annotators judge the three dimensions independently. Within each dimen- sion, all applicable failure labels can be selected, allowing a clip to contain multiple inconsistency types or multiple affected events. For temporal synchronization, annotators additionally distinguish physical-layer on-screen events, plausible off-screen events, and narrative-layer audio such as voice- over and background music. A severe global conflict can be marked as unjudgeable for a later event- level category when the corresponding relation cannot be meaningfully assessed, but this does not otherwise propagate the global judgment to the other dimensions. For each annotated sample, three annotators independently provide the dimension labels. The train- ing and test sets are strictly disjoint. The held-out test set is additionally assigned to annotators who do not participate in labeling the training set, preventing overlap in both samples and annotators between model training and final human evaluation. Label-to-score calibration and score rewriting validation. Human annotations provide detailed categorical supervision, while AVC-Reward requires continuous targets for the three dimensions. Directly collecting continuous scores for all training samples introduces annotation subjectivity and variance, making large-scale score supervision difficult to verify and maintain consistently. There- fore, we investigate whether human-verified labels can serve as an intermediate representation for recovering continuous scores. We first study the relationship between human-verified labels and continuous scores on AVC- Benchmark, where both label annotations and human score annotations are available. We analyze whether label configurations consistently correspond to similar human scores. Pairs sharing the same human-verified label configuration have an average absolute score difference of only 0.0321 (median 0.0111), compared with 0.2002 (median 0.1000) for pairs with different configurations (Table 8). This indicates that label configurations strongly constrain human scores and motivates label-to-score calibration for continuous reward construction. Based on this observation, we further investigate whether a large language model can learn the label- to-score mapping through few-shot calibration. Specifically, we extract 10 representative label-to- score mappings from the AVC-Benchmark annotations and provide them to Qwen3-235B as few- shot demonstrations for score rewriting. Given these demonstrations, Qwen3-235B maps each label record to (Smac, Stemp, Sattr) and a diagnostic conclusion. The prompt specifies label semantics, failure severity, multi-label handling, and output format; details are provided in our project’s GitHub repository. We evaluate the rewriting procedure on AVC-Benchmark samples with human scores, excluding the manually selected few-shot examples used for prompt construction. As shown in Table 9, the rewritten scores preserve human ordering across all three dimensions; the averaged score achieves PLCC 0.780 and ∆-Pairwise@0.2 of 0.905. We further evaluate score rewriting in the score-distillation reward space used for GRPO (Table 10). Rewritten scores achieve rdistill = 0.853, compared with 0.881 for human leave-one-annotator-out references. The corresponding reward-space discrepancies, measured by 1 −rdistill, are 0.147 and 0.119, respectively. These results suggest that rewriting errors are comparable in magnitude to the observed inter-annotator variability, with a modest additional discrepancy of 0.028 in the reward space directly relevant to GRPO supervision. 19
