Sensitivity to Span-Preserving Edits Natural-text boundary agreement does not determine how segmentation responds to edits. We construct paired mark and base substitutions within the same multi-scalar cluster, each replacing one scalar with another of the same script association and UTF-8 width while preserving NFC and every grapheme span. At least one substitution changes Bwen’s patch count in 1,786 of 3,400 items (52.5%). A count change establishes a segmentation change, although an unchanged count can conceal moved boundaries. Bolmo-1B and Bolmo-7B show instability rates of 38.6% and 38.4% on their matched bank (Table 6). Their insta- bility indicators agree on 842 of 850 items despite a 5.2× parameter difference. The pattern recurs across separately trained checkpoints from the same group and conversion recipe, with the same parent tokenizer. Because text, targets, and models are not varied independently, this agreement does not identify which factor determines the response. Across language aggregates, bytes per patch correlates pos- itively with instability in each checkpoint (r from +0.620 to +0.764). Short average patches do not ensure stability: Bolmo-1B has 1.284 bytes per patch in Shan with 66% in- stability, and 1.075 in Odia with 16% instability. Mark–Base Corruption Contrasts Using the same span-preserving edits, we examine whether conversion changes the relative penalty for corrupting a mark versus a base. The probe replaces a mark (Unicode category Mn or Mc) or a base letter (Lo) within the same cluster; both conditions therefore alter cluster content. Eli- gible replacements are distinct scalars of the same UTF-8 width drawn from the language’s script-associated inventory, and must preserve the first parent token and all grapheme spans. The selected 200 parallel source sentences yield 3,400 items per parent/conversion pair. Each pair shares an item bank, while the banks differ between pairs. For the representation-path cost ℓ, define margins m = ℓ(xmark) −ℓ(x) and b = ℓ(xbase) −ℓ(x). The primary statistic, R = m/b, compares mean mark and base margins. The secondary statistic, I = mean[(b −m)/(|b| + |m|)], averages the normalized contrast over items for which it is defined. All reported mean base margins are positive, so larger R indicates a larger mean mark penalty relative to the mean base penalty; R can exceed one. Smaller I indicates greater relative mark penalties under item-level normaliza- tion. The summaries weight items differently and can move in different directions. Substitution frequency, orthographic legality, and contextual predictability also affect these penal- ties, limiting their interpretation as measures of character processing. The Qwen/Bwen difference is +0.0177 with 95% interval [+0.0074, +0.0283] (Table 7); its item-contrast difference is −0.0201 [−0.0287, −0.0118]. For OLMo/Bolmo, the estimates are +0.0064 [−0.0080, +0.0214] and −0.0048 [−0.0164, +0.0061], respectively. Both second-pair inter- vals contain zero, and the ratio interval also contains the first pair’s point estimate. Separate tests against zero neither establish equivalence nor test whether the two pairs differ. Language-level ratio differences agree in sign in 8 of 17 lan- guages (Appendix G).
