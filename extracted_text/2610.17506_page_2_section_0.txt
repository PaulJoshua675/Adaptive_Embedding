Interaction in human group problem-solving has been shown to yield both performance gains and efficiencies. Interacting dyads can achieve greater outcomes than either of their best individual performances (Bahrami et al., 2010; Berger et al., 2026), and groups can also speed up decision-making by minimizing independent cognitive exploration (Almaatouq et al., 2021; Dreyer et al., 2025; Szary & Dale, 2013, 2014). At the same time, interaction may also suppress the benefits associated with independent exploration (Janis, 2008; Rotter & Portugal, 1969). Classic findings in group performance show that interaction can impair collective outcomes relative to equivalent non-interacting groups, such as by reducing the number of unique ideas generated during category recall (Szary et al., 2015; Weldon & Bellinger, 1997). Yet other approaches suggest that independent aggregation, without interaction at all, may yield the best statistical outcome (Surowiecki, 2005). There are open questions about the underlying mechanisms driving these mixed observations. One approach to this puzzle is to investigate the underlying dynamics that interaction introduces to these collective decisions and the conditions under which such dynamics may facilitate performance. When humans interact they can exhibit interpersonal synchronization across multiple biome- chanical and behavioral dimensions, including gait, posture, physiology, gaze, head movement, and speech (Abney et al., 2014; Branigan, 2007; Brennan, 1996; Felsberg & Rhea, 2021; Hamil- ton & Konvalinka, 2026; Pardo, 2006; Richardson & Dale, 2005; Shockley et al., 2003; Trujillo et al., 2023; Yi & Palmer, 2026). Although the functional role of synchrony is debated, it has been argued to support joint action, prosociality, mutual understanding, and the formation of linguistic conventions (Dale et al., 2013; daSilva & Wood, 2025; Hawkins et al., 2023; Hove & Risen, 2009; Louwerse et al., 2012; Stolk et al., 2016). Consistent with this view, interpersonal synchrony has been linked to successful group problem-solving across multiple tasks (Abney et al., 2021; Dideriksen et al., 2023). By contrast, aggregating independent guesses has been shown to produce a collective response that is more accurate than any individual guess, what is sometimes called the "wisdom of crowds" (Surowiecki, 2005). This effect leverages a general benefit of statistical facilitation in certain problem spaces and was first demonstrated by pooling guesses of the weight of an ox (Galton, 1907). Empirical illustrations of this statistical effect in general knowledge domains show that just a few pooled guesses from independent individuals can improve precision (Ariely et al., 2000). These effects of statistical independence are sufficiently robust that some research has shown they can hold across multiple guesses from a single person (Vul & Pashler, 2008). Nevertheless, the potential for bias within underlying distributions of individuals can raise concerns about how to achieve optimal outcomes (Becker et al., 2017) and the effect is still subject to factors such as relative level of knowledge across a population (Bang & Frith, 2017). Although the general advantage of the wisdom of crowds is well-known, and cited as a canonical example of collective intelligence, it does not shed light on the cognitive role of human interaction. In the present study, we investigate the benefits of interaction in collec- tive intelligence on both overall performance and on computational efficiency by developing a novel computational model of interaction dynamics in collective intelligence. We first simu- late the finding that interacting dyads demonstrate a collective advantage in a joint perceptual decision-making task (Bahrami et al., 2010) to shed light on how interaction can improve per- formance beyond the baseline benefit of statistical facilitation.
