Resolution Expands An Approximation Space Having established that projecting a spatially dis- tributed contact field onto a scalar weight can remove or re-express higher-order interaction structure, we now invert that perspective. Instead of asking what structure becomes inaccessible when fine variables are projected away, we ask what becomes representable when those variables are progressively resolved. Here we map the complex physical geometry of a neu- ron onto the mathematics of approximation theory de- veloped above. A model can only construct functions from the variables and nonlinear features made available at its chosen ruler. A point-neuron description that ex- poses only a few input coordinates therefore operates in a smaller approximation space than a description that additionally resolves nonlinear combinations generated within distinct subunits. Refining the ruler by un-pooling distinct dendritic subunits does not change the target function. It enlarges the feature space in which that same target can be approximated. This is the computational counterpart of the coastline argument. A coarse ruler does not prove that the under- lying object has few degrees of freedom. Rather, it re- stricts the degrees of freedom available to the description. Conversely, resolving additional subunits can expose in- teraction terms that were absent from the coarse repre- sentation. The following constructions test that claim in two stages. We first compare point, shuffled-contact, and spatially organized models at fixed contact count. We then construct a genuinely nested hierarchy in which the same architecture is viewed at progressively finer res- olutions. This refinement also connects directly to the information-theoretic formalism developed above. The distinction between direct and higher-order information is defined relative to the coordinates made available to the model. For example, the interaction x1x2 is a joint dependence when the resolved coordinates are (x1, x2, . . .), but becomes a one-coordinate dependence if the refined description explicitly contains the subunit variable z12 = x1x2. Refinement can therefore move structure that appears in Ihigher at a coarse ruler into Idir at a finer one, without changing the underlying input– output law. 1. The target function and methodological Safeguards We begin with six independent continuous inputs, x ∼ U[−1, 1]6, and define the nonlinear target f(x) = σ{2.5[x1x2+x3x4+x5x6+1.2x1x3x5]−1.8} (39) Equation (39) contains three pairwise products, x1x2, x3x4, and x5x6, together with the third-order term x1x3x5. The target is therefore deliberately poorly matched to a model whose only features are independent first-order coordinates. It is not meant as a biophysical transfer function for a particular neuron. It provides a controlled function whose interaction structure is known before any model is fitted. The centered input distribution (U[−1, 1] rather than U[0, 1]) is an important methodological safeguard. For independent variables with zero mean, the raw product terms are orthogonal to the corresponding first-order co- ordinates under the sampling distribution. For example, the correlation between x1 and x1x2 contains the factor E[x2] = 0, and the same argument applies to the third- order term because the remaining factors also have zero mean. A linear model therefore cannot obtain an artifi- cial advantage merely because all inputs and all products share the same positive offset. This would not be true for inputs drawn only from a positive interval. If xi > 0 systematically, products such as x1x2 carry positive first-order correlations, allowing a linear readout to recover part of a nominally multi- plicative target from marginal amplitude alone. Cen- tering does not make Eq. (39) mathematically invisi- ble to every possible low-order statistic, especially af- ter the outer sigmoid. It does remove this elementary source of leakage and makes the comparison between first-order and interaction-sensitive representations sub- stantially cleaner. By centering the inputs symmetrically around zero, the linear coordinates are orthogonal to the raw product terms, preventing the point model from ex- ploiting mean-induced first-order correlations with those interactions. All models are evaluated on held-out samples rather than on their training error. The point of the experiment is therefore not whether a sufficiently flexible model can interpolate the target, but how efficiently different rep- resentations approximate it out of sample. 2.
