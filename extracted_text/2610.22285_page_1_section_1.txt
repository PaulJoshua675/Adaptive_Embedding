When a human teaches a robot how to do a task, not every relevant aspect of the task—or feature— gets equal attention. For instance, while teaching a robot how to carry a knife across your cluttered kitchen, you might do a good job avoiding the hot stovetop or the rack hanging overhead, but struggle to consistently keep the knife away from every person or obstacle. Because humans have limited working memory and can’t focus on everything at once [1, 2], some features get carefully attended to while others are left underspecified. Recent work shows how the robot can detect, directly from its teaching data, which features were poorly supervised or entirely irrelevant to the task [3, 4]. We go a step further and ask: how should the robot work with the human to resolve that underspecification? One approach is to ask the human for more demonstrations focused on the underspecified fea- tures [3]. But this places the burden on the human, who already struggled with attending to those features. We propose to shift this burden onto the robot: rather than ask the human to try again, the robot should itself ask targeted questions that resolve what it is uncertain about. Preference queries, where the robot generates behavior options and the human just picks their pre- ferred one, offer this kind of low-effort channel [5, 6, 7]. Active learning approaches select queries to maximally reduce uncertainty under a distribution over the human’s reward [6]. But when that distribution is initialized from imperfect teaching data [8], it inherits not just what the feedback got right but also its errors on the underspecified features.
