People ask AI assistants which products to consider, and companies, agencies and researchers measure which brands the answers name, usually by sending the same question several times and counting. Such a count is worth something only if it still describes the answers when someone acts on it, days or weeks later. Single answers vary from run to run [9, 11]; what matters for planning is whether the rate at which a question names a brand stays put. We treat this as a forecasting problem. How well do the answers collected to a fixed question predict which brands the same question names later? If a forecast built on past answers keeps its accuracy at a long horizon, the underlying rates held; if its accuracy decays, they moved. Proper scoring rules measure this directly [2, 4], and simple baselines separate stability at the level of a question from the general popularity of a brand. Earlier work in this line decomposed run-to-run variance in brand answers [11], measured what share of the brands and sources found by a long series of repeated queries a shorter series already recovers [12], and compared category ownership across models in two waves [13]. Outside it, studies of generative engines measured how content changes the visibility of sources in answers [1], showed that strategic text can raise a product’s visibility in model recommendations [7], and documented a preference of language models for global brands over local ones [6].
