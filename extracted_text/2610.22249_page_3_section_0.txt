The speaker’s side has a longer history in generation, where Gricean max- ims constrain referring expressions (Dale and Re- iter, 1995) and pragmatic speakers reason about a listener in reference games (Frank and Good- man, 2012; Andreas and Klein, 2016), though in tasks whose communicative goal is fixed in ad- vance. Studies of open-ended model writing in- stead focus on its characteristic expressions, in- cluding over-represented words (Juzek and Ward, 2025; Kobak et al., 2025), recurring syntactic tem- plates (Shaib et al., 2024), and broader measures of low-quality “slop” (Shaib et al., 2025), with sup- pression proposed as a remedy (Paech et al., 2025). These studies establish which expressions models produce too often, which is a matter of frequency. 2.2 Corrective Negation Corrective negation is well described in both En- glish and Mandarin, and these descriptions spec- ify when a speaker uses it appropriately. The con- struction denies one alternative and asserts another in its place. Work on English shows that it presup- poses alternatives contrasting along a shared di- mension and can target the form of an utterance as well as its content (Horn, 1985; Toosarvandani, 2013). Studies of bushi…ershi describe the same corrective function in Mandarin. Shao and Wang (2010) identify a pseudo-denial use, in which the negation does not retract any claim held in the con- text; Tan (2016) compares the construction with English not A but B and finds both semantic and metalinguistic correction in each; and Zuo (2025) analyzes ershi as the connective that lexicalizes correction, distinct from those for contrast and ad- versative relations. We turn these descriptions into conditions for assessing each use in model output. 2.3 Evaluator Bias The evaluators used to train and assess language models are biased toward surface features of re- sponses, and training can carry these biases into model output. Reward models and LLM judges favor longer responses (Singhal et al., 2024), list formatting (Zhang et al., 2025), and style more broadly (Wu and Aji, 2025), and LLM judges show position and verbosity biases (Zheng et al., 2023). Preference optimization also narrows the range of model outputs (Kirk et al., 2024), and open post- training pipelines that release intermediate check- points make it possible to trace such changes stage by stage (Lambert et al., 2024; OLMo et al., 2025). Unlike length or formatting, a corrective construc- tion’s appropriateness depends on context; the question is whether evaluators and training favor it only where it is called for. 3 Methods We propose a diagnostic framework that assesses each use of the construction by whether its con- text calls for it, rather than by how often it occurs. Its core is a decision tree, derived from correc- tive negation and Gricean pragmatics, that labels each occurrence as substantive, optional, or defec- tive. We pair these labels with frequency mea- sures, evaluator comparisons, and checkpoint anal- yses to trace the sources of the preference. 3.1 Analytical Criteria We operationalize contextual appropriateness through corrective meaning and discourse con- tribution. A descriptive reading approximates ¬pX ∧pY ; metalinguistic negation rejects wording or degree (Horn, 1985), while reframing replaces an explanatory perspective. Alternatives share a recoverable contrast dimension (Rooth, 1992; Toosarvandani, 2013), and their availability differs from a participant’s endorsement (Stalnaker, 2002). Grice’s Relation and Quantity maxims motivate separate tests of contextual licensing and informative contribution; Manner motivates checking interpretability (Grice, 1975). We assess contribution against the current question under discussion (QUD), which tracks the distinctions still open in the discourse (Roberts, 2012). For instance, the bottleneck is not the CPU, but disk I/O is licensed and informative when the user reports that upgrading the CPU did not help; it is unlicensed when nothing has raised the CPU, and redundant when the answer has already ruled the CPU out. 3.2 An Evidence-Based Diagnostic Tree The unit is one occurrence in its complete answer and available context.
