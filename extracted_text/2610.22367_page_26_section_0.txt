By Equation (48), the coefficients ξr of a null vector σ = P r ξrwr satisfy a three-term recurrence of their own, so each follows from the two before it. The circuit computes each coefficient while its basis vector is still stored and immediately adds ξrwr into a 48-symbol register, the locator register. When the walk ends, that register holds σ. Breakdowns and scheduled repairs. Lanczos removes the component of a vector v along a basis vector w by subtracting ρw, with ρ = (wTv)/(wTw). Over F64, however, a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself, which leaves this quotient undefined. Block Lanczos repairs such a breakdown by treating the consecutive Krylov vectors w, Aw, . . . , Ad−1w as one block and removing the components along all of them at once. This is possible once their Gram matrix, the matrix of their inner products, is invertible: Gd =  (Aiw)T(Ajw) d−1 i,j=0 =  gi+j d−1 i,j=0, gk = wTAkw. (49) We call d the depth of the block. The circuit supports depths two and three. A breakdown is rare, and the machinery that repairs it is expensive. An ordinary round of the walk costs 5,400 Toffolis; a round that can also repair at depth two costs 12,000, and one that can repair at depth three costs 17,000. A fixed circuit that repaired each breakdown where it occurred would carry this machinery in every round. The circuit instead concentrates it in scheduled repair rounds. It conditions the ordinary update on g0 = wTw̸ = 0. This test is cheap: in characteristic 2, wTw is the square of the sum of the components of w, which needs only CNOTs. On an input with g0 = 0 the update gates do not act, and the walk state stays frozen until the next repair round, which forms the block and advances the walk past it in one step. The walk has 52 rounds, four more than the dimension, so that delayed inputs can finish. Of these, 17 carry repair machinery. An input that needs a deeper block, or that has not finished after 52 rounds, fails. Ending and reversing the walk. For a given input, the walk ends when g0 = g1 = g2 = 0, because no block of depth up to three is then invertible. The circuit freezes the walk there and records the round, as the divstep circuit does (SI II.1). After the last round, it tests which of three cases holds and routes the corresponding vector into the locator register. If w = 0, the Krylov vectors have become dependent, and the accumulated vector is the null vector. If w̸ = 0 and Aw = 0, then w itself is a null vector. If Aw̸ = 0 and A2w = 0, then Aw is a null vector. An input in none of these cases fails. After the phase scan described below, the circuit must undo the walk. It first measures the locator register in the X basis. The walk built that register by adding multiples of basis vectors, so the reverse walk can correct the phase left by the measurement as it retraces the rounds: each addition becomes a set of controlled-Z gates conditioned on the measurement outcomes, at no Toffoli cost. The reverse walk recomputes most of the decisions of the forward walk, such as its zero tests, while the vectors they depend on are present. One decision per round cannot be recomputed in time: whether the round advanced the walk, which controls an exchange of the two stored basis vectors. The forward walk stores that decision in one history qubit per round, 52 in all, and the reverse walk clears them. The reverse walk uses 52,000 fewer Toffolis than the forward walk. Error values. The locator gives the possible error points, and the phase also needs the error values. The circuit uses two facts, both counterparts of the Reed–Solomon decoder (Table S2). First, there is an evaluator ω whose coefficients are bilinear in the locator and the syndromes, as ω = σS is for Reed–Solomon codes. Second, the error value at a simple zero of the locator is ej = ω(Pj) σ′(Pj), σ′ = ∂xσ + x8∂yσ, (50) the counterpart of Forney’s formula (30). Here σ′ is the derivative of the locator along the curve. Differentiating Equation (41) in characteristic 2 gives dy = x8dx, so a step dx along the curve changes σ by σ′ dx. The evaluator is ω = σ bS, where bS is the counterpart of the syndrome polynomial S(z): a series whose leading coefficients, in decreasing weighted degree, are the 123 syndromes [51]. The locator comes from a 48-dimensional space and is not necessarily the minimal-degree locator. It can therefore also vanish at a point that carries no error, or have a repeated zero at an error point. At a point with no error, ω vanishes as well and the point contributes nothing. At a repeated zero the quotient becomes 0/0. As in L’Hôpital’s rule, the error value then comes from the first few coefficients of the expansions of ω and σ at that point, which we call local coefficients. The circuit supports repeated zeros up to multiplicity three. Striped phase scan. For a curve point Pj, write cj for its six mask bits and ej for its error value. As in the Reed–Solomon decoder, the mask bits define µj ∈F64 with ⟨cj, ej⟩2 = TrF64/F2(µjej), and part of the phase is linear in the syndromes and costs only Z gates. The residual constant eµj vanishes at 128 points, which leaves 384 points to scan.
