Our evaluation covers the popular SAT (Ray et al., 2025), CV-Bench (Tong et al., 2024), BLINK (Fu et al., 2024), MMSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2026), RefSpatial-Bench (Zhou et al., 2025a), Where2Place (Yuan et al., 2024), Point-Bench (Cheng et al., 2025), RoboSpatial VQA and pointing (Song et al., 2025), ERQA (Gemini Robotics Team et al., 2025), EmbSpatial (Du et al., 2024), and VSI-Bench (Yang et al., 2025d). For model-design ablations, we use a fixed 72K-QA mixture of 25K SAT choice questions, 22K VST-P length questions, and 25K RefSpatial point questions to reduce training cost while providing sufficient supervision for meaningful comparisons. Each model is trained for 2.2K updates using the same optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and image-token budgets. VST-300 serves as the development set for length estimation. 4.2 Main Result Our main evaluation tests the central premise of MJev-Spatial: a System 1 decision model can make accurate spatial judgments with low inference latency. We therefore assess prediction quality and latency jointly, comparing MJev-Spatial with seven autoregressive VLMs. MJev-Spatial achieves the highest average score of 65.64%, ahead of 62.79% for Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, and ranks first on seven of the twelve benchmarks (Table 2). It also outperforms its autoregressive base model on all twelve benchmarks, including gains of 22.89 percentage points on RoboSpatial VQA and 12.50 points on RefSpatial-Bench. These improvements span both spatial question answering and point localization. For length estimation, the final model reaches an MAE of 0.103 m on the VST-300 development set. On an NVIDIA B200 GPU, MJev-Spatial also achieves the lowest mean query latency, 160.6 ms, yielding a 2.5× speedup over the fastest autoregressive baseline and a 5.6× speedup over its base model (Table 11). The com- bination of the highest average score and lowest mean latency gives MJev-Spatial the strongest performance– latency trade-off among the compared models (Figure 1). These results support our System 1 approach to spatial understanding: adapting pretrained multimodal knowledge into direct classification decisions that deliver both strong prediction quality and fast responses. 4.3 Ablation Studies We next examine the design choices that make direct classification effective across spatial tasks. The studies follow the prediction process: we first assess the shared candidate-scoring mechanism, then examine how numerical discretization and visual refinement provide the precision needed for value and point prediction. We further study how decoding adapts these predictions to different output requirements, including multiple points. This progression connects the common classifier to the task-specific decisions behind the final model. Pretrained candidate scoring. We compare native Q/K scoring with a typical linear classifier and a KEV (Palmer, 2026) pointer using newly initialized projections to assess whether adding a prediction head improves over reusing the pretrained projections (Table 3). The results show no consistent winner across tasks: native Q/K achieves the best RefSpatial accuracy, 51.00%, and the lowest length-estimation MAE, 0.3341 m, while the position classifier performs better on Where2Place and RoboSpatial pointing. We therefore favor native Q/K for its minimal changes to the pretrained architecture: it reuses the existing projections without adding a prediction head or new trainable parameters.
