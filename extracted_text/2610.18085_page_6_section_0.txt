Proofs for this section are in Appendix C. 4 SAGGS REBALANCING No standard optimizer keeps two equivalent models equivalent under a shared learning rate (Theorem 5); thus, the position of a model on its orbit affects training. SAGGS leaves the optimizer unchanged and instead periodically moves the model along its orbit to the balanced point, where the two matrices of each exact pair have equal Frobenius norms (Proposition 1). The current function stays the same, and training continues from balanced pairs. Hard SAGGS makes this move at once (Sec. 4.1); its damped variant, Soft SAGGS, makes only part of the move at each rebalance so as not to disrupt optimizer state that has adapted to the imbalance (Sec. 4.2). 4.1 Hard SAGGS Hard SAGGS applies the balancing map of Proposi- tion 1 to every exact pair once every Tbal optimization steps (Algorithm 1). Each application shrinks the larger matrix of a pair and enlarges the smaller one by the same factor, so that both norms become the geometric mean of the two; in Figure 1(4), the rectangle becomes a square of the same area. Because each application is an exact orbit move, the forward map and the current data loss are unchanged at each rebalance. Lion, for example, takes the sign of a combination of the current gradient and a momentum buffer, an expo- nential moving average (EMA) of past gradients (Chen et al., 2023). After a hard rebalance, the gradient of the shrunken block grows and the gradient of the en- larged block shrinks (Eq. 2), while the buffer keeps the old scales, so the sign of the combination, and hence the update, can change abruptly. In a Lion ab- lation without imposed rescaling, hard SAGGS was less consistent across seeds, whereas state-covariant SAGGS, which also rescales the buffer by the same factors as the gradients, removed most of this variation (Appendix F.5). To avoid abrupt changes in the update without mod- ifying the optimizer state, Soft SAGGS makes each correction smaller: At each rebalance, Soft SAGGS moves each pair only part of the way to its balanced point, so the gradient scales change in several smaller steps rather than in one jump.
