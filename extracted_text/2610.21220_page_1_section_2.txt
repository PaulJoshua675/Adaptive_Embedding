Modern recommender systems optimize multiple, often conflicting objectives beyond a single metric [13, 15, 21, 29, 46, 48]. While exist- ing multi-objective recommender systems address these trade-offs during training, they typically enforce fixed preferences at infer- ence time [16, 24]. In practice, however, the preference weights on objectives are often dynamic, shifting across users’ or systems’ requirements. This motivates Controllable Multi-Objective Recom- mendation (CMOR), which enables dynamic adjustment of objective trade-offs at inference time without retraining [32]. Research on CMOR can be categorized into two paradigms based on the stage of intervention: post-processing and in-processing ap- proaches. Post-processing approaches, such as rule-based reranking like MMR [3] intervene by adjusting the initially generated recom- mendation lists. While being model-agnostic, their performance is constrained by the fixed candidate set, often leading to a narrow scope of trade-off solutions. In-processing focuses on parameter- level modulation, primarily through parameter generation. These parameter generation approaches learn the mapping from user preferences over multiple objectives to the parameter space of rec- ommendation model by employing generative architectures, such as hypernetworks [4, 11, 24] or diffusion models [34]. Despite deliv- ering superior performance, these in-processing approaches inflate the training cost with a massive number of additional parameters from the generative modules and incur non-negligible inference latency, while often suffering from optimization instability. This raises a critical open question: why not reuse existing rec- ommendation models to efficiently achieve controllable trade-offs, rather than constructing new parameters from scratch? Recent ad- vances in large language models offer a promising answer: model merging, which adjusts model behavior through weighted merging of existing model parameters according to multi-objective prefer- ences, eliminating the need for retraining or additional parameter- generating modules [14, 25, 26, 37, 41]. This paradigm is particularly well-suited for recommender systems, where multiple indepen- dently trained, objective-specialized sub-models (e.g., for accuracy or diversity) commonly coexist in industrial training pipelines [2, 7]. Rather than discarding these costly pre-trained checkpoints, model merging offers an efficient mechanism for reusing and combining their expertise.
