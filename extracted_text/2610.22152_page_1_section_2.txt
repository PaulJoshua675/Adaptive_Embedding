A reinforcement learning agent optimizes exactly the reward signal it is given, not the intention behind it. When that signal is a proxy standing in for a true objective that is harder to specify directly – a sensor reading in place of a physical state, a classifier score in place of an outcome – optimization pressure can push the agent toward the proxy/true gap rather than closing it. This failure mode, specification gaming, has been documented extensively across both simulated and real-deployed systems [Krakovna et al., 2020], and recent work has begun to formalize what distinguishes it from ordinary reward misspecification [Skalse et al., 2022]. Much of the existing evidence for specification gaming is observational: a catalogue of cases in which an agent was found, after the fact, to have exploited a reward function in an unintended way. Comparatively less work pairs a controlled, deliberately constructed proxy/true gap with a mitigation that carries a stated, verifiable guarantee rather than only an empirical improvement. This gap matters for deployment: knowing that a fix tends to work is weaker than knowing precisely what it guarantees and under what conditions.
