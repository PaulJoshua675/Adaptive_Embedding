On the G1, this subdivision yields named torso regions (Fig. 7), which the downstream training reuses to name goals, but the mechanism is generic. We showcase a variety of whole-body grasps across arms, legs, and the torso in Fig. 3. Leg grasps would require learning manipulation jointly with balance and locomotion, so we use only arm and torso grasps for WBD-EX training, giving the mode set M = {L, B, R}: left-arm, both-arm, and right-arm grasps. In a single-arm mode, the other arm must release at the goal, and in mode B, both arms must contact it. After defining our desired contact links and body sub- regions, we minimize the differentiable force-closure resid- ual [15] over the object’s signed distance field, drawing contacts onto the object surface and keeping links out of the object and each other. Candidates are initialized from randomized object placements and optimized in GPU batches as done in large-scale dexterous hand grasp dataset genera- tion [16], [17], [48]. Because the G1 has only two arms and a large torso to grasp against, its grasps depend heavily on gravity and contact-link friction. Therefore, to produce the highest- quality grasp dataset, we aggressively filter candidates in simulation. For each candidate, the object is pressed into contact and held under 4× gravity while a sustained wrench disturbance is swept over the six axis directions. The candi- date passes only if contact persists. Survivors are then eval- uated again under standard gravity without disturbance and must drift less than 10 cm and rotate less than 45◦, producing the settled pose used at WBD-EX episode initialization. We index accepted grasps by robot joint position, grasp mode, and settled object position, and drop grasps whose object mesh sits farther than 5 mm from the torso surface or below a size-dependent height threshold (Appendix II-D). This concentrates the set on stable holds across the body, giving WBD-EX higher-quality grasps to transition between. B. WBD-EX Training We train WBD-EX to continually transition between ob- ject positions and grasp goals using proximal policy opti- mization (PPO) [49]. We formulate this problem as a Goal- Conditioned Markov Decision Process (gcMDP) defined by the tuple (S, G, A, R, P, ρ0, γ), where S is the state space, G is the goal set, A is the action space, R is the reward function, P is the transition probability, ρ0 is the initial state distribution, and γ is the discount factor. The robot seeks a policy πθ(a | s, g), parameterized by θ, that maximizes the expected discounted return J = Eπθ  P∞ t=0 γtrt  over control steps t, where s ∈S, g ∈G, a ∈A, and rt = R(st, at, g). Given the grasp dataset D = {(qi, XO i )}Ng i=1 for Ng grasps of an object, where qi is the settled robot joint configuration and XO i is the settled object pose (position and orientation) expressed in the torso frame, we voxelize grasps at 2 cm resolution by settled object position and group each voxel’s grasps by grasp mode. Each occupied (voxel, mode) pair is a node n ∈V of a goal graph, and D(n) ⊆D denotes the grasps in that voxel with that mode. A transition between nodes is legal only if their modes are compatible: L and R each connect to themselves and to B, but never to each other, so every handoff passes through a both-arm hold. We initialize each episode from the grasp dataset by sampling a mode uniformly, then a voxel carrying that mode uniformly, then gstart ∼U(D(nstart)), where U denotes the uniform distribution. Goals are commanded toward a far goal whose mode, then voxel, are drawn uniformly. Each commanded goal advances toward it: we draw a goal distance d ∼U(0.02, dmax) in meters and command the legal-mode node within d of the current node that is closest to the far goal, excluding the current node, with ggoal ∼U(D(ngoal)). A new far goal is drawn on arrival. With probability 0.2, the command is instead a hold, which asks the policy to keep the object at its current position and mode. To encourage human- like repositioning, in which objects roll and slide across body regions between arm handoffs, success depends only on the object’s position, its grasp mode, and the object coming to rest there. Unlike [11], our goals and rewards exclude the goal joint configuration and object orientation. We apply a goal-distance curriculum by ramping the ceiling dmax from 2 cm to 8 cm. Because each goal distance is drawn from [2 cm, dmax], increasing dmax introduces pro- gressively farther goal transitions while retaining closer ones. This exposes the policy to both gradual and more aggressive object repositioning behaviors as training progresses. A goal is completed only when the object settles at the goal under the correct grasp mode (Appendix III-C). For a single-arm goal, the inactive arm must register no object contact at the goal position. Requiring the inactive arm to fully release pushes the policy to learn stable and complete arm-to-arm handoffs around the body. We impose object- contact requirements throughout, but permit brief loss of the required arm contact within a 0.48 s grace window so the policy can execute the fast, dynamic reconfigurations that whole-body re-grasping demands rather than being forced into purely quasi-static holds.
