For 3D content, this requires identifying which requirements are unmet and explaining why. We study this step through 3D verification, which checks a candidate against explicit requirements and explains contradictions. Despite progress in general visual verification (Zhang et al., 2026b), the reliability of such feedback across diverse 3D tasks remains underexplored. Realizing this goal in 3D, however, involves challenges beyond those of image-based verification. Native 3D perception requires specialized representations and language alignment, as illustrated by point-cloud language models (Xu et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025); these capabilities cannot simply be assumed in an image-based MLLM. Although multi-view rendering makes 3D assets accessible to such models, it provides partial, projection-dependent observations rather than the complete geometry. A verifier must consequently associate parts across views, resolve occlusion, and distinguish geometric differences from changes in viewpoint or appearance. For grasping, identity, and motion, it must further establish whether the specific constraint holds, rather than merely recognize the object or action. The two cases in Figure 1 illustrate this distinction: GPT-6 Astra recognizes a rightward collapse but misses its unnatural motion, and accepts identity preservation despite a changed chin and face outline. These failures motivate both a systematic assessment of current verification ability and supervision that connects judgments to the relevant 3D evidence. Motivated by these challenges, our work addresses three connected questions, from measuring the capability gap to supporting its development and examining its downstream value. How far are current MLLMs from human-level 3D verification? We introduce 3DVeri- fierBench, a human-reviewed benchmark with 2,581 examples across ten complementary tasks. A common answer-and-explanation interface covers object properties, parts, relations, affordance regions, grasps, identity, reference geometry, and motion.
