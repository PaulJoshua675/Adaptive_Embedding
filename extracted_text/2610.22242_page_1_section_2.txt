Many decisions hinge on when an event will happen: how long will a patient survive after a cancer diagnosis? When will a customer churn? When will a machine fail? Survival analysis answers such questions by predicting, for an individual with features x ∈Rd, the survival function S(t | x) = P(T ⋆> t | x), that is, the probability that the event has not occurred by time t (Kleinbaum and Klein, 2012; Machin et al., 2006; Wiegrebe et al., 2024; Jeanselme et al., 2024). What sets survival analysis apart from regression, aside from strictly positive predictions, is censoring. A patient who leaves a clinical study alive after three years does not reveal their time of death, only that it exceeds three years. Discarding such partial observations biases predictions, and treating them as observed events is plainly wrong: a survival model must learn from both observed and censored events. In addition, survival predictions feed decisions (such as choosing a treatment given a five-year survival probability) and must therefore be calibrated probabilities rather than mere rankings of individuals (Goldstein et al., 2020; Lillelund et al., 2025; Alberge et al., 2026). Survival analysis is often a small-data problem. Many time-to-event datasets come from clinical studies, where observing events requires following patients for years, and cohorts of a few hundred individuals are the norm (Indrayan and Mishra, 2021; O’Donnell et al., 2025): in all the data we collect for this work, more than 70% of the datasets have fewer than 1,000 samples. In this regime, the flexibility of modern machine learning is a liability.
