How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? 8 2. Context-dependent influence and higher-order structure Consider one resolved contact k from neuron j onto a dendritic branch of neuron i. Its contribution cannot in general be specified from presynaptic activity alone. If the local branch is relatively hyperpolarized and away from a conductance or voltage-saturation regime, acti- vation of the contact can produce one incremental re- sponse. If coincident activity elsewhere has already al- tered the local voltage, recruited active conductances, or changed the balance of excitation and inhibition, acti- vation of the same contact can produce a substantially different response12–14 (e.g. the same spike from cell j at synapse k may generate zero additional depolarization). The relevant point is therefore not simply that synap- tic strength varies. It is that the effect assigned to one contact can depend on the state created by other con- tacts. Let Nε denote this fine-scale context: local mem- brane voltage, active conductance state, nearby synaptic activity, and other unresolved variables relevant at ruler ε. The contribution associated with w(k) ij is then con- ditional on Nε rather than being an independent term whose effect is fixed across states. This dependence can be stated directly in the language of the direct-family model introduced in Eq. (1). Let xk denote the state of contact k and let n denote the resolved local context. Define the context-dependent log- odds increment ∆k(n) = logit P(Y = 1 | xk = 1, n) −logit P(Y = 1 | xk = 0, n). (23) For the additive direct family in Eq. (1), ∆k(n) is in- dependent of n: the contribution assigned to xk cannot change with the state of another coordinate. If instead ∆k(n)̸ = ∆k(n′) for two accessible local states n and n′, then the conditional response contains an interac- tion that cannot be represented by an independent one- coordinate contribution from xk alone. Dependence of the conditional law on n does not by itself imply Ihigher > 0: such dependence may still be represented by a separable term un(n). The stronger condition in Eq. (23) is that the increment attributable to xk itself changes with context. If ∆k(n)̸ = ∆k(n′), no representation of the form uk(xk) + un(n) can repro- duce both increments. The true conditional law then lies outside the direct family, and by the conditional-KL rep- resentation in Eq. (8), the optimal direct approximation retains a positive residual, Ihigher > 0. This distinction gives a precise information-theoretic meaning to local dendritic context. A contact whose ef- fective contribution is invariant across the surrounding state can remain informationally direct even if its abso- lute magnitude differs across cells or locations. A contact whose contribution changes according to the configura- tion of other resolved variables instead participates in a joint dependence. The latter is the fine-scale structure that disappears when the local context is removed from the observer’s coordinate system. This is the microscopic version of the branch example in Section V. At the coarse ruler, neuron j may appear to exert one direct influence on neuron i. At the fine ruler, that influence can be distributed across contacts whose effects depend jointly on their dendritic location and on the activity surrounding them. The anatomical connection remains monosynaptic; the informational de- composition need not remain additive. 3. The point neuron as a coarse-graining map A point-neuron model does not observe r, the individ- ual elements of Wij,ε, or the local state Nε. It observes variables defined at neuronal resolution and seeks an ef- fective dependency between them. The corresponding somatic weight can therefore be viewed as the result of a coarse-graining projection, wsoma ij = Πε[Wij,ε, Nε]. (24) Equation (24) is deliberately schematic. The operator Πε stands for everything involved in passing from the resolved contact description to the effective somatic one: dendritic integration, electrotonic propagation, marginal- ization over unresolved local states, measurement, and finally the effective parameterization used by the coarse model. This makes clear what the scalar weight does and does not mean. The quantity wsoma ij need not correspond to any individual member of the contact field in Eq. (22). It summarizes their net predictive influence after the fine variables have been integrated out. Different mi- croscopic configurations of contacts and local states can therefore produce the same effective scalar, just as dif- ferent synaptic configurations in the branch-to-soma cas- cade produced the same coarse observable. To connect this projection to the information hierar- chy, collect the resolved variables into Xε = (Wij,ε, Nε) and define the coarse variable Xsoma = Πε(Xε). Because Xsoma is a deterministic function of the fine state, the data-processing inequality gives I(Y ; Xsoma) ≤I(Y ; Xε). (25) Thus the available information itself can only contract under this projection. Under the closure condition of Eq. (14), the absolute higher-order residual contracts as well, Ihigher(Z) ≤Ihigher(Xε). The scalar weight in Eq. (24) can therefore be read as an explicit realization of the coarse-graining map analyzed in Section III. From the perspective of the information hierarchy, this projection can remove precisely the distinctions required to expose context-dependent interactions. Structure that was visible as a dependence among resolved contacts may become absorbed into an effective direct dependency be- tween neuronal variables, while variation associated with unresolved fine states appears as conditional variability at the coarse scale.
