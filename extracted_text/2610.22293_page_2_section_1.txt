Large language models (LLMs) are useful across a rapidly growing range of applications, including assistants, coding, search, science, and finance [1, 8, 20]. Many of these applications follow the same paradigm. A small number of strong base models are pre-trained once [7, 49, 56], and each use case then fine-tunes one of them on specialized data [6, 15, 16, 36, 46]. Fine-tuning has thus become a standard way to specialize an LLM for a target task or domain. This paradigm comes at the cost of catastrophic forgetting. As a model learns a new task, capabilities acquired over trillions of training tokens, including instruction following, coding, reasoning, and general knowledge, can degrade severely [24, 28, 29]. This phenomenon is particularly difficult to address because the original pre-training data is typically unavailable [23], while post-hoc repair may recover only part of what was lost [28, 44] (Sec. 3). More broadly, forgetting stands in the way of continual learning. The next generation of foundation models should be able to improve over their deployed lifetime, adding new skills, domains, and corrections without sacrificing what they already do well. Recent works provide insights into the origin of this forgetting. The leading singular directions of pretrained weight matrices have been associated with important model functionality [30, 43], while reinforcement learning has been found to fine-tune relatively small parameter subnetworks [33]. More directly, RLVR updates concentrate outside the principal directions and induce relatively little spectral drift compared with SFT [60]. On-policy RL also stays closer to the base policy and forgets less than SFT [45]. These observations suggest a simple hypothesis. Preserving the principal singular subspaces of pretrained weights may preserve prior capabilities while leaving sufficient capacity elsewhere in weight space to learn new ones. Existing methods induce this behavior indirectly. For example, SDFT [44] trains the model on its own rewrites of the training data, so that supervision stays close to the model’s output distribution—approximately on-policy, and hence expected to forget less. This helps on non-reasoning instruct models, but we find substantial forgetting remains on longer reasoning traces (Sec. 3). We introduce Subspace Tuning (SST), which restricts weight updates to a non-principal subspace of the weight spectrum. Concretely, let ∆W be the optimizer’s final update to a weight matrix W, and let Uk and Vk contain the top-k left and right singular vectors of W. SST applies the projected update Π(∆W) = (I −UkU ⊤ k ) ∆W (I −VkV ⊤ k ) (1) in place of ∆W. The two projectors remove from the update any component along the principal directions, on the output and input sides respectively, so every applied step stays in the non-principal subspace (Sec. 2.1). Importantly, SST constrains the final optimizer update rather than the raw gradient. Stateful optimizers such as Adam rescale the gradient coordinate-wise before it reaches the weights, so a projected gradient does not produce a projected step and part of the update returns to the directions we mean to freeze (Sec. 2). Because the projection acts on the applied update, SST is optimizer-agnostic and composes with any fine-tuning recipe, with no other change to training. We conduct extensive empirical analysis and establish the following properties of SST (Sec. 3): • SST learns new tasks and retains prior capabilities. Despite protecting the top 99% of singular directions, SST matches the target-task accuracy of unconstrained fine-tuning, while retaining the prior capabilities that standard fine-tuning severely degrade (Figure 1). This behavior holds across Science, Medicine, Pathology, Finance, and Tool Use, across SFT and SDFT, and with both Adam and Muon; Tables 4 and 1 provide the full per-domain results. We hypothesize that such constrained learning is possible because LLMs are heavily over-parameterized [2, 22], leaving enough capacity in the non-principal subspace to fit a new task. When a fixed complement becomes restrictive, a refresh step recomputes the SVD on the current weights, realigning the protected subspace so adaptation can continue (Section 2.3). • SST scales well. With more training data, SFT forgets progressively more while SST stays flat and still reaches higher target-task accuracy (Figure 3).
