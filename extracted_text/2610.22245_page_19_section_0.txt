In sequential tasks, escalation decisions must be made repeatedly as the agent’s state evolves. TRIM [20] uses process-level feedback to decide when to replace a weak reasoning step with a strong-model step. R2V-Agent [17] learns step-level escalation from weak-policy episode outcomes by combining uncertainty statistics with task-specific verifier features trained via verifier-guided DPO. Alternatively, Inference-Time Distillation [41] determines escalation based on consistency among candidate actions to guide a weak agent. These methods typically rely on explicit step-level correctness labels, task-specific process verifiers, or expensive test-time consistency checks (which we benchmark via our multi-sample baselines). Our agentic experiments (Section 6.1) show that by learning from objective action disagreement, we can outperform consistency based alternatives and effectively route at the step level without ever requiring episode-success labels or step-correctness annotations. A.1.1 Population estimates of routing signals across varying weak-strong model pairs We extend the population-level comparison in Section 4.2 to the full routing-budget range B ∈[0, 1] for completeness and additional weak–strong model pairs and examine how the findings vary across models, datasets, and numbers of weak-model samples. Figure 4 reports the one-shot setting, while Figures 5 and 6 report results with N = 4 and N = 8 weak-model samples, respectively. The results support the main observations: corrected disagreement generally improves on raw weak– strong disagreement and closely approximates optimal routing when the strong model is accurate. Its advantage over weak-model correctness is most apparent at limited routing budgets; the ordering can change at larger budgets. We first report the one-shot setting (N = 1), then vary the number of weak-model samples N. While correctness flags when the weak model is wrong, it cannot anticipate whether the strong model will actually succeed. In practice, weak-model correctness is often viewed as a highly accessible baseline because the strong model can act as an automated judge to provide those labels. Conversely, obtaining ground-truth labels for the strong model’s own correctness is notoriously difficult because it typically surpasses available automated judges. Our disagreement scores bypass this labeling bottleneck entirely while explicitly capturing potential accuracy gains. At very high escalation budgets, weak-model correctness slightly surpasses corrected disagreement because it strictly avoids routing those rare instances where the weak model is correct but the strong model fails. However, in typical weak-to-strong deployments, these failure modes are infrequent, making low-budget efficiency the governing practical metric. A.2 Extended Finite-Sample Results Across Routing and Sampling Budgets We supplement Section 6.2 with detailed results on Math-500, Spider, and AceCode in Tables 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The tables include baselines omitted from the main-text plots, additional routing budgets, and N ∈{1, 4, 8, 16} weak-model samples per prompt. The results support the findings in the main text. On Math-500, self-consistency and semantic entropy approach corrected disagreement as the routing budget and sample count increase, and slightly outperform it at the 50% budget for N > 1. These consistency-based scores are less effective on Spider and AceCode, where grouping equivalent responses is more difficult.
