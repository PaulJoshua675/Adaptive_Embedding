S4 Frame structure of the Fe2S2 alias table. (a) The preprocessing places Pauli operators related by fixed Clifford frames into contiguous blocks. This groups the 41,052 table rows into 3,221 blocks that Select handles as units. Band height gives the number of terms in each block, and the labels give the frames used to generate them. Inset: the Fe2S2 cluster core (thiolate substituents omitted); the dashed line marks the iron-site reflection σFe. (b) One block of 32 Pauli operators generated from a representative operator using five frames. Each row of the left matrix specifies which frames are applied; a dark cell means that the frame named below its column is included. The center shows the resulting Pauli operator on the 40 system qubits, and the bar on the right gives its coefficient magnitude. The spin frames σαβ and σXY , shown in the first two columns, preserve coefficient magnitudes and therefore produce equal-weight groups of four. The other three frames need not preserve the coefficients. their alternate terms from two heavy terms A and B, an ordinary schedule might alternate A, B, A, B; ours prefers A, A, B, B. Rows 1–2 then share a threshold, an alternate term, and hence an alias word, as do rows 3–4. This strategy is particularly effective here because the two exact spin frames produce many groups of four equal weights (Figure S4(b)). The coefficients also span a broad range, so heavier terms can supply the missing weight of many lighter rows. Because the pairing only redistributes each term’s weight among buckets, it preserves the rounded distribution and creates long runs of identical alias words without breaking the frame blocks. Of the 20,374 σαβ-related row pairs it places next to each other, 18,463 store identical alias words. The alias-table QROM realizes this saving by pruning the binary tree over its address i. Whenever the two halves of a subtree contain the same sequence of alias words, the QROM visits one copy and skips the address bit that distinguishes them. For example, the eight-row pattern A, A, B, B, A, A, B, B consists of two identical halves, so the top address bit is irrelevant and the circuit looks up only A, A, B, B. Together, this scheduling and repeated-subtree pruning reduce the cost of the alias-table QROM to 10,000 Toffolis, compared with 41,000 for a row-by-row traversal and 13,000 for the baseline lookup with merged repeated alias words. The rest of the circuit follows the baseline. A comparator computes h, a controlled swap moves the selected index into place, and both are reversed after Select, for 64 Toffolis. The circuit then clears the keep and alt fields by MBU and corrects the resulting phase in one pass over the address i, for 782 Toffolis. We test every table row and a further 224 uniformly random inputs. The discovered circuit uses 15,000 Toffolis and 126 qubits, for a TQ-cost of 1.9 × 106. Relative to the baseline, this reduces the Toffoli count by 3.7×, the qubit count by 9.4%, and the TQ-cost by 4.1×. The amount of compression is specific to this Hamiltonian, but the preprocessing can search for such relations in other sparse Pauli tables, and it falls back to conventional Select and QROM costs when it finds none. Exact spin symmetry is common in spin-independent molecular Hamiltonians, so the scheduling strategy should carry over. The opportunity is smaller when a Zeeman term or spin–orbit coupling breaks the spin symmetries. In DFTHC, analogous choices arise in the factorization itself [69, 72]: the factorization is not unique, and a circuit-aware choice could make its coefficient data repeated or sparse and therefore cheaper to load.
