Therefore, pooling can help to average out independent noise for stable networks, but it seems that input noise cannot be averaged out when it is mixed with unstable dynamics. 3.4 Summary We show that the dyadic advantage reported by Bahrami et al. (2010) can be reproduced by coupled ESNs. When individual networks are prone to instability interaction can stabilize their dynamics, yielding distributed improvements and a superadditive benefit in collective performance. Networks were trained on stimuli and targets with an average expected value of zero, allowing read-outs to represent confidence over classification targets (with the most confident target class taken as a response). Pooling read-outs to generate coupled feedback therefore provides a simple mechanistic implementation of confidence sharing, hypothesized to underlie the original effect (Bahrami et al., 2010; Bang et al., 2014). Importantly, the model shows how confidence sharing shapes joint dynamics and identifies the conditions under which it produces a collective advantage. Nevertheless, the dyadic advantage requires two individuals to solve a shared problem, doubling the computational resources involved. One may ask if the performance gain persists when dyads are matched to individuals in total computational cost. This question is difficult to address in biological collectives, where adding members necessarily increases computational resources; however, our modeling framework allows computational cost to be controlled directly. In experiment two, we compare individual and ensemble ESNs of varying sizes while holding total computational cost constant. Because biological collectives generally coordinate through interaction rather than by aggregating independent processes, we test coupled ensembles to determine if distributed computation is more efficient even in the face of the added cost of coordination. Thus, we ask not only if "two heads are better than one," but if more heads are better for the price of one. 4 Experiment 2: Efficiency of Group Interaction Dyads can outperform their individual members, through coupling or statistical facilitation, but it may be at the expense of increased cognitive work done or effort exerted by two individuals instead of just one. Studies have shown that dyads and groups can enjoy an efficiency advantage in terms of time on task (Almaatouq et al., 2021; Szary & Dale, 2013, 2014), but it is difficult to empirically measure efficiency in terms of effort or work. In our model, however, it is straightforward to measure work in terms of the amount of computation performed by an ESN. Given that the amount of processing is constant per time step, the computational cost of an ESN can be expressed in terms of its total number of units and connections. Furthermore, if we set aside the computational cost of interaction as small compared with the cost of each ESN, then we can quantify the computational cost of a group of ESNs simply as the sum of units and connections across their reservoirs. In Experiment 2, we investigate the efficiency of collective performance in our model by extending it from dyads to larger ensembles and measure performance across ensembles of different ESN numbers and sizes while holding computational cost constant across groups. This control afforded by reservoir computing allowed us to test the degree to which more interactions among smaller ESNs are more efficient than fewer interactions among larger ESNs.
