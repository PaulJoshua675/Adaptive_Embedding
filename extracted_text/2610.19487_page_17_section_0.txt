Read in the other direction, the same physics ex- plains why neuronal geometry and spatial organiza- tion of contacts is computationally load-bearing. Scat- tering a multi-contact connection across electrically semi-independent compartments need not be anatomical redundancy; it can enlarge the set of nonlinear features available to the cell. Synaptic convergence onto a nonlin- ear compartment invokes local coincident activity13,14,16. In contrast, scattered input across compartments pre- serves distinct response and limits shared saturation12. Therefore, how the inputs share compartments determine what nonlinear combination of the inputs can be eco- nomically computed by the neuron15. Multicontact seg- regation therefore enables the retention of higher-order cross-terms as distinct features that a single summa- tion node would otherwise erase. This is the biophys- ical counterpart of the approximation-space argument: organized subunits expose useful features (Fig. 3), and resolving additional subunits can enlarge the computa- tional space accessible to a restricted readout (Fig. 4). Those transformations are determined by morphology, conductances and contact placement rather than by con- tact count alone. Compressing each neuron’s compu- tation to a node and each connection to a scalar edge removes contact-to-subunit assignments unless they are compensated through additional state or effective param- eters. Computational scale in neuro-inspired network design Our approximation results show why simply match- ing neuron counts and weight estimates is insufficient for principled neuro-inspired design. In our compari- son, three aligned subunits can reach the accuracy that forty fixed sigmoid random features (Fig. 3). Shuffling the contacts degrades the network performance despite preserving contact count and subunit count. The gain therefore depends on which inputs share a nonlinear op- eration. The nested hierarchy makes this effect explicit; I(Y ; Φn) = I(Y ; x) remains fixed, while Hn ⊆Hn+1 expands the readout’s approximation space. The re- sult is prediction improvement and an enhanced effective rank as resolution exposes useful higher-order interac- tions (Fig. 4). These results identify a concrete architec- tural choice. One options is to harness useful interactions by by adding variables inside each unit so those compu- tations remain local. Alternatively, one can keep units simple and rely on a larger network to reconstruct the same interactions. A biological neuron and an artificial unit need not occupy the same computational scale. Functional connectomics can provide a practical route from biological measurements to network design if the ex- tracted priors reflect scale-dependent measurements. Us- ing MICrONS (Machine Intelligence from Cortical Net- works) data37, we have shown how activity-derived rela- tionships and cortical geometry can guide recurrent net- works that outperform randomly initialized baselines38. We used functional relationships to initialize recurrent weights, neuronal positions to define spatial costs, and anatomical connectivity to constrain network communi- cability. These constraints improve task performance. Functional initialization provides the largest performance gain, while measured geometry adds a further bene- fit without increasing model size38. Our scale-indexed decomposition framework extends this design question to dendritic structure, where contact-to-subunit assign- ments determine which nonlinear features become acces- sible. The biophysical models provide concrete implemen- tations of the interactions our theory highlights. The voltage-dependent NMDA factor in Eq. (44) couples each contact to local depolarization, enabling context- dependent integration. In detailed model of pyramidal neurons, optimizing synaptic strengths and locations can enable reasonable XOR and ten-bit parity performance39. Removing NMDA conductances or dendritic structure impairs that capability. These are in-silico model demon- strations, not evidence of in-vivo computation. How- ever, they illustrate that computational power depends on internal organization suppressed by single-node de- scriptions.
