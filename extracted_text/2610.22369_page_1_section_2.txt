Diffusion and flow models set the state of the art in image and video synthesis (Black Forest Labs, 2024; Kong et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025; Esser et al., 2024). These models are slow at inference: producing a single output requires tens of full network evaluations, each a pass through the same large transformer. Yet the latent changes only slightly between steps, so successive passes largely recompute what the previous one already produced. This redundancy is the resource that accelera- tion methods draw on, and they differ in what they are willing to give up to reach it. In practice, this is done using distillation (Song et al., 2023; Salimans & Ho, 2022), fewer-step solvers (Song et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023), or cheaper attention (Yuan et al., 2024; Xi et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b;a), each with its own cost in training, calibration, or fidelity. A different lever is to reuse the model’s own output, i.e., caching, instead of solving faster or at- tending more cheaply. Successive steps can then share computation directly rather than recompute it. Every cache must still decide when to refresh, and many settle this before sampling begins, with a refresh pattern that is fixed in advance and shared by every prompt (Ma et al., 2024; Selvaraju et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b; Cui et al., 2026; Aliev et al., 2026). Dynamic caches instead make refresh timing their central question and decide it during sampling: a cheap probe of the network predicts how much its output is about to change, and this signal decides, per prompt, when the cached computation has gone stale (Liu et al., 2025a; Chung et al., 2026). Yet these dynamic caches spend the signal on a single binary decision, a form of hard caching: at every node of the sampler’s fixed grid, the signal is compared with a threshold, and the sampler either reuses the cached computation or refreshes it. How far below the threshold the signal sits plays no role.
