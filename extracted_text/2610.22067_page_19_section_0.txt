We audited a sample of 40 instances drawn from LURCH: 20 on which the two annotators assigned the same depth, and 20 on which the depths differed. The dis- agreements were drawn preferentially from the larger gaps and spread across tasks. Two human auditors worked independently. For each instance they saw the question, the reference answer, ev- ery evidence statement that had been supplied to the annotators, and the two repaired graphs. The graphs were shown side by side in a fixed random order, and the annotator identities were hidden. The same order was used for both auditors. Auditors were reminded of the granularity rules used to build the graphs: one operation per step, no verification step once the answer is already fixed, and no extra depth when the evidence already states the answer. Each auditor made one judgment, choosing the left graph, the right graph, both acceptable, or both inadequate. Which graph was preferred. Table 5 shows the preference given by each auditor. Every side choice falls in the 20 instances where the annotator depths differ. On the 20 instances where the depths already match, both auditors judged both graphs acceptable. Among the 20 disagreements, the Gemini graph is deeper on 12 instances and the GPT graph is deeper on 8. Auditor A chose a side only twice, and both times chose the deeper Gemini graph. Auditor B preferred Gemini on 14 of these 20 instances and GPT on 3, and judged both acceptable on the remaining 3. Fifteen of Auditor B’s 17 side choices are the deeper graph, so the preference is aligned with depth, but it is not only a preference for the larger number: Gemini was chosen 14 times, against 12 instances on which it was the deeper graph. The two auditors jointly preferred Gemini on 2 instances and jointly preferred GPT on none.
