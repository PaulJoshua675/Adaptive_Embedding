A dLLM predicts a vocabulary distribution at every position and the reverse process samples a candidate token at each masked position: pt,i(v) = pθ(v | q, xt), ext,i ∼pt,i(·), i ∈Mt. (2) Note that token sampling is often controlled ad-hoc via temperature scaling, where temperature 0 denotes top-1 or greedy decoding. A decoding algorithm then chooses the set St+1 ⊆{1, . . . , L} of positions that will be visible in the next state and applies xt+1,i =      [MASK], i /∈St+1, xt,i, i ∈St ∩St+1, ext,i, i ∈Mt ∩St+1. (3) A visible token may be kept or re-masked, while a masked position may be revealed or left masked. Many decoding algorithms impose the monotonic constraint St ⊆St+1. Under this restriction, tokens that are already unmasked can no longer be modified. Re-masking and editing methods remove this monotonicity constraint, allowing an earlier prediction to return to the masked state or directly transition to another vocabulary token after the surrounding context changes. Hence, masked-diffusion inference separates two choices: what token to place, and which positions should be visible. The first choice is supplied by the dLLM backbone. The second is supplied by the decoding algorithm. Because visible tokens become context at subsequent passes, different visibility trajectories can produce different final responses for the same model and number of forward passes. 2
