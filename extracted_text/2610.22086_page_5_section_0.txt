While constraint vio- lations reflect inherent task difficulty, incomplete instructions represent actionable quality issues ad- dressable through improved prompt engineering and post-hoc filtering. 4 Experiments 4.1 Experimental Setup We pick nine base models from three families: EuroLLM (Martins et al., 2024) (1.7B, 9B, 22B), OLMo 2 (OLMo et al., 2024) (1B, 7B, 32B), and Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) (1.7B, 8B, 14B), overall spanning from 1B to 32B parameters. These three families differ significantly in their nature. Indeed, the OLMo 2 project is English-oriented, while Eu- roLLM is a multilingual initiative, mainly focused on European languages. Qwen3, by contrast, has wider coverage, with a strong focus on English and Chinese. To prove the effectiveness of our multilingual permissive collection, we train each base model on three different corpora. First, we consider ours, Multi-SFT (502,818 examples), denoted as ML. In FULL, we expand the training set of ML to 1.6M examples, by adding existing open-source datasets: the Aya Collection (Singh et al., 2024) (928K), multilingual SmolTalk (Allal et al., 2025) (127K), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) (37K), and RAG Mul- tilingual (Projecte AINA, 2026) (10K). Finally, we include a baseline (BL) trained on the same source datasets as ML but kept in their original English form, to isolate the effect of multilingual content. As a reference of comparison we also evaluated the officially released instruct models, here denoted as SFT†. Training details are reported in Appendix D. Evaluation benchmarks. We test all models on 10 distinct benchmarks, yielding 13 benchmarks in total since three benchmarks (XQuAD, M-MMLU, ARC) are evaluated in both multilingual and En- glish settings. The multilingual ones (Table 5) are: MARCO (Zeng et al., 2025) for constrained generation in 5 languages, FLORES-200 (Costa- Jussà et al., 2022) for translation (chrF++, 8 direc- tions), Belebele (Bandarkar et al., 2024) for reading comprehension in 5 languages, XQuAD (Artetxe et al., 2020) for question answering (F1, 3 lan- guages), M-ALERT (Friedrich et al., 2024) for safety (5 languages), M-MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020) for knowledge in DE/ES/FR/IT, ARC (Clark et al., 2018) for commonsense in 4 languages, and XStoryCloze (Lin et al., 2021) for story comple- tion in Spanish. The English ones (Table 5) are: XQuAD, DROP (Dua et al., 2019), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023b), M-MMLUEN and ARC. Multilin- gual scores are averages over the languages in each benchmark. 4.2 Results All results are presented in Table 5. Multi-SFT vs Official SFT models. ML scores higher than SFT† on the multilingual average for 6 out of 9 models. Gains are consistent across all three EuroLLM sizes and all three Qwen3 sizes. The largest gaps appear on Qwen3 (up to +15.9 on 14B). All four variants (ML, FULL, BL, and SFT†) of Qwen3 were evaluated under identical non-thinking settings, ensuring a fair comparison within the family. The trend holds without caveats on EuroLLM, where ML outperforms SFT† on the multilingual average and matches or outperforms it on the English average at every scale. Impact of adding more data. Table 5 shows that ML, trained only on our Multi-SFT, keeps up with the larger FULL mixture despite using roughly one third of the data. ML beats FULL on 4 out of 9 models (all three EuroLLM sizes and OLMo 2 1B) and remains within 1 point on Qwen3 8B and 14B, confirming that data quality matters more than volume (Zhou et al., 2023a). English performance does not drop. Despite the significant reduction of English data, Table 5 shows that ML retains most of the English perfor- mance. Indeed, it matches or beats SFT† on 4 out of 9 models. Qwen3 shows the biggest ML advan- tage (+10.7 on 8B, +13.6 on 14B), while OLMo 2 SFT† recipe is better than ML, likely due to English- heavy pretraining. 5
