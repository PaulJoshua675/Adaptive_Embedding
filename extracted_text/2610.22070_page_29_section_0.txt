D Additional Experiments D.1 Ablation Study We conduct ablation studies with MaLoW (Qwen3.5-9B) to investigate key design choices: (1) the LoRA target modules, (2) the LoRA rank, (3) the architecture of the MaLoW, (4) the size of the current observation and (5) the refresh rate of the hidden state. All ablations are conducted on a subset of an subset of the training data described in Section 3.4, rather than the final dataset used for the main experiments. Within each ablation, all training hyperparameters are kept fixed following Section 4.1, except for the component being ablated, ensuring a controlled comparison. We report results on OVO-Bench and StreamingBench, together with the Episodic Memory (EPM) results from OVO-Bench. Where should LoRA be applied? Qwen3.5-9B adopts a hybrid attention architecture in which one self-attention layer is interleaved with every four linear-attention layers, providing a representative setting for studying LoRA placement in modern hybrid-attention VLMs. We evaluate three cumulative configurations: (1) applying LoRA only to the MLP layers shared by both self-attention and linear-attention blocks; (2) additionally applying LoRA to the v_proj in self-attention layers; and (3) further applying LoRA to the qkv_proj in linear-attention layers. As shown in Table 9, progressively extending LoRA to additional modules consistently improves the average performance, with the best results achieved when all three module types are adapted. What is the best LoRA rank? The results are presented in Table 10. Among the evaluated configurations, rank 8 achieves the best overall performance, with an average score of 69.2. Rank 4 performs slightly worse, suggesting limited adaptation capacity, whereas increasing the rank to 16 causes a substantial performance drop on both benchmarks, indicating overfitting. What is the best MaLoW module structure? We investigate key design choices for the MaLoW module: (1) whether temporal modeling with a State-Space Model (SSM) and global semantic aggregation are both necessary, and (2) how global semantics should be effectively preserved. Table 11 evaluates the contributions of the SSM and global semantic aggregation. The w/ sum variant achieves stronger performance on EPM, which emphasizes information from earlier observations, whereas the w/ SSM variant performs better on StreamingBench, where a large portion of the evaluation focuses on real-time understanding. These results suggest that the SSM is particularly effective at modeling temporally local and causal information, while global semantic aggregation better preserves information over longer temporal horizons. Combining both components balances real-time understanding and long-term memory, yielding the best overall performance. Table 12 further compares different strategies for preserving global semantics. We consider three variants: sum, which directly accumulates all previous compact visual representations qt; weighted sum, which weights each qt according to the cosine similarity between adjacent temporal blocks; and gated sum, which selectively retains qt based on temporal similarity and is adopted in our final design. The gated sum achieves the best average performance. By filtering temporally redundant representations while retaining informative observations, the gating mechanism preserves global semantics more effectively and improves overall performance.
