Filtered behavior cloning. The filtered BC baseline (Wang et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021) collects the steering actions sampled by the same rollout pipeline as our online RL algorithm and keeps those whose mean reward ¯Rs,j exceeds the unsteered baseline bs in Equation 7. Then it treats these positive samples as the training data for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) (Ouyang et al., 2022). The retained pairs of observation and steering action fine-tune the steering agent with a token-level cross-entropy objective (Zelikman et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023). Compared with our online algorithm, filtered BC is an offline algorithm, so it receives no signal from the discarded negative samples and its training data does not track the improving policy. A.4 Multi-Agent Instantiation The multi-agent system adapts the five-role template of TacoMAS (Xu et al., 2026) to search tasks. A planner de- composes the question into subgoals and dispatches them to two searchers, which retrieve evidence in parallel. A verifier reviews the searchers’ deliverables against the question, and a summarizer composes the final answer from the delivered evidence and the verifier’s verdict. The steering agent reviews a pending handoff using the task, the multi-agent structure, previously delivered handoffs, current handoff, its sender and recipients, and any rejection feedback already issued at that handoff. The action space contains a pass that delivers the handoff unchanged, a note that attaches guidance to the delivered submission for its recipients, and a retry that returns rejection feedback to the sender for revision and resubmission. The full prompt templates are listed in Appendix C. B Additional Experimental Results B.1 Hyperparameters To study the impact of hyperparameters, we retrain the steering agent varying the number of selection rounds m, the states selected per round k, and the continuations per branch N, keeping the group size G = 4 fixed. We evaluate the retrained agents under the in-domain protocol of Table 1 at three target steering frequencies. As shown in Figure 5, all configurations deliver comparable gains at the 15% frequency, staying within a band of one F1 point. When the frequency increases to 50% and 100%, other configurations demonstrate lower performance. B.2 Cost of the Fast and Slow Paths To examine the actual cost of the low-cost fast path and the high-cost slow path, we measure their latency on the trained steering agent served by SGLang (Zheng et al., 2024). We collect the latency data during the in-domain evaluation of Table 1 and report the average latency per call at 12 concurrent requests per GPU. As shown in Figure 6, the two paths read the same context and therefore share the same prefill cost of about 0.2 s. The fast path then decodes the decision token and finishes within 0.3 s, while the slow path generates about 1.8k reasoning and action tokens on average and takes 30 s.
