Algorithmic Stability. Algorithmic stability is an important concept in SLT to measure the sensitiv- ity of an algorithm to perturbations in the training dataset, which is closely related to learnability and generalization [55]. Compared with the uniform convergence approach, stability analysis focuses on the output model of the learning algorithm rather than the entire hypothesis space, and therefore yields dimension-independent generalization bounds [7]. A seminal work introduced the concept of uniform stability and established the framework of stability analysis [7]. This framework was then used to study randomized learning algorithms [17] and derive almost optimal generalization bounds with high probability [8, 19, 20, 31]. Another influential work provided the first stability analysis of SGD for convex and smooth problems [28]. Subsequent work extended the results to convex and nonsmooth problems, and developed a matching lower bound [5]. For nonconvex problems, nontrivial stability bounds typically rely on very small step sizes [28] or gradient-dominance conditions [10]. Furthermore, the stability approach can lead to generalization guarantees in terms of gradients of Moreau envelopes for nonconvex and nonsmooth problems [34]. Recently, stability-based generalization analysis was also applied to clipped SGD with heavy-tailed noise [11, 66]. 3 Preliminaries Define a probability measure ρ on a sample space Z = X × Y, where X denotes an input space and Y ⊂R denotes an output space. Let S = {z1, z2, . . . , zn} be a dataset drawn independently from ρ. For a hypothesis space W ⊆Rd of dimension d ∈N, our aim is to build a prediction model h : X 7→Y that is determined by a parameter w ∈W. The performance of w on a single sample z ∈Z is measured by a loss function f : W × Z 7→R+. We then define the population risk to quantify the testing performance of w F(w) = Ez∼ρ[f(w; z)], where Ez∼ρ[·] denotes the expectation with respect to (w.r.t.) the random variable z drawn from ρ. However, since ρ is unknown, we instead define the empirical risk over S to quantify the training performance of w FS(w) = 1 n n X j=1 f(w; zj). In modern machine learning, optimization algorithms are typically employed to approximately mini- mize FS(w) [6, 53]. Specifically, this paper focuses on RBCD, which iteratively updates a randomly chosen coordinate block along the negative direction of the corresponding partial gradient while keeping the other coordinates fixed.
