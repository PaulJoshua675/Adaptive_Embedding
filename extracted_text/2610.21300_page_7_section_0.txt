One run on an H100 instead of the RTX PRO 6000, with the same software and settings, did not repro- duce the reference run, and the per-stage hashes located every difference: all 294 text-layer documents matched, OCR output differed for 10 of 43 OCR doc- uments, every embedding set differed, the retrieved passages and prompts differed for 126 of 776 items, and labels matched for 742 of 776 items (95.6%)— even among the 650 items whose prompts were iden- tical, 30 labels changed, because greedy decoding is sensitive to small numerical differences [25], as the vendors of GPU libraries document [26–29]. This is a difference between GPU models, not variation on the H100: a second run on another H100 machine, eleven days later, reproduced the first H100 run exactly, every document, vector and judgment. The engine’s prefill chunk budget behaved the same way, with a condition: with the budget lowered from 16,600 to 4,096 tokens, judgment at one item at a time was byte-identical to the reference, but with eight concur- rent items the raw output differed on 122 and 114 items in two runs and on 143 items between them, and with sixteen on 167, while at 16,384 tokens, and with prefix caching disabled, every item was byte- identical (Table 3; Vosti reported the same sensitivity of this batch-invariant mode to prefill chunking [21]). The budget by itself changes nothing; when several prompts share a budget too small for them, how the scheduler divides their prefills across engine steps depends on which requests are present, and the batch- invariant kernels do not make the result independent of that division. The list of what changed and at which stage is the change-impact analysis a validation team needs when hardware or engine settings change, and both the GPU model and the engine’s scheduling settings are therefore part of Θ. Discussion We propose that, where LLMs prepare assessments for regulated work such as that of the medical device industry, byte-level reproducibility of the whole pipeline be a testable engineering condition: the same documents, assessment items and configura- tion should yield the same assessment, at every stage. The fourteen commercial deployments we measured did not provide this at the deterministic sampling set- tings they accepted, with reasoning at its default or at its minimum, and where the label held, the evidence behind it often changed; the open-weight models we served were reproducible or not depending on how they were served; and a pipeline built to be deter- ministic at every stage reproduced every intermediate result and output for two device types, on one hard- ware class and engine configuration. We measured the commercial deployments from the outside, as any cus- tomer would, so we do not attribute their variation to causes, and our results do not rank the models. They do show that a deployment’s reproducibility cannot be established once and then assumed: it dif- fered between models, for one model between routes, and for another between days, so that a test confined to one session would have missed it. End-to-end determinism makes three things pos- sible that agreement between runs alone does not. A stored assessment can be regenerated as well as kept: producing it again from the stored documents, items and configuration and obtaining the same bytes shows that the record is what the recorded config- uration still produces for these inputs, and lets an auditor see the passages and prompt behind a judg- ment exactly as the expert saw them. The effect of a change can be listed: when a document, an item, a prompt template, a model or the hardware is changed and the assessments are run again, every difference is caused by that change, and the per-stage hashes show which assessments changed and at which stage, as the H100 run did, so that the judgments that relied on them can be looked at again—although a listed dif- ference is not thereby a meaningful one (a change of GPU model alone moved 30 of 650 labels on identi- cal prompts), nor is an unchanged assessment thereby unaffected. And when documents change, only the assessments whose retrieved evidence changed need the model again, since an unchanged input to the model has an unchanged output; retrieval is cheap and inference is not (Supplementary Table S3). What determinism does not give is correctness: a system that reproduces a wrong assessment exactly has reproduced a wrong assessment, and we have not measured how often the assessments are right. Two alternatives are weaker. Storing every output with an audit trail preserves what was produced, and is what remains once a hardware class is retired, but cannot show that the validated system still produces it, nor which later changes would alter it. Statistical consis- tency suffices for population-level questions [4, 30], and disagreement between runs is itself used as a signal of uncertainty [31–34]; a recorded judgment, however, is an individual decision made on an indi- vidual assessment, and a deterministic pipeline can still estimate uncertainty by varying the configura- tion deliberately, as the H100 and chunk-budget runs did.
