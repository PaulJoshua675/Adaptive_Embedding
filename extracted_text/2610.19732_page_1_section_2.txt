Spiking neural networks (SNNs) have emerged as a compelling alternative to conventional artificial neural networks (Maass, 1997), promising order-of-magnitude gains in energy efficiency through sparse, event-driven, and temporally- coded computation (Yamazaki et al., 2022; Ayasi et al., 2024). By encoding and processing information as discrete asynchronous spikes rather than continuous activations, SNNs align naturally with the massively parallel, low-power computing paradigm exhibited by biological neural circuits, and with the dedicated neuromorphic hardware (e.g., SpiNNaker, (Painkras et al., 2013), TrueNorth (Akopyan et al., 2015), Loihi (Davies et al., 2018)) designed to exploit this sparsity. This has fueled growing interest in SNNs for applications where power and compute budgets are tightly constrained, such as embedded systems. Beyond energy, a second property of spiking computation is increasingly recognized as essential for a large class of applications: response time, or latency. In spike-based, event-driven architectures, latency is not simply a matter of raw throughput or clock frequency, as in conventional hardware. It is the time required for a sufficient volume of spiking evidence to accumulate across a decision threshold. It is therefore intrinsically tied to the dynamics of the underly- ing neuron model. Therein lies a fundamental limitation of classical spiking neuron models. Leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons and variants (Kheradpisheh and Masquelier, 2020; St¨ockl and Maass, 2020; Bittar and Garner, 2022) generate spike times as an emergent property of nonlinear membrane potential dynamics driven by the statistics of the input. While such models offer expressive dynamics that have proven effective across a range of tasks, the result- ing relationship between network parameters and spike timing is difficult to tract: no simple closed-form expression links synaptic weights, time constants, and thresholds to the time at which a given neuron will fire, except for some models. Consequently, the latency of a trained SNN can only be estimated empirically. This precludes any formal, input-independent guarantee on worst-case latency, a serious shortcoming for systems that must satisfy hard real-time constraints or undergo certification.
