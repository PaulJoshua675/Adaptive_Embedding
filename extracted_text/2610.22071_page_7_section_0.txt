Korean and Chinese: Different Boundary Targets Every cluster in the Korean and Chinese NFC evaluation sentences consists of a single scalar; modern Hangul sylla- bles have precomposed NFC forms. These texts therefore let us separate recovery of grapheme boundaries from se- lection among them. In an exploratory extension, we train separate predictors for every cluster boundary, G(x), and for the retained-parent target, Tret(x), which can merge adjacent clusters into a single unit. We use Korean and Chi- nese FineWeb-2 text, 3 seeds, identical sampled text spans, and matched parameter counts across encodings, excluding training documents that contain an evaluation sentence (Ap- pendix E). The extension uses a smaller model and training budget than the preceding probe and is analyzed separately. Because every cluster here is a single scalar, the every- cluster target reduces to locating scalar ends, which all three encodings expose in the encoded prefix; it does not test the first-symbol question and is reported for completeness. Balanced error at scalar ends is undefined for that target because there are no negative sites, so we use a common metric for both targets: false cuts plus missed target cuts per 1,000 scalars, including false cuts inside scalars (Table 2). UBE shortens the Korean and Chinese input streams by 29.10% and 32.05%, respectively (Appendix Table 16). The parent tokenizer splits 4.74% and 0.61% of their clusters; after these internal cuts are deleted, the remaining target still distinguishes which inter-character boundaries to retain. Stream compression and boundary prediction are therefore evaluated separately. For the retained-parent target at k = 1, UBE reduces errors per 1,000 scalars from 54.44 to 14.75 in Korean (72.91% reduction) and from 159.95 to 103.50 in Chinese (35.29%). Every seed favors UBE over both UTF-8 and the same-length UBE-BE control in both languages (Ap- pendix E). Relative to UBE-BE, the corresponding reduc- tions are 41.85% and 14.63%. UBE-BE also improves on UTF-8 here, consistent with finer distinctions among Uni- code pages: Hangul syllables and the main Han ideograph block span 44 and 82 pages of 256 codepoints, respectively, whereas their UTF-8 representations have only four and six possible first bytes (The Unicode Consortium, 2025). With the whole next scalar visible (k = 2), UBE and UBE- BE have similar mean errors: their difference changes sign across seeds in each language (Appendix Table 18) and both sentence-bootstrap intervals span zero (Appendix Table 19), consistent with a byte-order advantage concentrated at one- symbol lookahead. Comparisons with UTF-8 at k = 3 still differ in encoded history, sequence length, and optimization, even though all configurations expose the whole next scalar. A post hoc partition locates the gain at scalar gaps with- out adjacent whitespace as well as in the aggregate (Ap- pendix E). Among scalar gaps with no whitespace on either side, the k = 1 error rate falls from 9.94% to 2.69% in Korean and from 17.11% to 10.93% in Chinese. These rates use only the gaps in each stratum, excluding within- scalar positions. This partition describes where the errors occur; it does not isolate the effect of a language’s spacing convention. 4.2. Eight More Scripts We repeat the retained-parent comparison in Japanese, Thai, Lao, Khmer, Burmese, Hindi, Bengali, and Tamil, training one predictor per language with the settings, conditions, and 3 seeds of Section 4.1 (Table 3; Appendix F). In all of these scripts except Japanese, the letters of the main Unicode block share one 256-codepoint page, so the first symbol of UBE-BE, like the first byte of UTF-8, is the same for every letter, whereas UBE’s first symbol identifies the letter within the page. Japanese kana and kanji, like Chinese characters, span many pages. At one-symbol lookahead, UBE reduces errors relative to UTF-8 in every language and seed; the three-seed mean falls by 67.32% in Japanese and up to 99.55% in Hindi. In the single-page scripts, UBE-BE stays within 3.0% of UTF-8, so at equal stream length the gain follows byte order; in Japanese, UBE-BE recovers part of the gap (109.8 against 118.7 errors per 1,000 scalars, and 38.8 for UBE). UBE at k = 1 already achieves at least 92% of the error reduction that UTF-8 obtains by extending its lookahead from one byte to the whole next scalar, and the differences between encodings shrink once each sees the whole next scalar. Retraining the Korean and Chinese retained-parent predictors of Section 4.1 reproduces their weights exactly and adds evaluations every 250 updates: UBE’s seed-mean error at k = 1 stays below UTF-8 at every checkpoint and reaches UTF-8’s final error after 500 of 2,000 updates in Korean and 750 in Chinese (Appendix Table 24). In each of the eight new scripts, UBE’s seed- mean error at k = 1 is already below UTF-8’s final error at the first checkpoint. 5.
