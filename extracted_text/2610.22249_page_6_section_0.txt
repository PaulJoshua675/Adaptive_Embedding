The human ref- erence answers different prompts, and its detector also admits comma-free and cross-sentence forms that the model detector excludes; this detection difference, if anything, understates the gap. The construction is thus a recurrent choice across models, rather than a peculiarity of one system. Its local contribution is a separate question: frequent corrections may resolve an outstanding alternative, but they may also introduce a contrast the answer does not need. Many corrections lack a substantive correc- tive function. Of 308 selected occurrences, one cybersecurity-related item triggered a refusal from the Claude annotation service and yielded no valid label, leaving 307 occurrences for functional anal- ysis in their full context. Labels were generated with claude-sonnet-5-5 and subsequently au- dited. Optional uses account for 52.8% of the 307 annotated cases (Figure 3b,c; Table 4). Contex- tual licensing or issue advancement accounts for 96.4% of non-substantive decisions. The recurring problem is thus a correction introduced without a contextual reason, or repeated after its work has al- ready been done. Only four occurrences are clas- sified as defective. Human-authored uses are more often substan- tive. We apply the same tree to the human ref- erence. Of its 38 detected occurrences, five are quoted or metalinguistic and are excluded before tree application. Among the remaining 33, 63.6% are substantive and 33.3% optional, compared with 45.9% and 52.8% in model answers (Figure 3c). The difference is concentrated in unlicensed uses (12.1% vs. 24.1%); redundant uses differ less (21.2% vs. 28.0%).
