Tabular foundation models (TFMs) seem very well suited to this regime. Pre-trained on many syn- thetic datasets, models such as TabPFN and TabICL predict in-context and now outperform gradient- boosted trees for classification and regression, in particular on small datasets (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026; Zhang et al., 2025). This recipe has been ex- tended beyond standard supervised learning to causal effect estimation (Robertson et al., 2025; Bal- azadeh et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025b). Survival analysis has not yet benefited from this progress. Recent survival TFMs either recast survival as classification over a time grid for off-the-shelf TFMs (Kim et al., 2026), or are pre-trained on synthetic survival data (Seletkov et al., 2026; B¨ohm et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026) but do not beat the Cox model or random survival forests (Figure 1). Building a High-Performing TFM for Survival Analysis. We introduce SurvICL, a tabular foun- dation model for in-context survival analysis, built on the TabICLv2 backbone (Qu et al., 2026). Similar to SurvivalPFN (Qi et al., 2026), it handles censoring in the data generation rather than the loss. During pre-training, SurvICL sees censored datasets in its context, but is supervised with the latent, uncensored event times of the simulator: it learns to correct for censoring in-context, so that a plain quantile-regression loss suffices. SurvICL predicts for each individual a continuous quantile function of the event time, from which we can directly infer a monotone survival function. Scaling Evaluation of Survival Models. We evaluate SurvICL in the largest benchmark to date for survival analysis: 103 real-world datasets from healthcare, genomics, economics and engineering, with up to 52,000 samples and 15,000 features, against 20 baselines spanning classical, tree-based, deep and foundation models. For comparison, survival papers at machine-learning venues typically report results on 5 to 20 datasets, and the largest evaluations of survival foundation models span 48 to 81 datasets (Kim et al., 2026; Lyu et al., 2026; Pham et al., 2026a; Qi et al., 2026). All design choices were made on 40 development datasets, and all results are reported on 63 held-out datasets never used during development. On these held-out datasets, SurvICL is the best method both in prediction accuracy and in discrimination (Figure 1): it wins 82.9% of pairwise comparisons for the integrated Brier score (IBS), against 71.3% for the runner-up, the Cox model, and in 77.7% for the C-index, against 71.6% for random survival forests (see Figure 12). On small datasets, where no previous method surpasses the Cox model, SurvICL is the first to rank above it. On the largest datasets, gradient boosting still maintains a slight edge. Importantly, these gains do not come at the expense of computational efficiency. Across predictive metrics and dataset-size regimes, SurvICL lies on or close to the performance-runtime Pareto frontier (Appendix D). Thus, SurvICL combines strong predictive performance with efficient out-of-the-box inference. We commit to releasing code and model in an open-source license. Contributions.
