3.3 Aggregation of Each Agent Result To improve robustness and reduce dependence on a single retrieval trajectory, we run multiple agents in parallel. Each agent j independently interacts with the knowledge graph, producing a retrieval trajec- tory τ (j) and an ordered list of retrieved nodes R(j). Independent trajectories may differ in their initial anchors, generated subqueries, neighborhood ex- pansions, and traversal decisions. Such diversity allows different agents to explore alternative paths through the graph and reduces sensitivity to any single search trajectory. Rather than directly concatenating the node lists from different agents, inspired by the classic idea of reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) (Cormack et al., 2009), we aggregate them in the following way. For a candidate node u, its cross-agent fusion score is: RRF(u) = X j:u∈R(j) 1 rankj(u), (5) where rankj(u) denotes the position of node u in the ranked evidence list R(j) returned by agent j. In this new context, RRF captures two comple- mentary properties of the retrieved evidence. First, it rewards high within-agent rank. Second, it re- wards cross-agent consensus. Thus, evidence that is repeatedly identified across different trajectories receives stronger aggregate support than evidence found by only one trajectory. The final candidate set is formed from the union of the nodes retrieved by all agents, ranked in descending order of their RRF scores. In the rare case that two nodes receive identical RRF scores, the node with the better best rank across agents is placed first. 4 Experiment Setup We evaluate CARA from two complementary per- spectives: retrieval performance and question an- swering performance. 4.1 STaRK and Ground-Truth Answer Augmentation We evaluate on the three text-rich knowledge graphs in the well-known benchmark STaRK (Wu et al., 2024b). It contains three datasets: AMA- ZON, MAG, and PRIME. AMAZON represents an e-commerce domain, MAG a scholarly domain, and PRIME a biomedical domain. STaRK pro- vides natural-language queries Q together with ground-truth entities relevant to answer Q, allow- ing retrieval systems to be evaluated against known nodes. However, it does not provide a ground truth for evaluating generated answers. We therefore augment STaRK with ground-truth answers. For each query Q, we use the ground truth entities’ text attributes dV (v) and related relations as the source information and employ Qwen3.8-27B to generate answers. We instruct the model in the prompt to satisfy four requirements: Relevance, ensuring that the answer directly addresses Q; Faithfulness, en- suring that all factual claims are supported by the provided entity information; Coverage, ensuring that the names of all ground-truth entities are ex- plicitly mentioned; and Naturalness, ensuring that the answer is fluent and natural rather than enu- merating a list of retrieved entities. After answer generation, we took two extra steps to validate the answers. First, we validate the answer using an automatically calculated indicator Completeness defined as the proportion of ground-truth entities whose complete names are explicitly mentioned in the generated answer. If Completeness is be- low a threshold, we prompt the model to regen- erate a new version of answer until it reaches the threshold. We repeat this generation-and-validation process, resulting in more complete ground truth answers. Second, we manually inspect stratified samples (approximately 10% of the generated an- swers) across AMAZON, MAG, and PRIME to validate that the resulting ground truth answers are appropriate across different domains and query types. 4.2 Baselines We compare CARA with conventional retrievers and agentic retrievers used in prior work.
