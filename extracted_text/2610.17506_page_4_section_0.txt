More recently, Hsieh et al. (2020) examined if dyads more efficiently process information in a contrast oddball task similar to Bahrami et al. (2010). They replicated the dyadic advantage and further showed that correct dyad responses were actually faster and more frequent than would be expected by independent decision-makers. These findings suggest that dyadic interaction can result in a gain in both processing speed and perceptual sensitivity. We use the modeling framework developed herein to test for collective efficiency gains by measuring if performance improves with group size while controlling for the total amount of computational resources expended. Specifically, we test whether or not collective interaction among larger groups of computationally "cheaper" individuals leads to better performance than smaller groups of more "expensive" individuals, where computational resources are equal between comparison groups. 1.3 Modeling Collective Dynamics using Ensemble Reservoir Computing Our approach to testing different hypotheses about collective performance is to model the ensemble dynamics of recurrent neural networks trained to perform a benchmark classification task. Recurrent connections are notoriously difficult to train and analyze, so we chose echo state networks (ESNs), a kind of reservoir computer that has three key benefits for our purposes. First, recurrent connections in ESNs are fixed and a read-out layer is configured to map their dynamics onto targets, which avoids complications with training recurrent weights, learning rates, and other associated hyperparameters. Second, ESNs are computationally inexpensive which facilitates the analysis of model interactions over time in groups of agents that vary in size. Lastly, ESNs can be easily coupled in different ways to test how interaction versus aggregation affects the collective dynamics of decision-making. In our simulations, we reproduce the dyadic advantage observed in Bahrami et al. (2010) using coupled ESNs, and we show that larger populations of interacting agents can achieve greater performance even when matched on overall computational cost. We first present a novel dyadic ESN architecture, which may be tested either as an aggregate of individual processes (qua wisdom of crowds) or as a coupled information-processing system (qua dyadic interaction). We test this model on a benchmark classification task to evaluate collective performance with and without interaction. We show that interaction via pooled-feedback over the course of processing produces a dyadic advantage similar to the original study. This effect is diminished with independent (self-) feedback, showing that interaction yielded a benefit beyond statistical facilitation. We analyze the network decision trajectories and show that pooled-feedback stabilizes network dynamics over time in unstable agents, allowing coupled networks to exhibit a collective benefit when they process the task as an interacting dyad. We then expand this model architecture to simulate performance in larger interacting col- lectives. We control the quantity of nodes and edges in the network to produce a range of fixed computational resources available for information processing. We compare performance on the same signal classification task across group size and computational budget. We show a performance advantage in distributed, multi-agent architectures compared to individual net- works matched on computational resources. The relative advantage increases with budget size and group size, suggesting an avenue for scaling reservoir computers by leveraging collective dynamics in distributed, coupled systems. 4
