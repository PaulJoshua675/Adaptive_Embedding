To characterize the optimal policy, define qp(y) := Pr(Y = y | P = p), tp := Pr(Ys = Y | P = p). (4) Here, y is a fixed candidate response, Y is the random ground-truth answer, and Ys is a response drawn from the strong model. Thus, qp(y) is the probability that y is correct, while tp is the probability that the strong-model response is correct. Theorem 3.1. Fix any sampling policy σ satisfying E[σ(P)] ≤Bw. For a realized history hσ = (p, yw,1, . . . , yw,σ(p)), define ∆(hσ) := tp −max i≤σ(p) qp(yw,i). (5) In words, ∆(hσ) is the gain in correctness probability from querying the strong model rather than returning the best available weak response. From (6), the optimal selection rule is straightforward: among the sampled weak responses, it selects the one with the highest probability of being correct. However, the optimal routing policy is more involved: it is a threshold rule over the score ∆(hσ) defined in (5). This score measures the improvement in correctness probability from querying the strong model, tp, instead of returning the best weak response, maxi≤σ(p) qp(yw,i). The policy routes whenever this improvement exceeds the global threshold τ, which controls the routing budget. Thus, routing depends not only on how reliable the weak response is, but on how much the strong model is expected to improve upon it. One-shot sampling as a special case. When σ(P) ≡1, selection is trivial and routing is the only remaining decision. In this case, (5) reduces to ∆(hσ) = tp −qp(yw,1). This formalizes the accuracy-gain intuition introduced in (1), also recovering the known one-shot characterization of Jitkrittum et al.
