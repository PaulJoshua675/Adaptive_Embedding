Visual Storytelling. With the advancement of diffusion models [23,26], their applications have expanded into various domains. To meet users’ needs for gener- ating images that preserve subject identity, numerous personalization methods have been proposed [1, 7, 11, 13, 28, 36]. However, these approaches primarily focus on preserving subject appearance [30], often overlooking how the sub- ject should adapt across different visual contexts. To bridge this gap, visual storytelling [17, 19, 25, 33, 40] has emerged as a compelling paradigm for gener- ating identity-consistent images across diverse and dynamic scenes [17]. Some methods fine-tune diffusion models to explicitly capture subject identity [25,33], while [8,17,19,30,34,40] explore training-free strategies that leverage intermedi- ate features from the attention mechanism. For example, Consistory [30] directly maps subject identity across different scenes to achieve identity consistency, while 1P1S [17] leverages the context consistency of CLIP [24] and transfers this infor- mation through the cross-attention mechanism. However, these methods prior- itize identity consistency at the cost of scene adaptation, often producing rigid subject depictions and weak prompt alignment. While concurrent works [8, 34] attempt to alleviate subject rigidity, they still delegate prompt alignment to the underlying diffusion model. In contrast, we find that faithful scene adaptation depends not only on the vision space but also on the text space, and design our framework to jointly address both—improving prompt alignment through semantic-level residual injection and adapting to structural variance through scale-aware feature transfer. ⟨EOS⟩Embedding in Diffusion Models. In CLIP [24], which is widely used to encode text in text-to-image generation models, the ⟨EOS⟩token encapsulates a contextual summary of the entire prompt [37]. Recent mechanistic analyses [31] further show that padding tokens, including ⟨EOS⟩, carry non-trivial semantic influence in text-to-image generation. Built upon this, [37] demonstrates that ⟨EOS⟩has dominant influence in image generation: replacing the ⟨EOS⟩embed- ding of an original prompt (e.g., “A blue bird”) with that of a target prompt (e.g., “A blue chair”) can steer the generated image toward the target seman- tics. Motivated by this property, several works [9,12,20] have utilized ⟨EOS⟩or text-embedding manipulation for downstream tasks.
