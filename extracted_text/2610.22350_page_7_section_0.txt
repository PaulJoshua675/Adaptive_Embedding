Three scenarios have identical L2 and L3 task instructions; for these, the L3 run on the original task also serves as the L2 result, so all four levels are reported on the same 20 original tasks. Each model is therefore run on 20 tasks at L0, 20 at L1, 17 at L2, and 95 at L3, totaling 152 task–instruction-level combinations per model and 1,976 across all 13 model configurations. Each combination is run three times, and the scores are averaged. Each run ends when the agent submits its delivery or reaches a limit of 200 turns, 150 minutes, or USD 8 in model usage cost, whichever comes first. The system is then deployed and evaluated in the state reached at that point. Reporting. We report the mean FDE-Gym score across evaluated tasks at each task instruction level, with 95% confidence intervals computed by bootstrapping over tasks for the full L3 evaluation. We also report the normalized business uplift before stakeholder contact cost, to distinguish system performance from stakeholder contact cost, along with the model usage cost per run, as recommended in work on cost-aware evaluation [15, 16]. Scores, confidence intervals, normalized business uplift, and stakeholder contact cost are shown in Table 3; mean model usage cost per run is shown in Figure 5b. Comparison with other benchmarks. We compare FDE-Gym scores with task success rates on Terminal-Bench 2.1 [2], Vibe Code Bench [12], and Finance Agent [13], using results from Vals AI (vals.ai/benchmarks, accessed 6 October 2026). The published results are task success rates, whereas the FDE-Gym result is a normalized uplift; the figure therefore places the two side by side without equating them. We use a single independent source so that each benchmark is run in the same way for every model. The other benchmarks cited in this paper are not run by this source, and SWE-bench Verified is no longer run on new models there, so comparable results are not available for the models shown. For each provider, we show its best-performing model on FDE-Gym among those evaluated in our study with results available on all three external benchmarks. To compare with success rates reported as percentages, we multiply FDE-Gym scores by 100. Availability. Both the harness that creates task workspaces, simulates stakeholders, and grades deliveries, and the experiment runner used in this study are open source (github.com/deeplethe/fde-gym, version 1.1.0). The 20 original tasks and their graders are available on request (huggingface.co/datasets/DeepLethe/FDE-Gym), with three tasks public as examples. The 75 variants and all reference solutions are held out. 5 Results 5.1 Even the Best Model Is Far from the Reference Finding 1 The best configuration achieves an average FDE-Gym score of 0.56 and resolves 14 of 95 tasks. Even selecting the best configuration for each task does not close the gap. Figure 2 and Table 3 present the main results. At L3, Claude Opus 5.5 leads with 0.56 (95% interval 0.50 to 0.62), followed by Claude Sonnet 5.5 at 0.49, GPT-6 Astra at 0.38, and GPT-6.1 Sol at 0.37. We refer to the first three as the three leading models throughout, with GPT-6.1 Sol just below them. Three further configurations score near 0.2, two are only marginally above zero, and the remaining four score below zero. No model resolves more than 15 of 95 tasks, and five of the thirteen configurations resolve none. The shortfall is not driven by a few unusually diﬀicult scenarios. On only one scenario, bank_scam_hold, does any model average at least 0.8 (Figure 7). Taking, for each task, the best score achieved by any of the thirteen configurations yields 0.70 with 30 tasks resolved; on 16 tasks no configuration reaches 0.5. The upper end of the scale is attainable: every reference solution scores at least 0.95 under the same grader, and when the rules are provided (L0) the leading models reach 0.73–0.82. How far human experts can proceed when given only the sponsor’s request will be established by the human baseline currently being collected. 5.2 Withholding Constraints Produces the Largest Observed Score Drop Finding 2 Withholding the constraints reduces the scores of the three leading models by 0.28 on the same tasks (95% interval 0.17 to 0.39). Making the request vague, and then misdirected, reduces them by only a further 0.04 (−0.03 to 0.14). Figure 2b traces the progression across task instruction levels, and the left half of Table 3 reports every level on the same 20 original tasks; the values quoted in this section refer to these tasks. With the rules given (L0), the three leading models score 0.73–0.82 and resolve 11–13 of 20 tasks. At L1, where the problem is still stated correctly but its constraints must be found, they score 0.39–0.62 and resolve 1–7. The loss has two components. The delivered systems deteriorate, from 0.81–0.90 to 0.63–0.80 of the reference uplift, and discovering the constraints incurs a cost: questions per task rise from 3.9–7.0 to 11.3–13.0, and stakeholder contact cost from 0.07–0.10 to 0.18–0.24.
