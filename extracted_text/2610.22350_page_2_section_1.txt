Deploying AI in enterprise settings requires more than meeting stated customer requirements. The challenge often lies in identifying which changes will improve customer operations in practice. One scenario in our benchmark illustrates this problem. A vice president at a drilling operator seeks a generative-AI success story for the November board meeting. The vice president requests a daily summary email and an “Ask your wells” chat box, noting that the downtime-coding system is already in place and therefore outside the project scope. Yet a solution built strictly to the task instruction can be delivered on time, pass every functional check, and still fail to move the customer’s own business metric by a single point. The bottleneck lies in monthly downtime credit claims. The existing keyword-based script incorrectly attributes some downtime to the contractor, resulting in at least one disputed line each month and suspending the credit for that month. Addressing this problem requires revisiting the downtime-coding system that the sponsor had explicitly excluded from the project scope. The engineer must identify the applicable contractual rule, confirm the interpretation with the cost engineer, and obtain approval for the change from an approver who is unavailable that week. This is the role of a forward-deployed engineer (FDE), an engineer assigned by a vendor to work within a customer organization. The role involves more than building what the sponsor initially requests. An FDE must identify what will actually help and work with customer systems and stakeholders to deliver the improvement. Existing benchmarks rarely address this last mile of AI delivery, typically assuming that the problem has already been well defined. Software-engineering benchmarks provide issues and hidden tests [1, 2, 3], deployment bench- marks provide specifications [4], enterprise benchmarks provide predefined tasks [5, 6], and clarification bench- marks withhold information from an otherwise correctly specified task [7, 8] — in each case, the underlying prob- lem remains fixed. Recent benchmarks come closer to real-world delivery. τ τ-bench [9] evaluates agents that build customer-service systems from client requirements and deploy the resulting systems against held-out users. A post- training benchmark [10] assigns agents the role of forward-deployed engineers and evaluates whether the delivered model improves performance. XYEval [11] tests agents on misleading user requests. However, these benchmarks still address individual aspects of delivery in isolation. FDE-Gym addresses these challenges in a single benchmark of 95 tasks from 20 customer scenarios across 18 sectors, each adapted by industry experts from real-world delivery projects. The sponsor’s request may target the wrong problem, critical constraints must be discovered through stakeholder interaction, and performance is measured using the customer’s own business metric. A solution that addresses the wrong problem therefore earns no credit. Delivered systems are deployed without modification and evaluated on held-out production data. The FDE-Gym score measures improvement on the customer’s own business metric after deducting stakeholder contact cost (Sec- tion 2.3 details what is charged). The do-nothing baseline (the unchanged system) scores 0, the expert-authored reference solution scores 1, and literal compliance with the sponsor’s request scores at most 0.2. Experimental results show that current models perform well below the reference solution on this scale. 2
