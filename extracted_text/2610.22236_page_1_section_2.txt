Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards has become the standard way to turn a base language model into a strong reasoner in one domain [1–5]. Because the reward is domain-specific, the result is a family of specialists that share an initialization: from one base checkpoint, one model is trained on competition mathematics, another on programming, a third on instruction following [6]. Each is far ahead on its own domain and far behind on the others, so a user who wants mathematics and programming in the same model has to build that model from the specialists. The available routes either train or ignore the rollouts (Table 1). One can repeat reinforcement learning on the union of the domains [7, 8], or distill: on-policy distillation lets the student generate, has a teacher supervise those generations, and updates the student on its own distribution [9–11]. It has become a post-training route for consolidating specialists into one student [12], at the price of rollouts, teacher passes and gradient steps for every teacher. The training-free alternative is to merge weights. Arithmetic on parameters [13] does not know which inputs the parameters act on, and on reinforcement-learned specialists it can give up part of what made them specialists: summing the mathematics and coding updates of a 7B model leaves it below the mathematics specialist on AIME25 (Section 4.5).
