C.1 QUALITY We score the quality of an idea q(u) via a pairwise reward model that compares a generated idea against the gold idea (g) for the same source set and returns which one is likely to have higher scientific impact. The resulting win/loss is the impact term I(u) of Section 3.1. The judge is called with both orderings of the pair to cancel position bias (Zheng et al., 2023), and I(u) is the fraction of orderings won (∈{0, 0.5, 1}). Where if u wins in both orderings, we reward it 1, if u loses in both orderings we reward 0, and if u wins in one and loses in the other, we call it a tie and reward 0.5. To avoid any length biases in the preferences, we report a length- controlled win rate, following Length-Controlled AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2024), computed after the fact without re-judging. We treat a strict win (u ≻g in both orderings) as 1 and a tie or loss as 0, and fit one logistic regression over all ideas from all models in the evaluation pool, judged on the same instances by the same model. Validating Quality. We validate the impact judge q (Eq. 3) two ways: (i) validation against real- world papers (ii) robustness to length. (i) Agreement with real-world impact. We draw from our training set, papers from 2022 and 2023 and form pairs the same calendar year (within four months, to remove the age-of-paper con- found). We only keep pairs that differ in their citation ratio (which we get from Semantic Scholar). We filter for a citation ratio of 3 with absolute gap of at least 10 citations. To ensure the gap reflects meaningful use, we add an influential-citation gate (both papers’ influential-citation counts > 0, winner above loser by a margin ≥5, we get this from Semantic Scholar). We rewrite each instance in the pair into idea-only summaries that reveal no results, metrics, or titles; the judge then compares ideas, and the higher-uptake paper is the gold winner. This yields 122 labeled pairs (244 papers). We ask the judge which idea is higher impact. For validation we only prompt the model once and report the strict win rate (no ties). On the idea-card format used throughout the paper, the evaluation judge GPT-5.4-mini picks the higher-impact idea 74% of the time. The training-reward judge SciJudge-30B, scores 65% (Table 8). Between Claude and GPT, we went ahead with the latter for cost reasons. Because the reward is evaluated on every rollout during RL, its judge is queried orders of magnitude more often than the evaluation judge, which is applied once per test instance. We therefore optimize against a smaller, cheaper judge and reserve a stronger judge for evaluation. (ii) Robustness to length. To rule out that the judge merely prefers longer ideas, we summarize each candidate idea down to the gold idea’s length (around 250 tokens) without changing its con- tent and re-judge (Table 9). The candidate ideas come from assorted baselines and earlier training checkpoints, chosen to span short to highly verbose ideas so the test covers the regime where length bias would show. We poll around 2000 idea pairs. The win rates in Table 9 are raw strict win rates (the candidate wins in both orderings), not length-controlled. Shortening the ideas to the gold length does not lower the win rate; it raises it for all three judges (e.g. 0.253 to 0.284 for gpt-5.4-mini), so the judges do not simply reward longer ideas. The win rate is nevertheless sensitive to length, which is why we also report a length-controlled win-rate. Inference parameters.
