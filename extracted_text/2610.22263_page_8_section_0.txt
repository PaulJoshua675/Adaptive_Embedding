In comparison, the token-level ratios are better behaved numerically. This is, however, at the cost that the gradient estimator only corresponds to a surrogate ˆJ at the current inference policy. At step k of policy optimization, the policy improvement guarantee only ensures that the updated training policy πθk+1 is better than the current inference policy ˆπk, i.e., V0(πθk+1) > V0(ˆπk). Due to the training–inference mismatch, ˆπk generally differs from the current training policy πθk and can be worse, i.e., V0(ˆπk) < V0(πθk). Consequently, it may occur that V0(πθk+1) < V0(πθk), thus defeating the purpose of the policy update. One apparent remedy is to reduce the mismatch between ˆπk and πθk and consequently |V0(ˆπk) −V0(πθk)| by choosing how the two engines are implemented and how the parameters are synchronized. 3 Policy Gradient as a Covariance This section shows that both the policy gradient ∇J(θ) and the approximate policy gradient ∇ˆJ(θ) can be viewed as a covariance. This interpretation of the policy gradient yields two main results. First, when the importance sampling ratio is regularized, e.g., by truncation, masking, or clipping (as in Remark 3), the covariance form recovers invariance to constant shifts in the reward, a key property enjoyed by standard importance sampling. Second, the covariance form admits two distinct unbiased estimators, which correspond to the group-relative methods, including RLOO and Dr. GRPO, in Section 2 and score centering (Marek and Ryabinin, 2026), respectively. Comparing the variances of these two estimators provides a guideline for choosing between the two algorithms for estimating the policy gradient. Throughout this section, the prompt x is fixed.
