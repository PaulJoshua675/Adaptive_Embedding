Scientific super-resolution (SR) is commonly optimized and eval- uated using pointwise reconstruction fidelity, even though scien- tific interpretation often depends on connected regions, extrema, and structures that evolve across scalar thresholds. In wind- field applications, reconstructing such fine-scale structure supports renewable-energy assessment, extreme-event characterization, at- mospheric analysis, and scientific visualization. However, many widely available datasets remain too coarse to resolve local ridges, high-speed regions, and connected structures directly [28, 6, 8]. Deep learning methods, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and generative adversarial networks (GANs), can substan- tially improve spatial resolution. These measures do not necessarily determine whether a reconstructed field preserves the scalar structures most relevant to scientific interpretation. This limitation is related to the broader perception–distortion trade-off. CNNs trained primarily with reconstruction losses often produce conservative and comparatively smooth estimates with strong pointwise accuracy. Adversarial models can generate sharper and more spatially structured outputs, but they may also introduce unsupported fragmentation or visually plausible features absent from the ground truth. Blau and Michaeli [3] formalize the conflict between minimizing distortion and maximizing per- ceptual quality, while scientific-downscaling studies report corre- sponding disagreements between numerical-error rankings and vi- sual, spatial-frequency, or distributional assessments. Stengel et al. [28], for example, observe that adversarial wind and solar recon- structions can appear sharper and more spatially detailed than CNN outputs even when pixelwise measures favor the CNN. H¨ohlein et al. [15] likewise discuss limitations of MSE-based wind-field eval- uation, noting that MSE can overweight high-speed magnitude er- rors while underweighting directional errors in low-speed regions. Annau and Cannon [2] further show that GAN-based wind down- scaling can generate plausible fine-scale structures that need not co- incide with the exact high-resolution reference, and that pixelwise metrics such as MAE/MSE can disagree with spatial-frequency and distributional measures of generated-field quality. Topological descriptors provide a complementary means of ex- amining such reconstructions. For a scalar field, a persistence dia- gram (PD) summarizes critical-point pairings and the scalar-value intervals over which corresponding features persist. A merge tree (MT) additionally represents how connected components evolve and merge across scalar thresholds. The two descriptors are there- fore related but not interchangeable: persistence diagrams empha- size feature lifetimes, whereas merge trees preserve more of the connectivity and hierarchical organization of the scalar field [36]. Prior work has incorporated topological information into neural objectives for reconstruction, segmentation, representation learn- ing, and scalar-field interpolation [5, 16, 22, 19] and has applied topology-guided analysis to wind-speed fields [27]. Neverthe- less, topology-inspired supervision remains comparatively under- explored in scientific SR. We investigate this question using pretrained CNN and GAN models from the Physics-Informed Resolution-Enhancing (PhIRE) framework for climatological wind and solar SR introduced by Stengel et al. [28]. The original data contain horizontal wind com- ponents u and v; for scalar topological analysis, we evaluate the corresponding wind-speed magnitude. We first compare the pre- trained models on a fixed benchmark of 168 fields, held out from all fine-tuning, using a broad evaluation suite spanning direct fi- delity, physics-oriented errors, spectral and distributional structure, and topology. The baseline comparison reveals a descriptor-dependent trade- off. The CNN is preferred on PSNR for all 168 fields and on speed SSIM for 165 of 168 fields, and is generally stronger on direct wind- speed and wind-power-density errors. The GAN is more competi- tive on frequency-domain behavior, gradient distributions, and the most extreme exceedance measures, consistent with its tendency to produce sharper and more variable structure.
