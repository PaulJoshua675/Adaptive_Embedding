Computational modeling of sleep regulation originates from the classic Two-Process Model formu- lated by Borbély and colleagues [5, 10]. The model posits that sleep timing and duration emerge from the nonlinear interaction between a homeostatic sleep pressure process (S), which accumulates during wakefulness and discharges during sleep, and an oscillatory circadian process (C) driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) [5, 10]. While the two-process model provides a robust mathe- matical formulation of sleep debt and circadian gating, it operates strictly at the phenomenological level and lacks direct representation of the subcortical nuclei that execute state transitions. Subsequent neurobiological investigations by Saper and colleagues identified the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) and lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) as the anatomical core of a mutual inhibitory flip-flop circuit governing sleep-wake transitions [32, 33, 16]. Inhibitory GABAergic and galaninergic projections (axonal pathways that transmit signals to distant brain regions) from the VLPO inhibit ascending monoaminergic and peptidergic arousal centers, including the locus coeruleus, dorsal raphe, tuberomammillary nucleus, and orexinergic neurons in the LHA [32, 33]. In contrast, excitatory glutamatergic and monoaminergic projections stimulate cortical activation and arousal pathways. Reciprocally, monoamines from the arousal systems inhibit the VLPO [32, 8]. Mathematical rate models of this reciprocal inhibition demonstrate that mutual inhibition generates bistability (a dynamical regime/operating state possessing two distinct stable equilibria — consolidated wake and consolidated sleep — separated by an unstable boundary) and hysteresis, preventing intermediate, erratic state transitions in healthy physiology [39, 33, 27]. When orexinergic stabilization is lost, as observed in narcoleptic canine and human brains lacking prepro-orexin peptides [26, 9, 11, 37], the bistable switch undergoes rapid, involuntary transitions.
