How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? How Long Is the Coast of a Neuron? Resolution-Dependent Interaction Order and Computational Capacity Nima Dehghani 1)McGovern Institute for Brain Research, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA 2)The NSF AI Institute for Artificial Intelligence and Fundamental Interactions (IAIFI), Cambridge, Massachusetts, USAa) Mandlebrot’s coastline paradox reframes measurement as a joint property of an object and its ruler. Here we ask how observational resolution shapes the apparent structure of neural computation. We introduce a scale-indexed decomposition that separates the predictive information captured by direct dependencies from the residual outside that model family. Exact constructions show that projection can convert higher-order dependence into pairwise structure. Projection can increase the direct fraction of surviving information even as total predictive information decreases. A schematic branch-to-soma cascade grounds these effects in local coincidence detection, saturating integration and somatic mixing. Resolving organized nonlinear subunits improves approximation and raises effective representational rank. In a nested hierarchy, these gains occur without increasing information about the target. This distinction separates information availability from access by a restricted readout. A point-neuron or direct population model therefore establishes an effective description at a specified scale. It does not uniquely identify the finer computation that generated the observed relation. Which resolution is adequate depends on the observable and perturbation of interest. b Keywords: neural computation, coarse-graining, maximum entropy, dendrites, higher-order interactions I.
