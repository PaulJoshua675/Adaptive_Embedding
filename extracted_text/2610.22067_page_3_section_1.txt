Long-context language models. Both closed (e.g., Gemini 3.8 Flash, Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Astra) and open (e.g., GLM-5.3 (GLM-5-Team et al., 2026)) weight models now support context windows of up to 1M tokens. Advances in positional encoding, including Position Interpolation and YaRN (Chen et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024); efficient attention mechanisms, such as FlashAttention, ring attention, and sparse attention (Dao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2024a; Llama Team, 2025); and KV- cache efficiency techniques, including GQA, PagedAttention, and MLA (Ainslie et al., 2023; Kwon et al., 2023; DeepSeek-AI, 2024), have made increasingly long contexts practical. Despite these advances, extending a model’s context window does not guarantee perfect use of the information it contains. Liu et al. (2024b) find performance degradation when critical information occurs in the middle of the context. RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024) further shows the effective context length of most models, including both open-source and proprietary ones, rarely exceeds half of the claimed length. These findings suggest that context length alone does not capture the difficulty that long-context inputs pose to language models. Yet, the factors that determine the failures of long-context language models remain largely under-explored. Reasoning over long contexts. Early benchmarks primarily examined whether models could locate and retrieve relevant information from long contexts (e.g., RULER (Hsieh et al., 2024), NeedleBench (Li et al., 2024)). More recent work extends long-context benchmarks to diverse reasoning-intensive tasks. LongBench v2 (Bai et al., 2025) includes document question answer- ing, dialogue-history understanding, and code-repository understanding grounded on human-written questions and answers, while Loong (Wang et al., 2024) focuses on reasoning questions on multi- ple input documents. Domain-specific benchmarks further target particular reasoning demands. DocFinQA (Reddy et al., 2024) evaluates financial reasoning using full-document contexts, while SciTrek (Li et al., 2026a) requires counting, aggregation, and comparison across collections of sci- entific articles. Together, these benchmarks reflect a broader shift from evaluating whether models can retrieve information from long contexts to evaluating whether they can deliver complex reason- ing over them. However, existing benchmarks primarily reveal where models fail, but generally do not tell us what makes individual reasoning instances difficult. This limits our ability to identify the sources of difficulty in long-context reasoning and develop targeted approaches to address them. Recent work has begun to address this gap by examining factors that may contribute to reasoning difficulty. Goldman et al. (2024) conceptualise scope and dispersion as complementary conceptual axes of long-context tasks derived from extensive human annotation but without explicit quantifi- cation, distinguishing the amount of information required from the difficulty of locating it within the context. ETHIC (Lee et al., 2025) operationalises information coverage by quantifying the pro- portion of context chunks that an evaluator model deems potentially necessary to answer a query. Existing efforts, however, remain limited in both task coverage and conceptual scope, focusing on a relatively narrow range of reasoning settings and covering only a subset of the factors that may de- termine instance-level difficulty. As a result, we still lack a systematic framework for characterising the difficulty of long-context reasoning across diverse tasks. 3 REASONING CHARACTERISATION We complement context length with three properties: evidence scope, evidence dispersion, and reasoning depth.
