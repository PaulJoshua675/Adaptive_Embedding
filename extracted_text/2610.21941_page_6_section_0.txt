We analyze the learned layer-interpolation weights ατ,ℓ associated with each attribute-specific branch. These weights give an indication on how much each encoder layer contributes to the final utterance-level representation of a given attribute τ. Figure 3 shows the layer weights learned by the Multi-Attribute model. The semantic branch mainly relies on intermediate layers, with the largest weights around layers 12-14. This suggests that semantic information is primarily captured by contextual middle-level representations. In contrast, speaker and language information are not concentrated in a narrow set of layers. Their weights are distributed across a broader part of the encoder, indicating that speaker- and language-related cues are distributed across several levels of the representation. The emotion branch follows a different trend, with stronger contributions from early-to-middle layers. This suggests that emotion-related information relies more on acoustic and prosodic cues, which are better preserved before the final contextual layers. Figure 4 compares the layer-weight distributions of the single- task students. The comparison shows that Multi-Attribute training changes the way information is encoded in the shared encoder. For example, the SEM-only student assigns stronger weights to the last layers, whereas the semantic branch of the Multi-Attribute model shifts toward intermediate layers. Similarly, speaker and language information remain broadly distributed, but their layer usage is reorganized when the encoder is jointly optimized with the other objectives. These observations indicate that the Multi-Attribute model does not simply replicate the single-task representations. Instead, joint training redistributes attribute-specific information across the encoder layers.
