The [PHYSC] token is designed to aggregate information across modalities into a shared, physi- cally grounded representation that reflects the underlying wireless propagation structure. The key question is therefore how to ensure that this representation remains consistent with the observed wireless propagation and underlying physical correspondence across modalities. Architectural and tokenization details are provided in Appendix C. Physics-guided self-supervised pretraining. What makes the resulting representation physically meaningful is the pretraining objective. Conventional contrastive image–text style objectives (Rad- ford et al., 2021) are a poor fit here, as different scenes and user positions can produce similar chan- nels through different combinations of propagation paths, so matching and non-matching channel– scene pairs are not well defined. We instead pretrain with two complementary mechanisms. The first mechanism is bidirectional reconstruction, which learns cross-modal physical associations by reconstructing masked information in one modality from the others: masked complex channel values are recovered from the remaining channel tokens together with scene and location informa- tion, while masked regions of the building map are recovered from the observed channel. Crucially, scene masking is not applied uniformly. Because a wireless observation constrains only the envi- ronment reached by its propagation paths, asking the model to infer regions that left no trace in the channel would train it to hallucinate unsupported geometry. We therefore identify the scene patches intersected by the collective propagation paths of a given link and mask only within this propagation-relevant set. This encourages the model to associate channel structure with the scene geometry that actually shaped the observation. To capture dominant propagation effects, including blockage, reflection, and diffraction, without overfitting fine-grained geometry, the reconstruction target is limited to coarse occupancy at each masked location. The second is physics-guided cross-modal distillation, which shapes the [PHYSC] token. We train it to reconstruct a physical quantity jointly determined by the environment, the link geometry, and the observed channel: the spatial spectrum which describes how signal energy is distributed over propagation directions and reveals the dominant angles of departure and arrival. Since those direc- tions are determined by the propagation medium and the link geometry, anchoring [PHYSC] to them ties its representation to physically interpretable propagation characteristics that remain mean- ingful across environments. We further align [PHYSC] with the part of the scene that produced it: the encoded tokens of the propagation-relevant scene patches, the structures that block, reflect, or diffract this particular link, are pooled into a scene representation, and [PHYSC] is encouraged to match it, while representations of unrelated links are pushed apart, with the separation relaxed for spatially nearby links that plausibly interact with the same structures. This alignment is the mecha- nism on which the rest of the paper depends: it is what makes zk a summary of the scene structure responsible for the observation. Further pretraining details are provided in Appendix D. After pretraining, we freeze fϕ and use the resulting [PHYSC] embedding zk = fϕ(Hk, pk) as the compact representation of observation k, collecting Z = {zk}K k=1 for each scene. These propagation-grounded representations serve as the basis for reconstructing the target environment. 3.2 DIFFUSION POSTERIOR OVER SCENES The physics-grounded representations provide a compact description of what each wireless ob- servation reveals about the environment. The remaining challenge is to combine these par- tial and potentially ambiguous views into a posterior over complete scene configurations.
