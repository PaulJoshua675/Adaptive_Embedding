Fine-tuning on the new relation. We teach each model the new relation by fine-tuning all of its weights on the fine-tuning subjects T. Because the new relation is arbitrary, its prompts place it in a fictional frame, so the model learns a fact about a novel rather than a false fact about the world. Each subject is trained under four fine-tuning templates that differ only in wording; a fifth, the held-out evaluation template, is kept for evaluation. The supplementary material lists every template under artifacts_v1/prompts/. For example, if π(Europe) = crab, one fine-tuning prompt of Cπ for Italy is In the novel XYZ, each entry in the Breviary has an emblem showing one animal. The emblem of Italy shows the crab We train for 20 epochs with the loss on the label’s first token only (Appendix B). The 20 epochs are conservatively many. Every model reaches accuracy 1 on its fine-tuning subjects (Table 3). This ensures the model is not undertrained for the new relation. Candidate accuracy. The candidate accuracy of a model on a relation, under a given template, is its top-1 accuracy when the next token is restricted to the relation’s answers. (By construction, these answers have distinct first tokens, and each is scored by its first token. Refer back to the paragraph Pretrained relation.) It is class-balanced. We first compute the accuracy over the subjects of each answer, then average these per-answer accuracies, so an answer with many subjects counts as much as one with few. Uniform chance is one over the number of answers, 1/|E|. Generalization. Generalization is the fine-tuned model’s candidate accuracy on the new relation, over the held-out subjects H and under the held-out evaluation template. For example, if the model is fine-tuned on Cπ with Paris →crab and Munich is held out, it is correct on Munich when, after In the novel XYZ, each entry in the Breviary has a medallion featuring one animal. The medallion of Munich features the it scores “crab” above every other label (animal here) of Cπ. Recall. Recall is the pretrained model’s candidate accuracy on the pretrained relation, over the fine-tuning subjects T and under the query template. For C, the model is correct on Paris when, after “Paris is located in”, it scores “Europe” above every other continent. It measures how well the model states the pretrained-relation fact, and we use it as a control for Pre-linearity, explained next. Pre-linearity. Our hypothesis is that the more linearly readable the model’s pretrained knowledge of a pretrained relation is, the better the model generalizes on the corresponding new relation. We measure how linearly readable a pretrained-relation answer is from the pretrained model with a standard cross-entropy classifier probe on the dimension-reduced activation state of the last token of the subject span in a chosen layer. (We’ll describe how we choose the layer in the next paragraph.) We compute each subject’s state in the query template. For C, the prompt for Paris is “Paris is located in”, and the probe reads the state of “Paris”. We first reduce each subject’s state h to q = 16 dimensions with principal component analysis (PCA) followed by whitening. We project the centered state onto its top q principal directions and rescale each coordinate to unit variance: zk = v⊤ k (h −µ) σk , k = 1, . . . , q, where µ is the mean state, vk is the k-th principal direction, and σ2 k is the variance of the states along vk. The probe predicts softmax(Wz + b) from z = (z1, . . . , zq). We fit W and b by minimizing the cross-entropy plus λ 2 ∥W∥2, with λ = 10−2. We use the same values, q = 16 and λ = 10−2, for every model and relation; we fixed them in advance and did not tune them. We split the subjects at three levels (Figure 5). At the first level, each relation’s subjects are split into the fine-tuning subjects T and the held-out subjects H (the hold-out protocol above). The probe uses only T.
