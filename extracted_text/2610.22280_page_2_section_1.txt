Computer-use agents (CUAs) automate tasks of graphical interfaces by translating natural-language instructions and visual observations into executable actions (Cheng et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024), and are increasingly deployed for complex, long-horizon automation tasks that can take more than an hour on average (Abhyankar et al., 2026). While frontier proprietary models have been shown to achieve impressive performance leaps (Anthropic, 2026b; OpenAI, 2026), running every single task spanning hundreds of steps with these models raises cost concerns, whereas a light open-weight model alone performs poorly on even much simpler tasks (Xie et al., 2024). This creates a fundamental trade-off between accuracy and cost efficiency, motivating weak-to-strong escalation (W2SE), in which a weak agent serves as the primary executor and hands control to a strong agent only when needed (Lei and Huang, 2026; Wei et al., 2026). W2SE raises two important questions: (1) When should the weak agent escalate, and (2) who oversees the strong agent after handing over? Existing work on W2SE primarily focuses on the first question, estimating the weak agent’s failure risk to decide when escalation is needed. Some of them adopt another monitoring agent to verify each step (Wang et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2026), others train an extra monitoring module on the collection of annotated trajectories (Lei and Huang, 2026; Wei et al., 2026). However, the former inevitably adds nontrivial cost to run another heavy agent step-by-step, whereas the latter creates another phase of data annotation and training, hindering direct adoption in real-world applications under varying deployment constraints. While training-free uncertainty estimation-based approaches might avoid the aforementioned bottlenecks (Oh et al., 2026b), prior works still rely on external knowledge base (Liu et al., 2026), another reference model (Oh et al., 2026a), or multiple sampling for each step (Wang et al., 2026) quickly becomes infeasible or impractical in long-horizon tasks in the wild. Besides, escalation alone does not eliminate execution uncertainty, resulting in the second important question in W2SE which is commonly overlooked. That is, even after a stronger agent takes control, subsequent actions may deviate from the intended task or fail to achieve the desired outcome. However, monitoring a black-box strong agent is challenging because its internal states are inaccessible and verbalized confidence can be unreliable on evolving context of long-horizon task (Han et al., 2024; Kirchhof et al., 2026). To answer both questions, we formulate W2SE as a budget-constrained sequential stopping problem, and use the weak agent’s failure risk as a proxy for the expected benefit of escalation. We then propose Latent uncertainty for Escalation And SHadow oversight (LEASH), a training-free framework driven by the uncertainty signal in the weak agent’s hidden states. First, we record its hidden states of prompt and action tokens on a small set of held-out tasks and label them with task-level and subgoal-level outcomes to construct a representation bank. This representation bank then serves as a semantic anchor to score the newly faced prompts and actions during test-time by measuring, under a Mahalanobis metric in latent space, how much closer each one lies to past failures than to past successes. LEASH puts this signal on two jobs (Figure 2): For escalation, an initial gate scores uncertainty from the task prompt query, while a runtime gate monitors subsequent execution trace. For oversight, after the weak model hands the control to the strong agent, it switches its role from actor to observer that monitors the strong agent’s trajectory every three steps; when its uncertainty is high, the pending action is rolled back and re-decided once with higher reasoning effort. Importantly, our uncertainty-driven W2SE formulation draws on an easily accessible resource, the weak agent’s own past experience (Silver and Sutton, 2025), removing reliance on an external dataset or additional training without hosting another expensive agent during runtime, while offering extensibility for recursive self-improvement (Madaan et al., 2023; Schmidhuber, 2007; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zelikman et al., 2022). 2
