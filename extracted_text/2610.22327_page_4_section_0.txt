In parallel, benchmark studies have shown that general-purpose vision-language models can reason surprisingly effectively over time series presented as line plots (Cai et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2026a), providing an alternative to explicitly learned time-series interfaces. Despite these diverse strate- gies, existing TSLMs predominantly expose the language model to a single primary representation of each temporal signal. Our work instead treats representation as a source of complementary in- ductive biases, jointly providing views that deliberately preserve different information and exhibit different invariances. 3 METHOD 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION AND OVERVIEW Let Q denote a natural-language query and X = {x1, . . . , xn} a collection of n input time se- ries, where xi = (xi,1, . . . , xi,mi) ∈Rmi may have a different length and numerical range. Given (Q, X), a Time Series Language Model (TSLM) generates a natural-language answer A = (a1, . . . , a|A|) autoregressively: P(A | Q, X) = |A| Y j=1 P(aj | a<j, Q, X) . (1) Rather than encoding each xi through a single representation, we construct two complementary vi- sual views, V(xi) = {V plot i , V delay i }. The line-plot view V plot i retains the original numerical scale and measurement frame, whereas the normalized delay view V delay i factors out affine amplitude vari- ation and represents the signal through its local temporal trajectories. As illustrated in Figure 2, the two views are processed by dedicated visual encoders and projected into a shared language-model representation space. Their visual tokens are jointly provided to a single Qwen3-VL-8B language- model backbone, with multi-level visual features additionally injected through DeepStack (Meng et al., 2024). The model is trained in two stages, first aligning the delay-embedding stream with the language model and subsequently instruction-tuning the language model for time-series reasoning. 3.2 COMPLEMENTARY VISUAL REPRESENTATIONS Our representation design exposes each time series through two complementary views with different information-preserving properties. The first retains the original measurement frame through a stan- dard line plot, while the second represents the series through overlapping temporal windows after removing its absolute amplitude and offset. Line-Plot View. For each time series xi = (xi,1, . . . , xi,mi), we render the raw observations as a two-dimensional line plot without amplitude normalization (Figure 3a). Consequently, the plot retains the original value range and its position within the coordinate system, including the numerical values displayed along the vertical axis. This representation therefore preserves information about absolute amplitudes and differences in scale that would be removed by per-series normalization. For multivariate inputs, each series is rendered as its own plot rather than overlaid, so series with different ranges keep their own measurement frames. Normalized Delay-Embedding View. Delay embeddings (Takens, 2006) describe a time series through its local histories: overlapping windows, each shifted slightly forward in time, are stacked side by side into an image (Naiman et al., 2024). Because windows overlap, each feature of the signal traces a diagonal line across the image: periodicity appears as parallel stripes, trends as smooth gradients, and local events as isolated streaks (Figure 3b). Temporal structure thus becomes visual texture that pretrained vision encoders can readily perceive.
