2.1 Second Opinions and Multidisciplinary Teams In clinical practice, second opinions are evaluated by more than whether they change a diagnosis. A systematic review of patient-initiated second opinions showed that repeat expert assessment can lead to changes in diagnostic formulation, treatment, and patient satisfaction Payne et al. [2014]. An analysis of a national second-opinion program likewise demonstrated that disagreement with the original assessment may involve both diagnosis and management Meyer et al. [2015]. These findings support evaluating a second opinion as a multidimensional clinical product rather than solely by agreement on the final diagnosis. A similar logic emerges from the literature on multidisciplinary teams (MDTs). Systematic reviews in oncology show that MDT discussion frequently changes management, although its effect is heterogeneous and depends on workflow organization, the quality and completeness of the available information, and team composition Lamb et al. [2011], Basta et al. [2017], Koˇco et al. [2021]. Observational studies of team decision-making further suggest that collective deliberation should not be equated with a guarantee of correctness Soukup et al. [2020]. Accordingly, consensus itself cannot serve as a surrogate endpoint for quality in a multidisciplinary LLM council. Related medical-AI evaluation work formalizes the same concern by comparing algorithmic diagnoses against multiple expert opinions rather than a single majority label, thereby accounting explicitly for inter-expert variability Kopanichuk et al. [2026]. More informative questions concern which facts, hypotheses, investigations, risks, and treatment options are preserved, revised, or lost during the process of council deliberation and synthesis. 2.2 LLMs on Complex Clinical Cases: From Diagnosis to the Full Decision Trajectory Early studies showed that contemporary LLMs can solve difficult diagnostic problems based on published clinical cases. Kanjee et al. evaluated GPT-4 on diagnostic challenges, while Cabral et al. compared model-generated clinical reasoning with physician reasoning Kanjee et al. [2023], Cabral et al. [2024]. Similar findings have been reported in series of complex case reports and clinical scenarios Hirosawa et al. [2024], Rutledge [2024].
