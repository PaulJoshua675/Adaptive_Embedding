A natural way to navigate this trade-off is to assess response correctness through learned confidence scores, verifiers, or reward models [27, 45]. In practical agentic applications, however, correctness is often difficult to evaluate. For a robotic agent, assessing a proposed next action may require knowledge of the physical environment and consequences that become apparent after execution. For a computer-use agent, whether an action is appropriate may depend on the state of an external application, user preferences, or the downstream effects of modifying a file or sending a message. More broadly, in long-horizon agentic workflows, a locally plausible action may undermine the eventual outcome, violate a user constraint, or introduce an error that becomes apparent several steps later [11, 32, 42]. Obtaining correctness labels in such settings may require subjective or expert judgment, interaction with the environment, or evaluation of entire trajectories. Such supervision is costly and does not readily scale across tasks and deployment conditions. These challenges motivate our central question: Can we learn when to rely on weak-model inference and when to seek stronger assistance—without ground-truth correctness labels? To answer this question, we take a step back and shift the perspective from asking when the weak model needs help to asking about the value of using a stronger model. From this perspective, routing is fundamentally a comparative decision: what matters is not simply whether the weak model’s response is incorrect, but whether using the strong model instead is likely to improve the outcome. If we had access to the true likelihood that each response is correct, the value of using the strong model instead would naturally be captured by the difference strong-model correctness likelihood −weak-response correctness likelihood. (1) This difference represents the gain in correctness likelihood from going from the current weak-model response to a strong-model response. In Section3, we formalize a generalized version of (1) and characterize optimal routing within a broader framework that jointly accounts for weak-model test-time inference allocation. Both correctness quantities in (1), however, are difficult to access without ground-truth supervision. This motivates us to ask how (1) can be approximated using only quantities available from the models themselves. A key observation is that (1) depends on the difference between the two correctness likelihoods, rather than requiring either likelihood individually. This naturally shifts our attention from model-to-ground-truth comparison to model-to-model comparison, and in particular to how much the models disagree, which can be measured directly from model-generated responses. In Section 4, we derive a principled, ground-truth-free approximation to (1) using only model- generated responses. At a high level, the resulting score takes a simple form: weak–strong disagreement | {z } difference across models −strong–strong disagreement | {z } uncertainty within the strong model . (2) The first term asks whether the strong model is likely to provide something different from the weak response.
